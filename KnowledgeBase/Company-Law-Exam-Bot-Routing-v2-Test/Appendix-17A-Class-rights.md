# Appendix 17A: Class rights

## Appendix 17A:

## Class rights

Although there are variations upon each theme, there are basically two classes of share: ordinary shares (called common stock in the US), and preference shares (called preferred stock in the US).

## 1. Ordinary shares

An owner of ordinary shares generally has the following rights.

## i. Voting

An owner of ordinary shares (of common stock) may attend meetings of members of the company, propose and discuss motions and vote on them. Generally, an ordinary shareholder has one vote per share (on a poll). The ordinary shareholders control the company in general meeting and influence management by appointing the directors.

## ii. Dividends

An owner of ordinary shares (of common stock) may be paid a dividend when:

- a. the company makes a profit and
- b. when declared by the company.

But ordinary shareholders only receive the surplus of the declared dividend after a dividend has been paid to the preference shareholders according to their express rights thereto, in respect of which see section 2 below.

## iii. Return of capital

Ordinary shareholders have the right to the return of capital when the company is wound up either alongside (or equal with) other shareholders or, if the preference shareholders are expressly given priority, after the return of capital to the preference shareholders.

## 2. Preference shares

'Preference share' is a generic name given to various classes of share in order to distinguish them from ordinary shares. Their distinguishing features depend upon the rights conferred upon holders of them as set out in the memorandum, in the articles or in the terms of their issue. In a nutshell, they often have priority over ordinary shares in relation to the payment of a dividend and the return of capital when the company is wound up.

An owner of preference shares will typically have the following rights.

## i. Voting

A preference shareholder generally has limited voting rights. The right of a preference shareholder to attend meetings of members and, moreover, to vote on proposed resolutions is usually limited to such matters as the winding-up of the company, restrictions to vary their class rights or to vote in connection with their dividends that are in arrears. Other than the above they have no rights to vote and thereby influence the constitution of, or the direction of the business of the company, for instance by hiring and firing directors, etc.

## ii. Dividends

A preference shareholder generally has the right to a first claim upon any dividend declared by the company. The right to a dividend is presumed to be non-participating, unless the articles or terms of issue expressly state otherwise. This means that they are not entitled to participate (share) in the surplus dividend with the ordinary shareholders.

## Example 1

A company, XYZ Ltd, has issued 1,000 ordinary shares. It declares a dividend of $5,000. Each share receives a dividend of $5.

## Example 2

XYZ Ltd issues 1,000 ordinary shares and 500 preference shares which carry the right to a $2 dividend preference. It declares a dividend of $5,000. The preference shareholders receive a dividend of $1,000 (or $2 per share), and the ordinary shareholders share a dividend of $4,000 (or $4 per share).

## Example 3

If the preference shareholders have the express right to participate in the profits, on the facts of Example 2 they would receive a first dividend of $1,000, as above, and the balance of $4,000 would be divided equally between the ordinary and the preference shareholders ($2.66 per share).

The terms of the dividend preference is usually expressed as a percentage of the par (or nominal) value of the shares.

## Example 4

On the facts of Example 2, assume that XYZ Ltd issues 50010% preference shares with a par value of $20. If the shares are non-participating, the preference shareholders receive $1,000 (500$2010%).

A preference shareholder's right to a dividend may be cumulative . This means that if the company does not declare a dividend in any one year, the right is carried forward to subsequent years and paid before ordinary shareholders as soon as a sufficient dividend is declared.

## Example 5

XYZ Ltd does not declare a dividend in year one, but in year two it declares a dividend of $5,000. If the shares are cumulative and non-participating the preference shareholders receive $2,000 ($1,000 plus $1,000 carried forward from year one), and the ordinary shareholders share $3,000 or $3 per share.

The right to a cumulative dividend is presumed unless it is specifically displaced by express words in the articles or terms of issue.

## Example 6

On the facts of Example 5, assume that the articles of XYZ Ltd state that the preference shareholders have the right to a $2 dividend preference 'payable out of the yearly profits only' . In these circumstances they would not receive a dividend in year one, and would receive only $1,000 in year two, as no carry forward is permitted.

## iii. Return of capital

When a company ceases to trade and its life ends upon the company being formally wound up, the articles should state whether preference shareholders are entitled to the return of their capital in priority to, or alongside (with equal rights to), the ordinary shareholders. As a rule, however, they do not have any right to share the surplus assets after the return of capital to the shareholders.

## Example 7

Assume that XYZ Ltd issues 1,000 ordinary shares with a par value of $10 each, and 500 preference shares also with a $10 par value. The company is wound up with net assets (after paying all debts) of $20,000. The ordinary and preference shareholders may be entitled to the return of their capital equally, pari passu . So the ordinary shareholders receive a return of their capital of $10,000, and the preference shareholders receive $5,000. The surplus of $5,000 is paid over to the ordinary shareholders.

By contrast, the articles of XYZ Ltd or the terms of issue may give preference shareholders priority over ordinary shareholders, so that the ordinary shareholders are repaid their capital contributions only after preference shareholders have received theirs.

## Example 8

Assume that the facts are as in Example 7, save that the net assets of XYZ Ltd only amount to $10,000. The articles give preference shareholders priority rights to the return of their capital. The preference shareholders here receive $5,000, the return of their capital in full. The remaining $5,000 is shared pari passu between the ordinary shareholders - each share receiving a return of capital of $5 only.
