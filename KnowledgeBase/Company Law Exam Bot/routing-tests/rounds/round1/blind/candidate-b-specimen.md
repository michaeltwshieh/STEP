#### 1.2

The notice must be given to C, D and E as the registered members. Articles 27.1 and 27.5 also require it to be given to A and B because each is a director who is not also a shareholder. A person entitled to a share following a member's death or bankruptcy would also be entitled under articles 27.4 and 27.5, although no such person appears on these facts. An auditor should receive a copy if the applicable Companies Legislation gives that right.

Article 11.1 requires at least 14 clear days' written notice. Neither the day on which notice is served nor 28 August counts. Personal delivery or email to a registered email address should therefore be completed no later than 13 August. Email is the better practical method because article 27.2 deems service complete when it is sent, although evidence of transmission should be retained. If post is used, the letter must be properly addressed and prepaid. It is deemed served on the fourth day after posting, so posting should occur no later than 9 August and prudence favours an earlier date. Recorded delivery or courier delivery would provide useful evidence, but only a method authorised by the articles should be treated as formal service.

All entitled members may consent to shorter notice under article 11.2. That route should be documented rather than assumed.

#### 1.3

A non-accidental failure to give proper notice to an entitled person ordinarily invalidates the meeting and its proceedings. The same result follows if the required notice period is deliberately ignored without the unanimous short-notice consent required by article 11.2.

Article 11.3 saves an accidental omission to give notice and accidental non-receipt by a member. Attendance in person or by proxy will normally make an objection to non-receipt difficult. The distinction matters: article 11.3 does not cure a deliberate decision to leave D or E off the circulation list.

#### 1.4

C cannot transact the liquidation business at the appointed meeting merely because C owns 70 per cent. Article 12.2 requires two shareholders to be personally present, and C is only one person. The meeting remains inquorate even if C's holding is enough to carry a two-thirds poll.

If no second shareholder arrives within half an hour, article 12.3 applies. Assuming the directors called the meeting rather than the members requisitioning it, the meeting stands adjourned to the same day in the following week at the same time and place. If C alone remains present for half an hour at that adjourned meeting, the members then present constitute the quorum. C could then put the special resolution to a vote and, on a poll, C's 70 per cent would exceed the two-thirds threshold in the articles.

#### 1.5

On a show of hands article 13.1 gives each member one vote, whatever the size of the holding. C casts one vote for the resolution while D and E cast two votes against it. The chairman must therefore declare the special resolution lost on that vote.

C should demand a poll before, or when, the result is declared. Article 12.6 allows one member holding at least 15 per cent of the paid-up capital to do so, and C holds 70 per cent. Article 13.1 then gives one vote for each share. C's 70 per cent is greater than the two-thirds super-majority prescribed by the definition of a special resolution, while D and E together have only 30 per cent. The poll reverses the show-of-hands result and the special resolution is carried.

## Question 3

### SUBMIT THIS

The chosen jurisdiction is The Bahamas. Company is treated as a Bahamian international business company whose internal management is governed by the examination articles. The Bahamas is retained throughout.

#### 3.1

Company has two organs with different jobs. International Trust Company is the registered shareholder and acts through general meetings or written member resolutions. The board consists of the trust officer and Ms Avril Nath, and the board manages Company and its worldwide assets, including the Midcity Bank account.

The division grew out of the nineteenth-century corporate model. Investors supplied capital and became shareholders, while business management was entrusted to directors with the time and skill to run it. That arrangement allowed capital to be collected from numerous investors without requiring all of them to make daily decisions. It also preserved managerial continuity when shares changed hands. Limited liability placed commercial risk on Company and its creditors rather than exposing each shareholder to every Company debt, so control over the use of Company property was placed in a board that owed duties to Company.

The shareholder retains the constitutional and supervisory decisions. It may alter the articles, appoint or remove directors and decide matters reserved to it, including voluntary winding-up. The board takes the residual business decisions as a collective body. For this Company, that includes decisions about the worldwide portfolio and the Midcity Bank account. The model is less tidy in a private trust structure than in a listed company, since International Trust Company may supply both the shareholder and the staff who sit on the board, but the legal capacities remain separate.

#### 3.2

Article 19.1 gives the directors all powers of Company except those which the Companies Legislation or the articles require the members to exercise. It follows the older Table A model. A member cannot take over a board power merely by passing an ordinary resolution or giving an instruction.

Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon supports that result: where the articles place a power in the directors, the shareholders cannot usurp it by a resolution telling the board what to do. International Trust Company can alter the articles by the required member resolution or change the board, but article 19.1 itself contains no reserved power allowing the shareholder to issue binding directions. This differs from the later Table A 1985 form, under which a special resolution may direct the directors.

#### 3.3

The trust arrangement compresses the practical distance between ownership and control. International Trust Company holds Company's shares under the Redhouse Trust. As registered member, it exercises the voting rights. Company, however, owns the worldwide assets and Midcity Bank account in its own name. The trust beneficiaries do not become Company members merely because those assets are held within the wider trust structure.

The trust officer and Ms Nath owe their director duties to Company. Neither is a nominee director in the legal sense. A request from a trust relationship manager, a beneficiary or even International Trust Company acting informally must be assessed against Company's interests. Blind compliance would expose both directors if Company suffers loss. Management and control should also occur at genuine board level in The Bahamas if that offshore residence is part of the structure; a paper board that merely signs decisions taken elsewhere creates both duty and tax-residence risk.

International Trust Company still has strong lawful control as member. It can select the directors, remove them under article 15.3 and alter the articles. It may make recommendations. Article 19.1 stops it from converting those shareholder rights into daily management by informal command. That boundary is awkward in a managed trust company, but it is the boundary the actual article chose.

#### 3.4

International Trust Company may remove the trust officer by an ordinary resolution under article 15.3 before the end of the period of office. It need not prove misconduct. Because the member is a corporation, it must act through an authorised corporate representative at a properly convened meeting or through a person authorised to sign its written member resolution. Article 12.11 permits a written resolution signed by all members entitled to vote, and International Trust Company appears to be the sole member.

Article 15.4 allows the Company, again by ordinary member resolution, to appoint a replacement for the removed director. The incoming director should first consent in writing and confirm eligibility. The member may combine removal and replacement in one properly authorised resolution. Company must then update its register of directors and make any required Registrar notification. Ms Nath cannot appoint the replacement merely because she is the continuing director: the supplied articles do not confer a casual-vacancy appointment power on the board.

## Question 4

### SUBMIT THIS

A director controls assets that belong to the company and can bind it to liabilities far greater than the director's fee. The reward therefore comes with several distinct risks.

The first lies in authority. A director must read the legislation, memorandum and articles before committing the company. Royal British Bank v Turquand protects an honest outsider from some internal irregularities, but that protection cuts only one way: the company may remain bound while the director is liable internally for exceeding a power. A careful director records the board decision, checks quorum and voting, identifies the authorised signatory and follows the required deed or sealing form.

Fiduciary risk is broader. Section 120 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 requires honesty, good faith and conduct which the director believes serves the company's interests. The same basic common-law duty prevents the use of a power for a collateral purpose. Hutton v West Cork Railway Co shows why sincere belief alone is insufficient where corporate money is used irrationally. Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver required directors to account for profit diverted from a corporate opportunity, while Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi concerned a director trying to capture a company contract personally.

Conflicts are a recurrent problem in offshore administration. A director may be connected with the service provider that supplies accounting, legal or management services. Full disclosure and informed prior approval are needed before a director contracts with the company or profits from the office. Section 124 of the BVI Act supplies a statutory disclosure route, and the examination-style articles commonly bar the interested director from voting. Approval by disinterested directors or members should be obtained before the transaction. Concealing the interest may allow the company to set the contract aside and recover the profit.

Care and skill create a different exposure. Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co once allowed considerable room for the inexperienced director, but Dorchester Finance Co Ltd v Stebbing and Norman v Theodore Goddard apply an objective standard. A director cannot sign blank cheques, accept implausible accounts or leave all supervision to another person. Section 122 of the BVI Act asks what a reasonable director would do in the same circumstances. Section 123 permits reliance on employees, fellow directors and professional advisers only where the director acts in good faith, makes the inquiries called for by the circumstances and has no reason to distrust the material. Lexi Holdings plc v Luqman shows the cost of reliance when the records and relationships already give warning; Madoff Securities International Ltd v Raven shows that properly grounded reliance may be defensible.

A service-provider director is exposed in exactly the same way. Calling the office a nominee directorship changes nothing. Instructions from a beneficial owner must be tested against the company's interests. Bishopsgate Investment Management v Maxwell is a sharp warning against signing documents placed before a director by a dominant outsider without inquiry.

Insolvency raises the stakes. Fraudulent trading provisions derived from section 332 of the Companies Act 1948 may require a director knowingly involved in defrauding creditors to contribute to the company's assets. BVI law also has wrongful-trading exposure under section 256 of the Insolvency Act 2003, where the director knew or ought to have known that insolvent liquidation could not be avoided. Jersey has fraudulent and wrongful trading under articles 178 and 177 of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991. A Bahamian IBC has fraudulent-trading exposure but the course materials identify no wrongful-trading rule for that vehicle. The director must therefore monitor solvency, stop incurring debts when payment is no longer realistic, obtain insolvency advice and document the decision.

The company can sue for rescission, an account of profit, an indemnity or compensation. Foss v Harbottle ordinarily makes the company the proper plaintiff, subject to the fraud-on-the-minority exception. A liquidator will review the board's conduct closely because any recovery increases the fund for creditors.

Risk reduction starts before appointment. The director should obtain reliable information about the company and client, define the role in a director's service agreement, insist on accurate books, read the transaction papers and keep a reasoned record. Potential conflicts should be disclosed early. Prior member approval or later ratification can release some liabilities after full disclosure, including an interested transaction or negligent management, but a dishonest appropriation prejudicing minority members or creditors cannot be cleansed by majority vote.

The available contractual protection varies sharply. Sections 132 and 133 of the BVI Business Companies Act permit lawful exoneration, indemnity and insurance within their limits. Similar protection is recognised for Bahamian and Bermudian companies, and section 98 of the Companies Act 1981 of Bermuda invalidates protection for fraud or dishonesty. Jersey article 77 and the legislation of Guernsey, Hong Kong and Singapore restrict exoneration more severely. The articles and director's service agreement must be tested against the actual jurisdiction rather than copied across companies.

A company management agreement may give the service provider an indemnity from the client, but it may omit the individual director and it is only as good as the client's resources. A wide clause can also be struck down if it was not drawn to the client's attention or purports to excuse more than the law allows. Directors' and officers' insurance is usually the more practical backstop for negligence and inadvertent third-party liability. It will not insure deliberate default, fraud or dishonesty.

The safest director combines valid protection with disciplined conduct. Proper inquiry, independent judgment, disclosure, minutes, professional advice and early action on insolvency reduce the chance that an indemnity ever has to be tested. No document makes fraud, bad faith or wilful default safe.

#### 5.1

A members' voluntary winding-up assumes solvency. The directors first make full inquiry into the company's affairs and sign a declaration that all debts can be paid in full, normally within 12 months, with a statement of assets and liabilities attached. The members then pass the required winding-up resolution and appoint the liquidator. Notices are published and sent to the Registrar. The liquidator collects the assets, pays every creditor, returns the surplus to the members, prepares a final account and obtains final member approval or holds a final meeting.

A creditors' voluntary winding-up begins from the opposite financial position. The directors cannot honestly sign a declaration of solvency. They convene the members to pass the winding-up resolution and also convene the creditors. The directors present a statement of Company's financial position. The creditors choose the liquidator by majority of those voting in person or by proxy and have the greater voice in the process. The liquidator realises the assets, pays claims in statutory priority and presents the final account to both creditors and members before the dissolution filings are made.

Company must use the creditors' route. Its assets total $2,605,000, but liabilities total $3,375,000 before the liquidator's $40,000 costs. Blackacre produces only $1,700,000 against Mortgagee's fixed-charge debt of $2,400,000, leaving a $700,000 unsecured shortfall. The remaining free assets total $905,000. After liquidation costs of $40,000 and the $55,000 preferential tax debt, only $810,000 remains for ordinary unsecured claims of $1,620,000. They receive 50 cents for every dollar proved. A declaration of solvency would therefore be false.
