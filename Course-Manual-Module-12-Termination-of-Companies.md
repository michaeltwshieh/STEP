## Module 12: Termination of Companies

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- review the giving of security by fixed and floating charge and enforcement thereof by the appointment of a receiver
- outline ways in which an insolvent company may continue to trade as an alternative to liquidation
- review the typical process and procedures involved in
- -a compulsory winding-up or liquidation of a company
- -a voluntary winding-up
- explain the difference between dissolution and striking off and take a look at voluntary striking off.

## Introduction

This module is about the termination of companies. Nonetheless, we start in section 1 by looking at some alternatives to a company liquidation, including entering into a compromise arrangement with creditors, and the appointment of receivers and corporate administration. In section 2 we begin our analysis of formal termination.

A company is created by a strict legal process. Similarly, it can be terminated only by a legal process, by following the winding-up provisions of applicable companies legislation. This procedure brings the life of a company to an end and is dealt with in sections 2, 3 and 4. The process involves the appointment of a liquidator to take control of the company and:

- collect in its assets and debts receivable and convert them into money
- pay all outstanding debts to creditors of the company
- distribute any surplus to shareholders according to their rights, if there is anything left over.

When this has been carried out, the company is formally dissolved and is removed from the register of companies.

Finally, there is a process by which the registrar may strike off a company on the basis that it is no longer carrying on business. In this regard, a hitherto informal practice of voluntarily striking off a company that is no longer required now has a statutory basis in England insofar as a company can request its own striking off and removal from the register of companies. Striking off is dealt with in section 6.

First, we will consider courses of action that are alternatives to the winding-up of a company.

## 1. Alternatives to liquidation

There are two things to note about the alternative procedures to liquidation in the US and UK. Firstly, the statutory provisions apply to companies incorporated in the jurisdiction where such legislation is enacted. Accordingly, the US and UK provisions apply to US and UK companies respectively. The critical question, therefore, is whether similar provisions apply to companies incorporated offshore. Secondly, the issues that relate to a compromise arrangement with creditors, appointment of receivers and corporate rescues are relevant mainly to commercial, actively trading companies, rather than passive asset-holding vehicles. Nonetheless, they do present some interesting issues.

In a nutshell, whenever creditors realise that a company is in financial difficulty and cannot repay borrowings, or pay for goods or services it needs to carry on business, the creditors have several commercial options available to them beyond simply suing for recovery.

- A creditor who has security over corporate assets as collateral can take steps to enforce the security, which typically involves appointing a receiver to assume control of the secured assets, often selling them and applying the proceeds to discharge the debt owed to the secured creditor; any balance belongs to the company (reviewed in section 1.1 below).
- A creditor who does not have security could precipitate discussions to induce the board of directors to propose a compromise arrangement with the company's creditors (reviewed in section 1.2) or consider appointing an administrator, in those jurisdictions where this remedy is available (reviewed in section 1.3 below).
- Alternatively, a creditor could simply force the company into liquidation by way of a compulsory winding-up order, reviewed in section 2 below.

The company itself, by its directors, could also initiate the procedures outlined in (b) above as an alternative to liquidation when the company is insolvent or is approaching insolvency, and either propose an arrangement with creditors (section 1.2), or apply for an administration order or Ch.11 protection, wherever these procedures are available (section 1.3).

In reviewing these alternatives you will note that, save in relation to the enforcement of security, a modern objective of the legislation is to rescue the company as a going concern rather than forcing it into a winding-up so that the company must cease to trade and liquidate its assets.

## 1.1 Enforcing security by the appointment of a receiver and manager

When a company borrows money from a lender, such as a lending institution or bank, it is obliged to comply with the terms of the borrowing and is often required to give the lender some sort of security.

Typical terms of borrowing include express provisions containing a list of 'events of default' which trigger automatic and immediate repayment of the debt. For example, if repayment is 'on demand' , an act of default may be that a demand for repayment has not been complied with within 'x' days; and/or, in the alternative, that the company has defaulted upon an interest payment or scheduled capital repayment and fails to make good such default within a specified number of days, in which case the lender is entitled to call for immediate repayment.

Corporate borrowing may also be secured. A creditor may insist upon being given security for borrowed funds, such as having seniority if the debtor company goes bust. A company often provides security by way of a fixed or floating charge over its assets.

- A fixed charge is a legal mortgage over specified assets, usually real estate, which essentially allows the company to retain possession and use of the secured property but precludes the company from dealing with such property while the mortgage subsists.
- A floating charge is an equitable charge on some or all of the company's present and future movable assets, which permits the company not only to retain possession and use of such assets, but also the right to deal with the secured assets in the ordinary course of business. The floating charge will 'crystallise' over the secured assets and become a fixed charge on the occurrence of a 'crystallising event' . This may be any event described in the loan agreement or debenture, such as the company's insolvency, or when the value of its assets falls below a certain level, or when the company defaults on repayment of capital or an instalment of interest. When the repayment obligation is expressed to be 'on demand', a crystallising event may simply be that a demand for repayment has not been complied with.

Typical terms of an institutional loan agreement or debenture include a provision whereby a secured creditor is entitled to appoint a receiver and manager upon the occurrence of an event of default (for a fixed charge) or an event of crystallisation and default (for borrowing secured by a floating charge). 207  The appointment of a receiver is usually, therefore, precipitated by some sort of default on the part of the company, at which point the secured creditor gives notice to the company (and often the registrar and the company's other creditors). An application to the court is not necessary (although such an application can be made in certain narrow circumstances).

Once the secured creditor appoints a receiver, the powers of the directors over the secured assets cease. Where the secured assets comprise all the assets of the company, the powers of the directors therefore cease altogether. The receiver takes over. The receiver owes fiduciary duties to the secured creditor, not to the company. In other words, the receiver is conferred with wide discretionary equitable powers which must be exercised in the best interests of the secured creditor. In practice this means that the receiver must:

- run the business of the company, if feasible, in order to sell it as a going concern, or

- if the company cannot be sold as a going concern, the receiver must cease trading and sell the secured assets on behalf of the secured creditor for the best price, and
- -pay their own costs and expenses from the proceeds of sale
- -repay the amount owed to the secured creditor
- -prepare financial accounts, and account to the company for any remaining balance.

The appointment of a receiver is an equitable remedy, rather than one governed by companies legislation. Nonetheless, in English law, successive Companies Acts have interfered with the remedy in order to try and balance the interests of the secured creditor with the interests of the company. For instance, companies legislation requirements enacted in the 1948 and 1985 Companies Acts ensure that receivers are more independent (disqualifying companies and bankrupts from being appointed as receivers), providing for certain oversight and supervision (over receivers' remuneration and requiring accounts to be delivered and returns submitted to the registrar), while at the same time improving their effectiveness in protecting the interests of the secured creditor (by increasing their powers of obtaining information and documents). 208

English law has matured in the area of the appointment of receivers and the relevant statutory provisions have been removed from the companies legislation and are now found in the Insolvency Act 1986 as amended, where a receiver/manager is now known as an 'administrative receiver' . The critical question, however, is what is the nature of the legislation, if any, enacted offshore?

The remedy of the appointment of a receiver has its genesis in the law of equity, which applies throughout the offshore common law jurisdictions. The relevant legislation that has been grafted onto the law of equity as regards receivers tends to use the 1948 Act as a template, rather than the more sophisticated provisions of the English Insolvency Act. A receiver can be readily appointed out-of-court by a secured creditor with a fixed or floating charge according to the legal and equitable rights conferred upon the secured creditor in the instrument creating the charge. Companies legislation is largely peripheral.

## 1.2 Compromise arrangements with creditors

When directors realise that their company is insolvent, or is approaching insolvency, they are at risk of incurring criminal and civil liability for wrongful and/or fraudulent trading. They ought, therefore, to cease to trade and put the company into liquidation.

The directors may, however, believe that, with a fair wind and a modicum of cooperation, they may be able to nurse the company back to profitable health. In these circumstances, they can take the initiative and approach the creditors of the company and, by delicate negotiations, try to persuade the creditors to continue to support the company.

A proposed compromise arrangement put to creditors often, for instance, includes a plan whereby:

- creditors agree not to enforce payment of their debt, or enforce their security, for an agreed period of time, to enable the company to find its feet and become financially stable again and, in the meantime, interest payments are suspended or reduced
- a proportion of the debt may be written off or converted into equity.

In order for a negotiated compromise with creditors to work, all the company's creditors must be bound by it. The problem for a company that is struggling financially is that all the company's creditors seldom unanimously agree to the planned compromise. Any single creditor who disagrees with the proposed compromise could insist on being paid at once and in full. In other words, a dissenting creditor can seek recovery of the money owed in a court of law or apply for the company to be wound up. Accordingly, companies legislation was enacted that enable a prescribed majority of creditors to make a compromise arrangement that is binding upon the minority of creditors, rather than requiring a unanimous consensus.

The specific statutory provisions were introduced in England by the Companies Act 1929, and repeated in ss.206-207 of the Companies Act 1948, then ss.425-427 of the Companies Act 1985. In this procedure, the company, by its directors, makes an application to the court for it to convene a meeting of all creditors. The meeting of creditors is a formal opportunity for the directors to disclose the financial health of the company and propose a compromise arrangement: a 'rescue package' . The proposed compromise is debated at the creditors' meeting and voted on. If a majority of 75% in value of all creditors present and voting in person or by proxy agree to the compromise it becomes binding once approved by the court. In other words, dissenting creditors can be forced to accept the compromise. When the court determines whether a compromise arrangement should be approved it has been held that the test is whether 'an intelligent and honest person acting in their own interests would approve' . 209

The procedure described above as laid down in the old Companies Acts in England was made rather more sophisticated by the Insolvency Act 1986 as amended. This legislation introduced a requirement for the compromise arrangement to be approved not by the court but by an independent, qualified and regulated insolvency practitioner to ensure it is fair. Furthermore, statutory duties of oversight are imposed upon the identified insolvency practitioner to ensure that the compromise is implemented correctly. Dissenting creditors can apply to the court if they feel it is not.

The procedure for achieving a compromise arrangement with creditors is not often used in England (partly as a matter of historical commercial culture) but a similar type of compromise arrangement is common in the US, where it is described as 'filing for Ch.11 protection' , referring to the statutory provisions in Ch.11 of the US Bankruptcy Code. 210  It has been used on numerous occasions often, but not always, with successful results. 211  We have included a description of this procedure here under the heading 'compromise arrangements with creditors' , rather than a similar procedure enacted in England called administration (reviewed in section 1.3 below) because in Ch.11 the debtor company usually remains in control of the management of the business of the company, albeit subject to supervision and oversight of the court, rather than handing over management to a third party (an insolvency practitioner). 212  As you will see, the Ch.11 procedures are more far-reaching and sophisticated than the English 1948 Companies Act equivalent.

The directors of the company or its creditors can initiate the Ch.11 procedure but it is usually done by the directors, who file with a federal bankruptcy court for protection. Under Ch.11, the directors are required to put together a 'Chapter 11 plan' , which allows them to reorganise and manage the company for a fixed period. The period is designed to provide 'breathing space' for the directors of the company to reorganise its business, its finances and debt. The plan must satisfy statutory criteria and essentially reorganise the company's business and finances to enable the company to emerge from the bankruptcy in due course as a viable concern. During this breathing space, a so-called 'moratorium' exists so that any pending lawsuits against the company are stayed, creditors cannot seek to recover the monies due to them, and secured creditors cannot enforce their security. Thus, the directors are able to nurse the company back to health and stability.

The Chapter 11 plan of reorganisation is prepared by the directors and proposed to The Ch.11 plan of reorganisation is prepared by the directors and proposed to creditors, who are entitled to vote on it. If it is approved it becomes binding upon the company and creditors, subject to the approval of the court. If the plan is rejected by creditors, the court can convert the procedure to a Ch.7 compulsory liquidation or dismiss the suit, remove the statutory protection and return the company to 'normal' status.

Creditors usually support a Ch.11 application when they believe that they stand a better chance of recovering their outstanding debt than would be likely in an immediate Ch.7 liquidation. If approved and formally adopted by the court, the directors typically continue to run the company as a going concern, and to implement and give effect to the Ch.11 reorganisation plan subject to making regular reports to a supervising committee of creditors. Upon completion of the plan, it is anticipated that the company will emerge fitter and leaner, with a clean bill of health and in a financial position to continue as a viable going concern.

Again, the critical question for offshore corporate service providers is, which type of procedure, if any, has been enacted by the offshore legislature and is available to offshore companies?

The companies legislation enacted in the majority of offshore financial centres has copied the Companies Act 1948, rather than the more sophisticated version found in the English Insolvency Act 1986 or Ch.11 of the US Bankruptcy Code. 213  This requires a draft compromise arrangement to be drawn by the directors of the company and proposed to all creditors at a creditors' meeting. If approved by 75% of the creditors of the company by value present and entitled to vote in person or proxy, the draft compromise arrangement becomes binding subject to its being approved by the court.

The main exception is the BVI, 214  where Pt 2 of the Insolvency Act is modelled upon the more sophisticated English Insolvency Act 1986. The directors of a BVI BC may by resolution appoint a regulated BVI 'insolvency practitioner' as an 'interim supervisor' if they 'believe on reasonable grounds that the company is insolvent or is likely to become insolvent' (ss.20 and 21 of the Insolvency Act 2003). Statutory duties are imposed upon the interim supervisor to prepare a report for creditors (s.25) and convene a creditors' meeting (s.27). At such meeting, the creditors are asked to consider and vote on whether to accept or reject the proposed compromise arrangement (s.30). If the vote is in favour of the arrangement it becomes binding upon the company and creditors. But this is where the procedure departs from the 1948 Act model insofar as, rather than the board of directors implementing the approved compromise arrangement, the interim supervisor becomes the 'supervisor' who must then implement the arrangement (s.35) and submit regular reports and accounts to creditors and the court (s.37).

## 1.3 Corporate administration

As indicated above, modern corporate and insolvency legislation enacted onshore have a new objective, as is illustrated by the law relating to compromise arrangements and Chapter 11 protection. The objective is to try to facilitate a rescue of an ailing company as a going concern rather than to wind up the business and liquidate the assets. The relatively new English procedure for the appointment of an administrator under the provisions of the Insolvency Act 1986 as amended, has a similar objective.

Either the directors of the company itself or any one or more of its creditors may apply to a court 215  for an Administration Order in circumstances where:

- the company is, or is likely to become, unable to pay its debts

- the appointment of a corporate administrator is reasonably likely to achieve a statutory objective, 216  essentially a rescue of the company as a going concern while ensuring that creditors are paid or, at least, are likely to achieve a more advantageous realisation of the company's assets, or better percentage collection, than on a winding-up.

An Administration Order appoints a regulated and qualified insolvency practitioner as administrator of the company and confers certain duties upon them. The administrator's first duty is to investigate the affairs of the company and prepare a plan of administration to reorganise the business of the company and its finances and debt, to be put to creditors for their approval at a meeting of creditors. 217 If the proposal is accepted by a majority in value of those creditors present and voting in person or by proxy, the plan becomes binding. The court and registrar of companies are informed accordingly. If the majority of creditors vote against the plan of administration, the court can make a compulsory winding-up order or dismiss the administration altogether and let events take their course. The second major duty of the administrator is to implement the plan of administration and put it into effect as the agent of the company. In these circumstances, the administrator assumes all the powers of the directors, including the power to run the business, manage and sell assets and make all necessary payments, etc.

Here comes the critical question again: how far, if at all, have these provisions been copied by the major offshore centres?

The English type of administration procedure has been enacted in Australia and Canada, although it has not been universally adopted by the offshore jurisdictions. The corporate administration remedy for both the debtor company and its creditors is not available according to companies and insolvency legislation enacted by the major offshore jurisdictions reviewed in this study text save for the BVI, Gibraltar, Guernsey and Mauritius. These jurisdictions have enacted insolvency legislation that follows the English model found in the Insolvency Act 1986. The others have not. 218

## 2. Compulsory winding-up

The terms 'winding-up' and 'liquidation' are synonymous. There are two basic types:

- i. compulsory winding-up, reviewed in this section; and
- ii. voluntary winding up, reviewed in section 3.

The distinction is that a compulsory winding up is imposed on the company by an order of the court, whereas a voluntary winding-up is, as the name suggests, voluntary insofar as it is initiated by the company itself.

The grounds that must be established before a company may be wound-up compulsorily and the procedures that must be followed are based in statute and are therefore set out in applicable legislation. As you will know, the statutory provisions enacted by the major offshore jurisdictions are typically based upon those found in England's Companies Act 1948, and modified for local conditions. England has subsequently removed matters relating to winding-up from the companies legislation and enacted free-standing and sophisticated insolvency legislation, namely the Insolvency Act 1986 as amended. Of the major offshore jurisdictions reviewed in this study text, only the BVI and Guernsey have done something similar. Accordingly, in the offshore jurisdictions, save for the BVI and Guernsey, the 1948 Act remains the essential template.

## 2.1 Grounds for compulsory winding-up

There are occasions when the court may be asked to terminate a company and order that it cease trading, liquidate its assets and distribute the net proceeds of sale to creditors and any surplus to shareholders. The grounds upon which the court may order a compulsory winding-up of a company have been set out in successive Companies Acts in England 219  and similar provisions have been imported by the offshore jurisdictions. These grounds include the following.

- That the company is unable to pay its debts. 220 This is the ground most often relied on in support of an application by creditors of a company for an order that the company be wound up. A company is deemed unable to pay its debts when a creditor has served a written demand on the debtor company in the prescribed form requiring payment of a debt legally payable and due and which the company fails to pay or secure within 21 days. Having regard to the fact that most offshore companies are formed in order to hold assets, rather than trade in the traditional sense, very few offshore companies are wound up in this manner.
- That the court is of the opinion that it would be 'just and equitable' to wind up the company. 221  The court may consider a winding-up to be just and equitable in situations where, for example:
- -the objects of the company have failed or have become illegal since the company was set up
- -the company cannot continue its business for some reason, such as deadlock on the board and at shareholder level

- -the company is carrying on business in an illegal or fraudulent manner
- -the regulator suspects that the company has been involved in criminal conduct, such as an investment fraud or money laundering, when it often has the power under local regulations to terminate the company on the grounds that it is just and equitable to do so.

## 2.2 Procedure

The following is a summary of the general stages leading up to a compulsory winding-up order.

## 2.2.1  Application to the court

An application for a winding-up order can be made by the shareholders or directors of the company, but it is usually made by a creditor as part of an action to recover money owed. The application has to be in the prescribed form supported by evidence of the ground relied on. The debtor company can respond.

## 2.2.2  Hearing

The court hears the application. If, after reviewing the evidence and being satisfied that one or more of the statutory grounds have been established, the judge will usually make an order for the company to be wound up, and appoint a liquidator.

## 2.2.3  Winding-up order

In England, both under the 1948 and 1985 Companies Acts, and under the Insolvency Act 1986 as amended, the official receiver becomes the liquidator of the company once a winding-up order has been made. The official receiver's first duty is to call a meeting of creditors for choosing a liquidator, who will replace the official receiver. If there is no nomination, or for other good reason such as insufficient assets to cover the costs of the liquidation, the official receiver may remain as liquidator. Note, however, that there is no public officer similar in name or role to the official receiver in the offshore jurisdictions. Accordingly, the English practice has been streamlined so that the court appoints an independent liquidator who has previously consented to act.

The winding-up order must be sent to the registrar of companies and advertised in accordance with local requirements, typically in the press and Gazette . The notice and advert will look similar to Appendices 30C and 30D of the online Study Resources.

## 2.3 Consequences of the making of a winding-up order

When a winding-up order is made, the following things occur.

- i. A liquidator, usually a qualified accountant, is appointed by the court and assumes control of the books, records and assets of the company to commence the winding-up process described in section 2.4 below.
- ii. To preserve the assets of the company for the benefit of creditors, any further transfers of property belonging to the company, unless approved by the court, are void - s.232 of the Companies Act 1948.

<!-- image -->

- iii. Any lawsuits against the company are stayed, i.e. must cease, unless the court orders otherwise - s.231 of the Companies Act 1948.
- iv. The powers of the directors cease - s.232 of the Companies Act 1948.
- v. Floating charges over the assets of the company crystallise - s.322 of the Companies Act 1948. These are charges that float over the company's moveable assets rather than attaching to a specific asset (see section 1.1 above). A floating charge is said to crystallise when a 'crystallising event' occurs, as described in the instrument creating the charge. It also crystallises when a company ceases to trade or is wound-up. In these circumstances, the charge no longer floats over the assets of the company but descends and attaches itself to the class of secured assets belonging to the company at the time of crystallisation.
- vi. A statement of the company's affairs must be prepared and filed by the directors, setting out details of the assets and liabilities of the company, as well as a list of known creditors - s.235 of the Companies Act 1948.

## 2.4 Liquidator's duties

The core duty of a liquidator is to close down, rather than continue, the business of the company, and to collect in and sell the company's assets in the best interests of the company. A liquidator is considered, in law, an agent who owes a fiduciary duty as well as a common law duty of care to the company. When the company is insolvent such duty is, in practice, indirectly owed to the company's creditors. 222

In order to fulfil this core duty, the liquidator is given extensive statutory powers to assume possession and control of, to manage and to sell, the company's assets. 223  In practice, the liquidator performs the following tasks.

- The liquidator must assume control of the books and records of the company, and has extensive power to require persons to produce the same. The liquidator then conducts a thorough investigation into the financial affairs of the company to determine why it failed. The liquidator may, for this purpose, apply to the court for a public examination of the officers in order to investigate any misconduct in the management of the company. A liquidator who finds mismanagement, fraudulent or wrongful trading may file a lawsuit against the directors to recover compensation on behalf of the company. A liquidator who finds that a fraudulent preference has been conferred upon certain creditors may file a lawsuit against

the preferred creditor to recover the preference. 224  Any monies recovered are said to 'swell' the assets available to pay the company's creditors.

- The liquidator winds-up and liquidates the company by:
- closing down, rather than continuing, the business of the company, which essentially means terminating employees and disclaiming contracts, i.e. serving notice on parties with contracts with the company, stating that the company is no longer bound by such contracts - so that the other parties cannot insist upon performing the contract, thereby adding to the debts of the company (such parties becoming unsecured creditors for moneys outstanding as at the date of the winding-up order)
- taking possession of the assets of the company and collecting debts due to it
- selling the assets in the best interests of the company, with reasonable care
- determining the identity and value of creditors and whether they can 'prove' that money is owed to them
- paying proper debts due to creditors of the company according to a statutory order, discharging costs first, then preferential creditors, then ordinary creditors pari passu (explained further in section 4 below)
- preparing and submitting final accounts to the court and to creditors and applying for a final order of dissolution. A specimen statement of account is illustrated in Appendix 30G of the online Study Resources.

## 3. Voluntary winding-up

A company may be wound up voluntarily (without an order of the court) in circumstances where:

- the members of the company commence the winding-up process voluntarily, usually because they no longer wish the company to continue (the most common situation)
- creditors of the company decide that the company should be wound up because of debt or liquidity problems, but do not wish to involve the court in the process.

## 3.1 Members' voluntary winding-up

## 3.1.1  Grounds for a members' voluntary winding-up

There are often two grounds on which a members' voluntary winding-up may proceed.

- The articles may specify that the company should be wound up at the end of a specified period or on the happening of a particular event. If such a time arises, or event occurs, the members are usually required to pass an ordinary resolution recording their desire to proceed with the winding-up of the company.

- The members may decide that the company is no longer required and that the assets of the company (after discharging all its debts and liabilities) should be distributed to the shareholders. This ground occurs for reasons unconnected with the company's solvency. A special resolution of the shareholders is usually required in order to place the company into voluntary liquidation.

## 3.1.2  Usual procedure

The following account summarises how a members' voluntary winding-up should proceed under typical provisions contained in the Companies Act 1948 ss.303-310. For a more detailed review, see the specific legislation governing company liquidations in the jurisdiction where you work. 225

## i. Declaration of solvency

The directors must first prepare a declaration confirming that they have undertaken a thorough review of the finances of the company and that, in their opinion, there are sufficient assets to pay all the liabilities and debts incurred by the company in full. In other words, that the company is solvent. A specimen is illustrated in Appendix 30A. In order to substantiate this opinion, it is usual for the directors to append a statement of the assets and liabilities of the company to the declaration. The declaration of solvency is an important document. Directors who are fraudulent or negligent in checking the information contained in a declaration of solvency face criminal penalties (a fine and/or imprisonment).

## ii. Special resolution

The members must usually pass a special resolution to terminate the company and appoint a liquidator. A special written resolution of the members is illustrated in Appendix 30B of the online Study Resources. Some jurisdictions do not distinguish between an ordinary and special resolution. The IBC jurisdictions, such as the BVI and the Bahamas, permit either the members or the directors to pass the resolution, provided they are authorised to do so in the company's memorandum or articles. 226  A copy of the resolution must be sent to the registrar of companies.

## iii. Liquidator appointed

The appointment of the liquidator is usually made at the time, and in the same resolution, as the resolution to wind up the company - see para.2 of Appendix 30B. Upon the appointment of a liquidator, all the powers of the directors of the company cease. A notice of the appointment must be published in the local press and the Gazette and sent to the registrar. ## 3.2 Creditors' voluntary winding-up

If a company is insolvent (i.e. is unable to pay all its debts in full), the company does not have to wait for a creditor to apply to the court for a compulsory winding-up order. It may commence a voluntary winding-up. When the company is insolvent, the voluntary winding-up is called a creditors' voluntary winding-up . This is because the creditors of the company have a far greater say in the conduct of the winding-up procedure.

The usual procedure based upon ss.292-300 of the Companies Act 1948 is as follows.

## 3.2.1  Directors convene a general meeting of shareholders to pass a special resolution to wind up the company

In most cases, the directors of a company experiencing financial problems want to arrange for the company to be terminated as quickly as possible to prevent the financial situation from worsening and to protect them from incurring liability for wrongful or fraudulent trading. They are prevented from allowing a members' winding-up to proceed as they are unable to sign a declaration of solvency (as assets are insufficient to meet all debts). In such situations, the directors are allowed to call a general meeting of the shareholders so that they may pass a special resolution to wind up the company. Alternatively, some jurisdictions do not distinguish between ordinary and special resolutions and others permit the directors to pass the resolution. The resolution, when passed, must be filed with the registrar and published.

## 3.2.2  Directors convene a creditors' meeting to appoint liquidator

The directors then arrange a meeting of all the creditors of the company. At this meeting, the directors present details of the financial position of the company, including a list of its assets and liabilities. The creditors, rather than the shareholders, choose the liquidator by majority vote of those present in person or proxy.

## 3.2.3  Liquidator winds up the company

The liquidator, upon appointment, assumes all the usual powers of management and sale of the assets of the company as in a members' voluntary winding up. Subsequent meetings of creditors and members are then held periodically (at the same intervals as those for a members' voluntary winding-up). Once the affairs of the company have been wound up, and all assets collected and sold, and the proceeds shared between creditors in the manner described in section 4 below, final meetings of members and creditors are held, at which the accounts are presented for discussion and approval. Filings are usually required in line with those necessary for a members' voluntary winding-up.

## 4. Payment of debts

The winding-up of a company is conducted for the protection of creditors of the company. A liquidator is required to take all company assets into his or her possession, sell them and use the proceeds to satisfy the debts of the company. The debts must be paid in a proper order. Only when all the company's debts have been paid may the liquidator distribute any surplus to the shareholders.

<!-- image -->

The order for payment of creditors, according to ss.317 and 319 of the Companies Act 1948, is as follows:

- i. a creditor with a fixed charge over property of the company will usually enforce that security by sale of the secured property. A secured creditor with a fixed charge is not, therefore, usually paid by the liquidator. Only if the sale of the mortgaged asset does not produce sufficient funds to discharge its debt in full will the secured creditor claim the shortfall as an unsecured creditor;
- ii. the costs of the liquidation, including the fees of and expenses incurred by the liquidator, are paid in priority to all other debts, save debts secured by a fixed charge;
- iii. if any money remains after costs, etc. the preferential debts are paid next in line. These include any taxes, duties or other funds due to the government of the jurisdiction of incorporation and any wages outstanding to employees of the company (and moneys to depositors of a licensed bank, if a bank is being wound up).

If there is not enough money to pay each such creditor in full, the creditors are paid pari passu , i.e. each creditor receives the same percentage of the amount due to them (e.g. x cents per dollar owed);

- iv. next in line for payment are those creditors whose debts are secured by a crystallised floating charge over the assets of the company. These creditors are paid out in the order in which their floating charge was given. 229  For example, assume that four creditors (A-D) have each made a loan of $100,000 to the company, secured by a floating charge over the company's assets. The loans were made on 1 January, 1 February, 1 March and 1 April. If there is only $300,000 available from the net proceeds of sale of the charged assets, creditors A, B and C will each receive $100,000, and creditor D will receive nothing; and
- v. the ordinary unsecured creditors are paid last. Each debt is treated equally, which means that creditors are paid pari passu . So if there is insufficient cash to pay the ordinary debts in full, the creditors receive proportionate amounts (x cents in the dollar).

## 5. Reinstatement of liquidated companies

There is a procedure in most common law jurisdictions whereby the liquidator or shareholders can apply, within a very limited time period after formal completion of a liquidation (usually no longer than six months) for the liquidated company to be re-instated. This action may be taken if the liquidator discovers additional assets or liabilities which, for any reason, have been overlooked during the formal liquidation proceedings.

This procedure is very seldom used in practice, simply because company liquidation is a very formal procedure, and professional liquidators do not often overlook substantial assets or liabilities when they are carrying out their functions, and they make regular reports to creditors and/or the court.

## 6. Striking off

There are nowadays, in most jurisdictions, two ways for a company to be struck off the register of companies. Firstly the traditional, old-fashioned compulsory striking off by the registrar for just cause, which is based upon the Companies Act 1948 model and has been adopted across the jurisdictions. Alternatively, there is voluntary striking off, based upon a modern model which was introduced in England by the Companies Act 2006, and has been copied by a number of, though not all, financial centres.

## 6.1 Compulsory striking off

Under s.353 of the Companies Act 1948, a registrar of companies who believes that a company has ceased to carry on business may take steps to have the company struck off the register of companies. Similar provisions have been enacted offshore. 230

The registrar must have just cause before removing a company in this manner. Usually, 'just cause' arises where there is failure on the part of a company to reply to letters or failure to submit an annual return (where one is required) or pay the annual registration fee.

The process begins with the registrar sending notice to the company at its registered office, asking whether it is still carrying on its business. If a reply is received which suggests that the company is still operating, so be it; the registrar will cease the procedure provided all fees have been paid and other filings are up to date.

If there is no reply, the registrar serves a second notice, stating that if a response is not received within a certain time period, usually two months, the company will be struck off. On the expiration of this period, and assuming there is no evidence to suggest that the company is operating, the registrar will strike the company from the register. Upon being compulsorily struck off in this manner, any assets belonging to the company are forfeit to the state.

This method of termination was, for many years, used when the company no longer has any assets and the beneficial owner did not wish to incur the additional costs by following the formal voluntary winding-up procedure. (This is probably the reason why England created a new statutory voluntary striking off procedure in the 2006 Companies Act.) 231 In the absence of a statutory provision permitting voluntary striking off, while it may be practical and save costs, it is not regarded as a satisfactory way of dissolving a company, for several reasons.

- Firstly, the liability of the officers of the company for their acts or defaults (particularly for fraudulent or wrongful trading if the company is subsequently found to have unpaid liabilities) and the liability of the members of the company for unpaid calls on their shares continue and do not abate, notwithstanding the fact that the company has been struck off.
- Secondly, the registrar's power to strike off a company is entirely discretionary, so there is often no telling when a registrar may strike a company off; the company could remain on the records for many years before the process is complete.
- Finally, the company may be reinstated to the register, perhaps many years after the original striking off, and thus a striking off does not actually dissolve the company until such time as the period has expired within which it can be reinstated. 232

Any assets held by the company at the time it is struck off the register are essentially without a legal owner and so pass automatically to the state, including any funds left in a bank account.

## 6.2 Voluntary striking off

Some companies reach the end of their useful life and are no longer required. According to traditional company law, if it is solvent, a company can be placed into voluntary winding up by its members (or, in some jurisdictions, by its directors). But this involves a somewhat protracted and expensive process as it must be conducted by a qualified liquidator following statutory procedures. There must be an easier way of bringing the company's affairs to an orderly close, particularly if the company has no liabilities, no assets and is no longer trading. At one time, service providers were tempted simply to discharge all liabilities of the company, denude it of assets, and then deliberately fail to pay its annual registration fee, hoping that the registrar would, in due course, strike the company off the register by the method described in 6.1 above. As noted, however, this was a somewhat unsatisfactory solution. As a result, a number of the more progressive jurisdictions now permit voluntary striking off subject to the fulfilment of statutory conditions designed to protect creditors. 233

<!-- image -->

There are minor variations across the jurisdictions, but the application for voluntary striking off, and pre-conditions that must be complied with, are described below.

Firstly, an application is commenced by the board resolving to apply to voluntarily strike off the company in circumstances when the director(s) is (are) satisfied that the statutory conditions are met. A specimen board resolution is illustrated in Appendix 31A.

The statutory conditions are essentially designed to ensure that the company is in a 'zero state' , namely, it has:

- no assets, in particular no real estate in the jurisdiction of incorporation
- no debts or liabilities or outstanding claims against it
- no legal proceedings or insolvency matters are pending
- is no longer trading (and, in some jurisdictions, has not traded for a specified number of months).

The directors should then apply to the registrar for striking off. Depending upon the jurisdiction, this is done by filing with the registrar either a prescribed form containing a declaration of compliance confirming the statutory conditions are met (illustrated, generically, in Appendix 31B of the online Study Resources) or a copy of the resolution together with a statutory declaration of compliance (illustrated in Appendix 31C of the online Study Resources).

Upon receipt of an application in proper form, the registrar must publish concise details of the application in the Gazette, seeking objections. If no objections are received (presumably from creditors or claimants) within a prescribed period, usually two months, the registrar will strike the company off the register of companies, publish another notice in the Gazette and issue some sort of certificate of voluntary striking off.

Upon striking off in this manner, the company is dissolved and ceases to exist as a legal entity. Much as with compulsory striking off, however, it can be reinstated and restored to the register in the manner described in section 6.3 below.

## 6.3 Reinstatement of companies that have been removed from the register

It is possible for a company that has been removed from the register as a result of a compulsory striking off to be reinstated. 235

The reinstatement procedure is one of two types:

- i. judicial ; or
- ii. administrative .

In those jurisdictions that still follow the 1948 Act, reinstatement is a judicial act that requires an application to the court by a director, a member or a creditor of the company for an order permitting the reinstatement of the company by the register. However, some jurisdictions have followed the modern English approach by making reinstatement an administrative procedure in that it requires an application to the registrar rather than the court. This is the approach taken in the more progressive jurisdictions such as the Bahamas, BVI, Guernsey, Hong Kong, Isle of Man, and Singapore, where reinstatement is administrative in nature, saving attorney's fees. 236

An application for reinstatement has to be made within a prescribed period of time. The period differs from jurisdiction to jurisdiction - for IBCs an application must be made within five years from the date of the striking off; it is six years in England; 10 years in other jurisdictions; in Panama, reinstatement can take place at any time, but this is unusual.

The procedure to reinstate by court order requires the company's directors or members (or creditors) to engage attorneys to file an originating summons with an affidavit in support, establishing the applicant's legal standing and explaining why reinstatement is sought. A short hearing before a judge may be necessary, but the court will usually make an order for reinstatement on condition that all annual fees outstanding calculated to the date of reinstatement, have been, or will be, paid.

For an administrative reinstatement, the directors, members or creditors of the company file a prescribed form with the registrar. This form usually includes a declaration of compliance, which essentially states that:

- a. the applicant is eligible to apply to reinstate the company;
- b. the company's statutory registers are, or will be made, up to date; and
- c. all outstanding filing fees have been paid. A generic form is illustrated in Appendix 31D of the online Study Resources.

In those jurisdictions where voluntary striking off and administrative reinstatement are permitted, an administrative reinstatement cannot be made by the registrar following voluntary striking off. In these circumstances, an application must be made to court.

The general effect of a court or administrative order that authorises the reinstatement, and the registrar's reinstatement of the company's name to the register of companies, is that the company is deemed to have continued in existence as if it had not been struck off - it is owned by the same shareholders, managed by the same directors, owns all its pre-existing assets and, crucially, owes all pre-existing liabilities.

BVI, where the only two methods or disposing of a company voluntarily are voluntary winding up and compulsory striking off for non-payment of fees – see s.213 of the BVI BCA, b. Jersey, where instead of voluntary striking off there is summary winding up in accordance with ss.145–154 of the Companies (Jersey) Law, c. the Cayman Islands where there is no statutory basis for voluntary striking off but it is permitted by the registrar on an informal basis; and d. the Bahamas – s.165 of the IBCA.
