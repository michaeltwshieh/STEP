The reward side of the proposition is easy enough to state, and it is usually a fee: an annual retainer for the statutory functions and a meeting, with time costs charged on top for anything out of the ordinary. Pricing the other side is much harder. The exposure a director takes on is personal, it is owed to the company rather than shared with it, and the incorporation of the company he has agreed to run does nothing whatever to shield him from it. The veil described in Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd protects members from the debts of their company. It has never protected a director against the consequences of his own breach of duty. That asymmetry is the whole of the problem, and it is felt most sharply by the professional director sitting on the boards of managed companies whose business he did not choose, whose assets he has never seen, and whose beneficial owner regards him as a signature service.

Two broad duties generate almost all of the exposure. A director stands in a fiduciary position towards his company, in much the same way as a trustee stands towards beneficiaries, and he owes a separate common law duty to bring appropriate care, skill and diligence to the management of its business. Offshore, these obligations are sometimes left to the general law and sometimes restated by statute; s.120 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 requires a director to act honestly, in good faith, and in what he believes to be the best interests of the company, and s.122 imposes the care, skill and diligence a reasonable director would bring in the same circumstances. Where the legislation is silent, as in the Cayman Islands, the equitable and common law rules apply unchanged. The duties run to the company, which under the proper plaintiff rule in Foss v Harbottle is the only person who can normally sue on them. That sounds comforting until one notices who actually brings these claims. It is the liquidator, appointed after the company has failed, working through the board's decisions with the benefit of hindsight and with creditors to pay.

The fiduciary limb can be dealt with reasonably briskly, because its content is settled. Corporate powers must be exercised honestly and for proper purposes, and in Hutton v West Cork Railway Co it was said that the money is the company's and may be spent only on what is reasonably incidental to its business. Good faith alone is not enough; a director who gives corporate assets away in perfect sincerity is still in breach. A director must not deal with his own company on both sides of the transaction, and in Aberdeen Railway v Blaikie Bros the contract was set aside even though nobody suggested the terms were unfair. He must not take for himself a profit or an opportunity that belongs to the company, which is what happened in Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver, where the directors took shares in the subsidiary in their own names and had to account for the gain when the cinemas were sold. Nor may he accept a benefit from a third party by reason of his office, whether or not the payer meant to influence him and whether or not he was in fact influenced. The remedies bite hard: the transaction may be rescinded, an undisclosed profit must be handed over, and the director must indemnify the company for what his breach has cost it. Loss to the company is not a precondition of liability under the no-conflict rule. Being cornered in the conflict is enough.

It is the duty of care, though, that catches the offshore administrator, and the standard has moved decisively against him. Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co set a subjective test: honourable amateurs who failed to supervise a chairman who was defrauding their insurance company escaped liability because no more could reasonably be expected of men of their knowledge and experience. That protected precisely the sort of director from whom the company, its creditors and the public most needed protecting, and it did not survive the criticism. In Dorchester Finance Co Ltd v Stebbing the measure became what could be expected of an ordinary person; in Norman v Theodore Goddard it was raised again, to what could be expected of a person carrying out the functions of a director. Modern offshore legislation has locked in the objective approach, whether by the reasonably prudent person formula found in the Bahamas, Bermuda and Jersey or by the reasonable director formula of s.122 of the BVI BCA 2004. The subjective residue that remains is not the director's own competence but the circumstances: the nature of the company, the nature of the decision, and the responsibilities he actually undertook. A director cannot now plead his own inexperience. He can only argue about what the job in front of him required.

Applied to daily practice, the case law reads like a charge sheet drawn from the corporate services industry. B and C in Dorchester Finance signed cheques in blank whenever their co-director asked them to, and were ordered to make good the funds he stole. The director in Bishopsgate Investment Management v Maxwell signed, as was his habit, whatever documents his father put in front of him, and thereby signed away most of the company's assets; he was liable for making no enquiry at all into what he was executing or why. In Re D'Jan of London Ltd a director completed an insurance proposal form without reading it properly and left the company without cover. Re Barings plc confirms that functions may be delegated but responsibility for them cannot be, that a board with no adequate system of monitoring is in breach whatever its individual members knew, and, uncomfortably for the fee-earning director, that the level of a director's remuneration is relevant to the degree of responsibility he must reckon with. Reliance on others is permitted, but only within limits. The co-directors in Lexi Holdings plc v Luqman could not rely on their managing director when the state of the accounts should have raised suspicion and the family knew he had a conviction for deception, whereas in Madoff Securities International Ltd v Raven those outside the inner circle were entitled to rely on Mr Madoff's expertise and representations. Statute puts the same conditions on reliance: s.123 of the BVI BCA 2004 permits a director to rely on records, financial statements and professional advice only if he acts in good faith, makes proper inquiry where the circumstances indicate that inquiry is needed, and has no knowledge that his reliance is unwarranted. Honest error of business judgement remains outside the net, because commerce requires informed risk-taking and a company cannot sue its board every time a venture loses money. Uninformed error is a different matter entirely.

All of which exposes the largest single misconception in offshore practice. There is no such thing in law as a nominee director. The label has been used loosely by service providers for years, but a person is either a director, in which case he owes the full fiduciary and care duties and can be sued for breaching them, or he is not. Accepting the office in exchange for a fee means accepting the office. "I was doing what the client told me" is not a defence, and Article 19.1 of these articles says so in terms: the business of the Company is to be managed by the directors, not by the beneficial owner who pays for them. The client may recommend. He may not instruct. Where a service provider hands the beneficial owner a general power of attorney over the company's affairs, or unrestricted signing authority on its bank account, the directors have arguably abandoned the function they were appointed to perform, and they will answer for losses caused by their failure to safeguard the company's assets; if the client uses the company for unlawful purposes, the provider's exposure moves from the civil to the criminal, under anti money laundering and counter-terrorist financing legislation. The problem is compounded by the corporate director structure that most providers use. Those appointed to the board of the corporate director owe duties to it, and through it are linked to the affairs of every company for which it acts. One careless managed company can reach a long way back up the chain.

A narrower point can be disposed of quickly. If a director exceeds the powers the articles give him, for example by borrowing beyond the one million dollar ceiling in Article 19.3 without the ordinary resolution that article requires, the lender is generally safe. The rule in Royal British Bank v Turquand, codified offshore in provisions such as s.31 of the BVI BCA 2004, means the company is bound and cannot hide behind its own internal irregularity. The company's inability to escape the contract is exactly what leaves the director exposed on the inside. The third party recovers, and the board pays.

Insolvency is where the exposure turns from compensation into personal liability for other people's debts. Fraudulent trading, in the shape given to it by s.332 of the Companies Act 1948 and reproduced across the offshore world, allows the court on the liquidator's application to declare that those knowingly party to carrying on the business with intent to defraud creditors must contribute to the company's assets. Fraud is hard to prove, though it can be inferred, as in Re William C Leitch Ltd, where a company went on incurring debts when the directors knew there was no prospect of paying them. That difficulty produced wrongful trading, introduced in England by s.214 of the Insolvency Act 1986, which drops the requirement of dishonesty and asks only whether the director knew or ought to have concluded that there was no prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation. Whether it applies to a given director is an accident of geography. The BVI has it, in s.256 of the Insolvency Act 2003, and so does Guernsey; Bermuda, the Cayman Islands and Bahamian IBCs do not. A director of a group of companies spread across several centres is therefore exposed to different tests on identical facts, which is not a rational way to run a risk. Alongside this sits the declaration of solvency required in a members' voluntary winding-up, where a director who is fraudulent or merely negligent in checking the figures he swears to faces a fine or imprisonment. And the liquidator will look. His statutory job includes finding out why the company failed, with power to seek a public examination of the officers and to sue the board for mismanagement.

Disqualification deserves a sentence or two rather than a section. Some jurisdictions empower the court to bar an unfit person from the formation or management of companies, and where that power is absent the point is largely academic, because the financial services regulator can refuse or revoke the company management licence and end the business at a stroke. For a professional director, losing the licence is a graver commercial event than losing a lawsuit.

So what can actually be done about all this? The honest answer is that the first and best protection is not a document. It is doing the job. A director who insists on knowing what the transaction is, who reads what he is being asked to sign, who declines to sign cheques in blank, who convenes real board meetings and records real deliberation in the minutes he is required by Article 20.14 to keep, and who sees to it that proper books of account are maintained under Article 25.1, has removed most of the ground on which a claim under the duty of care would be built. Where he does not know enough, he should ask: professional advice taken and followed is the surest evidence of care, and a well drawn service agreement will let him consult the company's lawyers and accountants at the company's expense. Reliance on advice must be genuine reliance, made in good faith and after such enquiry as the circumstances call for. Prevention starts earlier still, at take-on, with proper customer due diligence on the client, the source of the funds and the intended use of the company. A director who is not comfortable with the bona fides of his client should not accept the appointment at any fee. Nor should he accept more directorships than he can honestly supervise, which is a point rarely made in practice and never made loudly enough by those whose income depends on volume.

Conflicts are managed by disclosure and abstention. Article 17.1(e) of these articles is a trap for the unwary, since it vacates the office of a director who is interested in a contract with the company; Article 17.2 rescues him only if he declares the nature of his interest at the first board meeting after the interest arises, and then does not vote, and Article 18.2 permits him to contract with the company on the same conditions. The interested director should stay out of the quorum as well as the vote. Where there is no disinterested quorum, or where the transaction is a large one, the safer course is to put it to the members and obtain their informed approval by resolution. Prior approval or subsequent ratification by the members in general meeting releases the director from liability for self-dealing, for a conflict, for a benefit received, and for negligent management, but the release has a limit that matters: under the doctrine of non-ratifiable breaches a director who is also the majority shareholder cannot vote through a whitewash of his own expropriation of corporate property, as Cook v Deeks shows. Fraud and dishonesty cannot be ratified away.

That brings us to the package the industry actually relies on, which is where a candid answer has to be sceptical. Article 29.1 exonerates the directors and officers from liability to the Company for error, oversight or mistake, but only in the absence of bad faith, fraud or dishonesty. Article 29.2 indemnifies them out of the assets of the Company, but not for their own wilful default, bad faith or fraud, and not so far as the indemnity would be void under the Companies Legislation. Article 29.3 is narrower still, and is expressly disapplied where the loss is caused by the director's own wilful default. Whether these clauses work at all depends on where the company was formed. They are lawful and effective in the Bahamas under s.58 of the IBCA 2000, in the BVI under s.132 of the BCA 2004, and in Bermuda under s.98 of the Companies Act 1981, which permits exemption and indemnity for negligence, default or breach of duty while making void any attempt to cover fraud or dishonesty. They are void in Jersey under art.77 of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, in Guernsey under s.157 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008, and in a number of other centres, and in England s.232 of the Companies Act 2006 has put both the exoneration and the indemnity beyond use. A director who relies on Article 29 without checking the statute of the jurisdiction of incorporation may find that he is relying on nothing. Even where it is valid, the indemnity is only ever as good as the assets it is payable out of, and the moment when a director most needs it is the moment when those assets have gone to the creditors. An indemnity from an insolvent company is a promissory note written on water.

The same scepticism belongs to the company management agreement. The typical exclusion clause protects the administrator from liability to the client for errors of judgement made in good faith, and the typical indemnity has the client hold the administrator harmless against third party claims; both usually carve out fraud, wilful default and gross negligence. Neither, on the face of the standard document, protects the individual director, who is not a party. Neither prevents a third party from suing anybody. And an indemnity given personally by a beneficial owner is worth precisely what the beneficial owner is worth, and only if he can still be found. There is also the drafting risk. Rae v Meek, followed offshore in West v Lazard Brothers and in Roywest Trust Corp (Bahamas) Ltd v Savannah NV, establishes that a clause pitched too widely, purporting to exclude all liability however caused, will be struck down, and that the client must have had the clause drawn to his attention and have understood what it meant. A clause that is too greedy protects nobody.

Insurance is the most useful of the protections and the most misunderstood. Article 19.4 empowers the directors to purchase and maintain cover for present and former directors and officers against liability incurred in the actual or purported execution of their powers, and offshore companies legislation commonly permits it in terms, as with s.133 of the BVI BCA 2004. A director's service agreement will normally oblige the company to maintain it. The policy will answer for the negligent director who acted in good faith and for inadvertent liability to third parties. It will not answer for deliberate default, dishonesty or fraud, because the insurance market will not underwrite moral hazard, and the risks a director most fears are frequently the ones it excludes. It is now mandatory in the regulated centres, which is a fair measure of how seriously the regulators take the exposure. It is also, in the standard service agreement, capable of being dispensed with when the directors judge the premium prohibitive, a discretion no prudent director should ever exercise in his own case.

Finally, resignation. Article 17.1(d) allows a director to leave by notice in writing to the company, and a director who has lost confidence in his client, or who is being pressed to do something he believes is not in the company's interests, should go, and should go early and in writing rather than drift. But resignation is a shield against tomorrow, not yesterday. It does not discharge a breach already committed, the liquidator can still pursue a former director for what happened on his watch, and the fiduciary obligation not to exploit information acquired in office outlives the office itself.

The statement in the question is therefore true, but it undersells the imbalance. The reward is a fee, fixed in advance and modest. The risk is open-ended, personal, and at its worst extends to paying the company's debts out of one's own pocket. The protections available are real, and a director who uses all of them properly is unlikely to be ruined by an honest mistake, since between them the exoneration article, the indemnity, the service agreement and the policy of insurance dispose of ordinary negligence rather effectively. What they do not touch is anything approaching dishonesty or deliberate default, they say nothing at all to a claim for fraudulent or wrongful trading, and they cannot restore a revoked licence. It is arguable, and a better view is, that the paperwork has been allowed to give the industry a false sense of security: the clauses are drafted by the people they protect, they are void in several of the jurisdictions where they are routinely inserted, and they are worthless against the insolvent company or the vanished client. The director who is genuinely safe is not the one with the thickest indemnity. He is the one who reads the documents, asks the awkward question, keeps the minutes, declares his interests and is willing to say no to the beneficial owner and resign if that answer is not accepted. A directorship taken on any other terms is a fee collected against a liability that has not been priced.
