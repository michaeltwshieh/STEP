1.2

Article 27.5 requires notice to be given to C, D and E as the registered members and to A and B because they are directors who are not shareholders. A person who had become entitled to a share following a member's death or bankruptcy would also receive notice, but no such person appears on these facts. The articles do not add an auditor to the recipient list.

Article 11.1 requires at least 14 clear days' written notice. Neither the day of service nor 28 August is counted, so service must take effect by 13 August. The safest course is personal delivery, with a signed receipt, or email on or before 13 August to each registered email address. Both are permitted by Article 27.1 and email takes effect when sent under Article 27.2. If post is used, Article 27.2 deems service to occur on the fourth day after posting. The notice should therefore be properly addressed, prepaid and posted no later than 9 August. Registered post or a recognised courier would give useful evidence, but actual delivery must still occur by 13 August unless all members consent to shorter notice under Article 11.2.

1.3

A non-accidental failure to give a person proper notice ordinarily invalidates the meeting and its proceedings. The same result follows if the required notice period is deliberately ignored without the unanimous short-notice consent required by Article 11.2.

Article 11.3 saves an accidental omission to give notice and accidental non-receipt by a member. Attendance in person or by proxy will normally make an objection to non-receipt difficult. The distinction matters: Article 11.3 does not cure a deliberate decision to leave D or E off the circulation list.

1.4

C cannot transact the liquidation business at the appointed meeting merely because C owns 70 per cent. Article 12.2 requires two shareholders to be personally present, and C is only one person. The meeting remains inquorate even if C's holding is enough to carry a two-thirds poll.

If no second shareholder arrives within half an hour, Article 12.3 applies. Assuming the directors called the meeting rather than the members requisitioning it, the meeting stands adjourned to the same day in the following week at the same time and place. If C alone remains present for half an hour at that adjourned meeting, the members then present constitute the quorum. C could then put the special resolution to a vote and, on a poll, C's 70 per cent would exceed the two-thirds threshold in the articles.

1.5

On a show of hands Article 13.1 gives each member one vote, whatever the size of the holding. C casts one vote for the resolution while D and E cast two votes against it. The chairman must therefore declare the special resolution lost on that vote.

C should demand a poll before, or when, the result is declared. Article 12.6 allows one member holding at least 15 per cent of the paid-up capital to do so, and C holds 70 per cent. Article 13.1 then gives one vote for each share. C's 70 per cent is greater than the two-thirds super-majority prescribed by the definition of a special resolution, while D and E together have only 30 per cent. The poll reverses the show-of-hands result and the special resolution is carried.

### SUBMIT THIS

The Bahamas is selected. Company is treated as an international business company incorporated under the International Business Companies Act 2000 of The Bahamas, with the articles attached to the examination governing its internal affairs. The shares are registered in the name of International Trust Company in its capacity as trustee of the Redhouse Trust.

3.1

Company has two organs with different jobs. International Trust Company is the registered shareholder and acts through the general meeting. It appoints and removes directors, alters the constitution and decides matters which the legislation or articles reserve to members. Ms Avril Nath and the trust officer form the board. The board manages Company's worldwide assets, including the Midcity Bank account, and makes Company's business decisions collectively.

The division grew out of the nineteenth-century public company. Investors supplied capital but were often numerous, geographically dispersed and without the time or skill to manage the business. Directors supplied the business expertise. Separation also allowed a change in share ownership without interrupting daily management, while limited liability left shareholders' exposure at the amount invested. Shareholders retained supervision through voting, appointment and removal, while management was delegated to the board. Section 40 of the International Business Companies Act 2000 follows that model by placing the business and affairs of a Bahamian IBC under a board, subject to contrary terms in its memorandum or articles.

The allocation is therefore one of ownership and supervision on one side, and control of Company's affairs on the other. The shareholders may alter the articles or replace the directors. Until they do so, the board exercises powers given to it and owes its duties to Company.

3.2

Article 19.1 confirms the traditional division. It gives Ms Nath and the trust officer the residual power to manage Company and to exercise every corporate power which the legislation or articles do not reserve to a general meeting. Matters such as altering the articles, appointing or removing directors and winding up remain with International Trust Company as member. Operation of the Midcity Bank account and investment or disposal of Company's worldwide assets are board matters unless another article says otherwise.

Article 19.1 follows the older Table A approach rather than the later form which allows members to direct directors by special resolution. Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon (1909) establishes that where management is conferred on directors in this form, a shareholder resolution cannot override the board on a matter entrusted to it. International Trust Company could alter the articles in the proper manner or remove the directors, but it cannot issue a binding ad hoc order requiring them to deal with Midcity Bank or another Company asset contrary to their judgment.

3.3

The Redhouse structure compresses ownership and management in practice, since International Trust Company holds Company's shares as trustee and employs the two people who sit on Company's board. The legal capacities must still be kept apart. Acting as trustee and registered member, International Trust Company exercises voting rights according to the Redhouse Trust and its fiduciary obligations as trustee. Acting as directors of Company, the trust officer and Ms Nath owe their fiduciary duty and duty of care to Company. They must decide whether each proposed act benefits Company and cannot defend a poor decision by saying that their employer or a trust beneficiary asked for it.

Company itself owns the worldwide assets. International Trust Company owns the shares as trustee; the Redhouse beneficiaries do not own the Midcity Bank account. A recommendation concerning the account may be placed before the board, but Article 19.1 leaves the decision with the directors. The trust company's power as sole member remains potent because it can replace the board by ordinary resolution, yet that prospect does not convert the directors into nominees.

The board should meet and take its higher-level decisions in The Bahamas, retain adequate minutes and control Company's accounts through authorised signatories. If the trustee or a beneficiary exercises management from elsewhere, Company may be treated as managed and controlled there for tax purposes. A narrow, documented authority for a particular act is safer than surrendering broad control by a general power of attorney.

3.4

International Trust Company may remove the trust officer before the end of the term by ordinary resolution under Article 15.3. It does not have to prove misconduct or other cause, although removal does not erase any contractual claim arising under a director's service agreement. Section 42 of the International Business Companies Act 2000 also recognises member control over removal, subject to the articles.

A general meeting route requires the notice and quorum prescribed by Articles 10-13. As International Trust Company is the sole registered member, it may instead sign a written resolution under Article 12.11. Office would also end without a removal vote if one of Article 17.1's events occurs, including written resignation, bankruptcy, unsoundness of mind or an unprotected interest in a Company contract. The rotation provisions in Article 16 apply at annual general meetings.

Article 15.4 allows International Trust Company, again by ordinary resolution, to appoint the replacement. The proposed director should first give written consent and confirm eligibility. The resolution should state the effective date, after which the register of directors must be amended and the Registrar notified. Ms Nath has no free-standing power under these articles to appoint a permanent replacement merely because a vacancy has arisen. The safer and direct route is a sole-member written ordinary resolution dealing with removal and replacement together.

The articles therefore preserve the orthodox legal division even in the Redhouse structure. International Trust Company controls the composition of the board, but management remains with the directors until a valid removal, replacement or constitutional change takes effect.

### SUBMIT THIS

A directorship carries risk because the articles give directors extensive control over property which belongs to the company. A director may borrow, issue shares, operate bank accounts, appoint agents and enter contracts, but each power has an internal boundary. Acting outside an article, using the wrong corporate procedure or signing alone where the board must act collectively can expose the director even when the company remains bound to an innocent outsider. Royal British Bank v Turquand (1856) illustrates the point: the lender could enforce borrowing despite the missing member approval, while the directors' internal breach was left intact.

The largest area of exposure is fiduciary duty. The director must act honestly for the company, use powers for their proper purpose and make an independent decision. Hutton v West Cork Railway Co (1883) treats corporate money as the company's money and permits expenditure only where it is reasonably incidental to the company's business. A director who diverts a corporate opportunity or asset for personal advantage may have to surrender the profit. Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver (1942) imposed that consequence where directors took for themselves an opportunity which should have belonged to the company. Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi (2005) gives the same warning where a director attempted to take a customer contract personally.

Loyalty creates a separate cluster of problems. A director should not transact with the company, obtain an unauthorised profit or accept a third-party benefit without full disclosure and informed approval. Aberdeen Railway v Blaikie Bros (1854) allowed an interested transaction to be set aside even though its terms might have been fair. The risk reaches loans to directors, Company guarantees for their liabilities, contracts with associated businesses, remuneration, gifts, bribes, confidential information, competing businesses and diverted opportunities. A director who has an interest should disclose it before approval, abstain from voting where the articles require and obtain approval from a disinterested quorum or the members. Concealment may lead to rescission, an account of profit or compensation.

Care and skill produces many claims without any dishonesty. The old subjective approach in Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co (1925) protected inexperienced directors who relied on a fraudulent chairman. Dorchester Finance Co Ltd v Stebbing (1989) and Norman v Theodore Goddard (1991) moved to an objective standard based on the conduct expected from an ordinarily competent person performing a director's functions. Bahamian section 55, Bermudian section 97 and Jersey article 74 express the test through the care of a reasonably prudent person in comparable circumstances. BVI section 122 asks what a reasonable director would do, with the nature of the company, decision and responsibilities taken into account. The modern test is more convincing than the leniency shown to the honourable amateurs in City Equitable because a fee-bearing office should carry a minimum level of competence.

Practical failures are often mundane. Signing a blank cheque allowed another director to misuse Company funds in Dorchester Finance. Completing an insurance proposal carelessly caused liability in Re D'Jan of London Ltd (1994). Bishopsgate Investment Management Ltd v Maxwell (No 2) (1994) imposed liability where a director signed away assets without asking what the documents did. Delegation is permissible, and directors may ordinarily rely on employees, lawyers or auditors who appear competent. The reliance must be honest and alert. Lexi Holdings plc v Luqman showed that poor accounts and a known history of deception can make passive reliance negligent; Madoff Securities International Ltd v Raven drew a line between directors who could reasonably rely on expertise and an inner circle who could not. The board must supervise, ask questions where warning signs appear and keep a record of the information on which it acted.

The offshore label does not reduce the standard. A service provider or employee described as a "nominee director" is a director in law. Following a beneficial owner's instruction is no defence unless the constitution permits the direction and the director has still complied with the duty owed to the company. Broad powers of attorney, uncontrolled bank access for a beneficial owner and unsigned or unexplained payment instructions transfer practical control while leaving the directors exposed. They also risk placing the company's management and control in another tax jurisdiction.

Insolvency sharpens the danger. Section 332 of the Companies Act 1948 model imposes fraudulent-trading liability where a person knowingly carries on business with intent to defraud creditors. The court may order that person to contribute to the company's assets. Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 introduced the wider wrongful-trading test in England where a director knew or ought to have known that insolvent liquidation could not be avoided. Offshore jurisdictions do not all adopt the wider remedy, so the local legislation must be checked. No indemnity provides a safe route for directors who continue incurring debts when they know creditors have no realistic prospect of payment.

Breach of fiduciary duty may allow the company to rescind an interested transaction, recover an undisclosed profit or obtain an indemnity for loss. Negligence leads to compensation. Foss v Harbottle makes the company the proper claimant, though a minority member may bring a derivative claim where controllers have committed fraud and prevent Company action. A liquidator will review the directors' conduct closely because any recovery enlarges the fund for creditors.

Exposure is reduced at the decision stage. Directors should read the legislation, memorandum, articles and transaction papers; obtain proper financial, legal and tax advice; disclose conflicts; insist on a quorate board; and record the material considered and the reason for the decision. They may obtain prior member approval or later ratification after full disclosure. Approval can cover an interested transaction or negligent management decision, but a majority cannot ratify its own dishonest appropriation of Company property to the prejudice of minority members or creditors.

Contractual protection comes next. In The Bahamas, BVI and Bermuda, the course materials recognise properly drawn exoneration and indemnity provisions for negligence, while fraud and dishonesty remain outside protection. English section 232 and the rules in several other jurisdictions take a narrower approach and may render such clauses void. A director's service agreement can define duties, information rights, fees, access to advisers and lawful indemnities. A company management agreement may make the beneficial owner indemnify the administrator, but that promise does not stop a third party suing and may not cover the director unless the drafting does so.

Directors' and officers' insurance can meet liability and defence costs for good-faith mistakes and negligent acts. It does not normally cover fraud, dishonesty or deliberate default. The director should check the insured parties, exclusions, limit, notification terms and the company's power to buy cover rather than assume that a policy held by the service provider protects every managed-company board.

The better protection is a documented, informed and independent decision made within the director's authority. Indemnities and insurance then deal with the remaining risk of an honest mistake. Fraud, concealed conflicts and insolvent trading remain personal hazards which paperwork cannot remove.

5.1

A members' voluntary winding-up is for a solvent company. The directors make a full inquiry and sign a declaration that all debts can be paid in full, supported by a statement of assets and liabilities. The members then pass the required resolution, appoint the liquidator and approve the liquidator's remuneration and powers. Notice is published and sent to the Registrar. The liquidator collects and realises assets, pays every creditor in full, distributes any surplus to members, prepares a final account and obtains dissolution after the final meeting or written approval.

A creditors' voluntary winding-up begins because the company cannot pay all creditors. The directors cannot honestly make the declaration of solvency. They convene a member meeting to pass the winding-up resolution and also call a meeting of creditors, presenting a statement of Company's financial position and a creditor list. The creditors have the stronger voice and select the liquidator by a majority of those present in person or by proxy. Progress and final meetings involve both members and creditors. The liquidator proves claims and distributes the available fund in the statutory order rather than returning a surplus to members.

Company must use the creditors' route. Blackacre is worth $1.7 million but secures $2.4 million, leaving Mortgagee with a $700,000 unsecured shortfall. The remaining assets total only $905,000. After the $40,000 liquidation costs and $55,000 preferential tax claim, $810,000 remains for ordinary unsecured debts of $1,620,000. Each ordinary creditor therefore receives 50 cents for each dollar owed.
