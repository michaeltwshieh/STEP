**1.1**

All business transacted at a general meeting other than the annual general meeting is special business (art.12.1), so the notice must give the general nature of that business as well as the place, day and hour (art.11.1). The resolutions are put as special resolutions, the articles defining a special resolution as one carried by a two-thirds super-majority.

---

**COMPANY LIMITED (Company [number])**

**NOTICE OF [EXTRAORDINARY] GENERAL MEETING TO CONSIDER SPECIFIC RESOLUTIONS**

To:

The members of the Company

The directors of the Company

[The auditors of the Company]

NOTICE IS HEREBY GIVEN THAT a general meeting of the Company will take place at [venue] on 28 August [year] at [time] for the purposes of considering and, if thought fit, passing the following resolutions:

NOTE:

(A) THAT the directors have proposed that the Company be wound up voluntarily.

(B) THAT the directors have declared that the Company is solvent and able to pay its debts in full.

TO RESOLVE AS SPECIAL RESOLUTIONS:

1. THAT the Company be wound up voluntarily.

2. THAT [name and address] and [name and address] (the 'Liquidators') be and are hereby appointed as liquidators of the Company to act jointly and severally for the purposes of such winding up.

3. THAT the Liquidators are authorised to charge for their professional services rendered during the course of the winding up of the Company at their published scale rate of charges.

4. THAT the Liquidators be and are hereby authorised to exercise all of the implied powers of a liquidator and that they be conferred with the power to divide any part of the assets of the Company as they shall think fit among the members of the Company in specie or in kind.

5. THAT the Liquidators' accounts of receipts and payments need not be audited.

DATE

SIGNED

[Company Secretary, by order of the board]

Note: A member of the Company who is entitled to attend and vote is also entitled to appoint a proxy, who need not be a member of the Company, to attend, speak and vote instead of him.

---

**1.2**

Article 27.5 answers the first question, and it answers it exhaustively. Notice of every general meeting goes to every member who has supplied the Company with an address (or registered facsimile number) for the giving of notices, to any person entitled to a share through the death or bankruptcy of a member, and to every director who is not also a shareholder. The article then shuts the door: no other person is entitled to receive notice.

On these facts that means C, D and E as the registered holders, and A and B as directors who hold no shares. A and B have no vote at a general meeting, but they are entitled to be told of it and to attend. Nothing in the facts suggests a deceased or bankrupt member, so limb (b) is idle here. The standard precedent notice is addressed to the auditors as well, and if auditors have been appointed under art.26 it is sensible practice to copy them in, but art.27.5 confers no right on them and their non-receipt could not vitiate the meeting.

As to method, art.27.1 gives three routes: personal service, post to the member's registered address, or email to the registered email address the member has supplied for the giving of notices. The choice matters, because art.27.2 attaches different deemed-service dates to each. A posted notice is deemed served on the fourth day after posting. An emailed notice is deemed served upon sending.

Email is therefore the route to prefer for any member who has supplied a registered email address, and it should be used here. It collapses the four-day lag to nothing and, since the winding-up is likely to be resisted by a 30 per cent minority, it produces a clean and datable record of service. For any member without a registered email address, service should be effected by courier or recorded delivery rather than ordinary post, so that despatch and delivery are both evidenced, even though the deemed-service rule in art.27.2 still governs the date.

Timing is where the real trap lies. Article 11.1 requires at least 14 clear days' notice in writing, exclusive both of the day on which notice is served or deemed served and of the day of the meeting. Working backwards from 28 August, the fourteen clear days run from 14 to 27 August inclusive. Service, or deemed service, must therefore be effected no later than 13 August. If the notice goes by email, send it on or before 13 August. If it goes by post, it must be posted on or before 9 August, because the fourth day after posting is 13 August. Personal service must likewise be completed by 13 August.

Short notice under art.11.2 is theoretically available, but it needs the consent of all the members entitled to receive notice. D and E are unlikely to give it. Do not build the timetable around it.

**1.3**

The starting position is a hard one. Unless the articles say otherwise, a failure to give proper notice to a person entitled to it means the meeting is not properly convened, and the business transacted at it is invalid. The resolution to wind up would be a nullity, the liquidators' appointment would rest on nothing, and the copy of the special resolution filed with the registrar would be worthless. A hostile minority holding 30 per cent has every incentive to take that point.

Article 11.3 softens this, but only so far. It provides that the accidental omission to give notice to, or the non-receipt of notice by, any member does not invalidate the proceedings. The saving is narrow in two respects. It reaches only accidents, and 'accidental' is a word that has never been easy to pin down; a decision to leave D and E off the circulation list, or a failure to serve them that suits C's arithmetic on quorum, would not be accidental on any view, and the meeting would be bad. It also speaks only of members. On its face it does not save an omission to notify A or B, who are entitled to notice under art.27.5(c) as directors who are not shareholders. It is arguable that a court would read the saving as covering them too, since the mischief is the same, but the point is untested on the wording and the safe course is simply to serve them.

Two further practical points. A member who turns up and takes part, in person or by proxy, will generally be treated as having had good notice, so a defect is cured by attendance. And a defect that is spotted before the meeting can be cured outright by obtaining short-notice consents under art.11.2, assuming the members will give them.

**1.4**

C cannot pass the resolution on 28 August. He can pass it a week later.

Article 12.2 fixes the quorum for a general meeting at two shareholders personally present. Company is not a sole-shareholder company, so the concession in the second half of that article does not apply. With only C in the room the meeting is inquorate, and no business may be transacted at all; anything purportedly resolved would be invalid.

What happens next is prescribed rather than discretionary. Article 12.3 provides that if a quorum is not present within half an hour of the appointed time, a meeting convened on the requisition of members is dissolved, but in any other case it stands adjourned to the same day in the next week, at the same time and place. This meeting is being convened by the directors, not on a members' requisition, so it is the adjournment limb that bites. The meeting stands adjourned to 4 September, same time, same venue. No fresh notice is required: art.12.5 calls for notice of an adjourned meeting only where the adjournment is for 10 days or more, and this one is for seven.

The sting is in the tail of art.12.3. At the adjourned meeting, if a quorum is again not present within half an hour of the appointed time, the members present shall be a quorum. So if C alone attends on 4 September, waits out the half hour, and D and E stay away, C by himself constitutes a quorum and can proceed to business.

Can he then carry the resolution? Yes. The applicable legislation calls for a three-quarters majority or such lesser super-majority as the articles define, and Article 1 of these articles defines a special resolution as one passed by a super-majority of two-thirds of the votes of shareholders entitled to vote. Two-thirds is the operative threshold. C holds 70 per cent of the issued capital. On a poll, which he may demand under art.12.6 as a single member holding not less than 15 per cent of the paid-up capital, he casts 70 of the 100 votes, which clears two-thirds even on the strict reading of Article 1 that measures the majority against the whole of the votes of shareholders entitled to vote rather than merely the votes cast. On a show of hands he would have his one vote under art.13.1 and no one to oppose him, so the motion would carry that way too, but a poll is the prudent course because it puts the arithmetic on the face of the minute and forecloses any later argument that a lone member's single hand cannot make a two-thirds super-majority.

The written resolution route offers no shortcut. Article 12.11 requires a resolution in writing to be signed by all of the shareholders entitled to receive notice of a general meeting and vote at it. D and E would each have to sign. If they would sign, there would be no dispute in the first place.

Two riders. The half hour must actually be allowed to run, both on 28 August and on 4 September. And if this is to be a members' voluntary winding-up, the directors must first have made a declaration of solvency; if they cannot, the process becomes a creditors' voluntary winding-up and the creditors, not C, choose the liquidator.

**1.5**

On a show of hands the motion is lost, and lost decisively on the face of it. Article 13.1 gives every member present in person a single vote on a show of hands, without regard to the size of his holding. C raises one hand in favour. D and E raise one each against. The vote is two to one against, and if the chairman declared the result there and then his declaration would, under art.12.7, be conclusive evidence of the fact without proof of the numbers behind it.

That is exactly why C must not let the matter rest on the show of hands.

Article 12.6 allows a poll to be demanded, before or on the declaration of the result of the show of hands, by at least three members present, or by one member (or two together) holding not less than 15 per cent of the paid-up capital of the Company. C holds 70 per cent. He is squarely within the second limb and can demand a poll on his own. The demand must be made before or at the moment of the declaration; leave it any later and art.12.7 locks in the chairman's declaration.

On the poll the arithmetic reverses completely. Article 13.1 gives every member one vote for each share he holds, so on a holding of 100 shares the votes are 70 for and 30 against. The threshold, as explained above, is the two-thirds super-majority that Article 1 prescribes for a special resolution, the articles having taken up the invitation in the legislation to define a lesser super-majority than three-quarters. Seventy per cent clears two-thirds. Under art.12.8 the result of the poll is deemed to be the resolution of the meeting, and the resolution to wind up is carried.

The narrowness of that margin deserves a word. Had the articles been silent, or had they simply mirrored the statutory default, C's 70 per cent would have fallen short of three-quarters and D and E, between them holding 30 per cent, would have held a blocking minority. C's ability to liquidate this company over their objection rests entirely on the definition in Article 1. It is a reminder that in a company with a substantial minority the definition of 'special resolution' in the articles is not boilerplate; it is the whole of the minority's protection, or the absence of it.

Two things C should do beyond demanding the poll. He should secure the chair, or at least ensure that the election of the chairman is itself taken on a poll: art.12.4 has the members elect a chairman by majority vote, which on a show of hands D and E would win two to one, whereas art.12.10 requires a poll demanded on the election of a chairman to be taken forthwith, and on a poll C's 70 per cent prevails. The chairman controls the declaration of the result and holds a casting vote under art.12.9, and C should not hand that to the opposing side. He should also see that the poll and the votes cast are recorded accurately in the minutes, that a certified copy of the special resolution is filed with the registrar, and that notice of the winding-up is published in the press and the Gazette once the liquidators are appointed.

The short answer to the question, then, is that the show of hands defeats the motion, that C can and should demand a poll, and that on the poll the company goes into voluntary liquidation.
