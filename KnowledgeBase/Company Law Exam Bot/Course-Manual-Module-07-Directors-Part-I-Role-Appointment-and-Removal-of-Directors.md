## Module 7: Directors Part I - Role, Appointment and Removal of Directors

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- carry out an initial review of corporate governance under the traditional onshore corporate model
- illustrate how the traditional Anglo-American corporate model has been used as a template by legislators and service providers offshore
- examine the provisions conferring powers of management upon the directors
- analyse the different methods of appointing the initial directors during the incorporation process
- review typical provisions dealing with the removal of directors and the appointment of subsequent directors.

## Introduction

This is the first of two modules about directors. It begins with an analysis of corporate governance generally and the shortfalls of the traditional company structure (section 1). It then moves on, in section 2, to review the division of power between the shareholders in general meeting and the board of directors, and how this is largely determined by the articles (which may, of course, be altered by resolution of the shareholders) rather than prescribed by statute. Section 3 deals with typical rules relating to the appointment of directors, followed by rules relating to their vacation of office, removal and disqualification in section 4. Module 8 continues with an in-depth analysis of the powers conferred upon, and duties owed by, directors.

## 1. The traditional corporate model and corporate governance

The structure of a traditional company was developed as an efficient means of conducting business. It resembles a structured partnership between entrepreneurs and capitalists. The capitalists invest capital in the company, becoming shareholders, and the company delegates the management of its business to the entrepreneurs, who together comprise the board of directors.

A large, public, widely held company usually has many shareholders, and its shares may be listed on a stock exchange so they can be traded conveniently. The shareholders do not involve themselves in the day-to-day business of the company. Business is typically conducted by executives who are full-time officers and who may own none, or only a small percentage, of the shares of the company. There is, therefore, a distinct separation between ownership of the company, on the one hand, and the power to run the company, on the other.

The provisions that regulate the relationship of the shareholders and directors, the formalities that must be observed and provisions defining the distribution of power between the two groups are often complicated and cumbersome, particularly in relation to a private, closely held company. Such companies usually have their shareholding concentrated in the hands of one or two shareholders. The shares are typically subject to pre-emption rights and are not freely transferable. The shareholders not only own the company, they also run it, insofar as they are also its directors. There is therefore a blurring of the boundary between ownership and control. Ownership and control of a private company is often vested in one and the same person.

The traditional corporate model is nevertheless based upon the separation of ownership from the power to control the business of the company. This gives rise to two important questions.

- i. How is the power to control and manage the company supervised?
- ii. How is a company governed?

In brief, the promoters initially set up the company and establish its constitution. Thereafter it is the shareholders who, by passing resolutions in general meetings of the company, may alter the memorandum and articles, and thereby re-allocate responsibility for running the company. The shareholders appoint the directors and exercise a supervisory function over their performance. Shareholders have power to remove the directors if their performance is unsatisfactory. The shareholders may vote at general meetings to pass appropriate resolutions to implement major decisions affecting the company, such as:

- an amendment of its constitution
- the appointment and removal of directors
- in many cases, the approval of accounts, appointment of auditors and declaration of the dividend
- the voluntary winding-up of the company.

Beyond these key matters, however, shareholders usually delegate the power to manage the business of the company to the directors.

The board of directors, acting as a unit, typically controls the company, directs company affairs, and makes and implements key strategic decisions affecting the business, such as the organisational structure of the workforce, and employment of its capital, machinery and inventory, and the development and marketing of its products and services. There are a number of logical reasons for allowing the directors to control the business.

- Expertise - shareholders, being numerous, and often without specialist knowledge, are unlikely to be equipped to manage the business. Corporate executives are therefore chosen because they are experts in their field, and well qualified to run the business.
- Capital - the separation of ownership and control enables entrepreneurs to obtain the required capital for their enterprises by raising funds from the public.
- Continuity - a shareholder's investment, although open-ended in duration, is nevertheless considered to be liquid in so far as shares in publicly listed companies can easily be sold. A shareholder who wishes to influence the

business of a company will generally do so by voting to shape the board, but not take a more active role than that. A shareholder who does not like the way the company is being run may seek to remove a director and restructure the board, but will in practice be far more likely to sell the shares. If the shares of the company are sold, it is important that the change of membership should not disrupt the management of the company.

- Liability - the shareholders enjoy limited liability for payment of the debts incurred by the company and, therefore, although they may lose their initial investment if the company fails, there is no risk that they will incur any further, open-ended liability caused, for example, by incompetent management.

The directors, in exercising their powers of control and management, owe an equitable fiduciary duty and common law duty of care to the company. It is sometimes said that the directors are accountable to the company. Basically, this means that the company is effectively run for the benefit of the shareholders. The directors must please the shareholders, otherwise they may be removed from office. The traditional capitalist shareholder is an investor who is predominantly interested in profits. All other interests within the traditional corporate structure are subordinate.

This model, involving as it does a separation of ownership from control, was devised for clear economic reasons in the 19th century. 39  Since then, the needs of commerce and the interests of others in society have developed and grown in sophistication. It is now widely recognised 40  that the legal principles and rules determining corporate governance have been left behind by developments in the real world of economics and market forces. The legal structure of a company is still based on the notion that the only two participants of merit in the company are the (ordinary) shareholders (of common stock) and the directors. The latter are accountable to and owe their duties to the former. In reality, other persons and entities have an interest in the proper government of a company, but their interests are not represented by the traditional corporate model. A dynamic tension exists within a company involving some or all of the following participants, only two of which ((1) and (2) in the diagram below) have significant rights and duties under a traditional corporate structure. This is why the rules of corporate governance were modified in England in 2006 by the introduction of s.171 of the Companies Act 2006, which we shall return to at the end of this section.

## 1.1 Preference shareholders (holders of preferred stock)

The right to vote at general meetings of a company (and thus the ability to appoint, monitor and supervise the performance of the directors) is usually conferred only upon ordinary shareholders, i.e. the holders of common stock. This is justified on the grounds that the return enjoyed by the ordinary/common shareholders is linked to the success of the company, since they receive their share of the profits (and return of capital upon winding-up) only after all other shareholders, creditors and claims have been satisfied. Preference non-voting shareholders are a body of equity owners of a company who have no voice or vote to affect the course of the company. In the US and the EU this has led to changes in the law relating to the board structure, non-executive directors and the role of auditors.

## 1.2 Bondholders, long-term and short-term creditors

Creditors, in this context, include investors who have lent money to a company in exchange for corporate bonds, notes or other securities that carry the right to receive a fixed rate of interest and for the principal to be repaid upon maturity. They are referred to as bondholders and, for short-term lending, holders of commercial paper. Creditors also include long-term institutional lenders and banks that lend to a company via short-term loan or overdraft. Trade creditors who supply goods and/or services to a company on credit are excluded from this category of creditor.

Bondholders and other creditors have a significant interest in the well-being of the company but have no right to vote at its meetings. In theory they cannot, therefore, influence corporate decision making to ensure that the company is being governed competently. The justification for this is that creditors are not equity owners of the company. They, therefore, assume little risk, as the return to lenders/bondholders must be paid to them before the company calculates its net profits for distribution to shareholders. Accordingly, creditors have no rights to influence the affairs of a debtor company. Now, however, onshore corporate governance is changing. Institutional lenders, such as banks, pension funds and investment companies, monitor the financial position of their debtor companies very carefully. Banks, in particular, can intervene or influence business decision making under threat of calling in the loan (if it is repayable on demand). In the event of poor performance, default or business collapse, an institutional lender often retains the right to appoint specialist consultants to investigate the financial affairs of the company or, as a last resort, appoint a receiver to take over from the board and run the company, not on behalf of the shareholders but on behalf of the secured lender.

## 1.3 The workforce

There are very few ways in which the employees of a company can influence management, take part in the affairs of the company or ensure that it is being governed correctly. They cannot vote at shareholders' meetings (unless they also own shares). The directors do not owe the workforce specific duties, nor do they consult them (unless they are represented on the board) in relation to business matters. The company workforce is an integral part of the economic well-being of the company and yet its interests are largely ignored by the onshore corporate model structure.

## 1.4 Executives

Corporate decisions are made by the board of directors as a whole. In a large company, especially, however, there are inevitably an infinite number of decisions made each working day about numerous matters such as marketing, production, organising staff and assigning work. These day-to-day decisions are not made inside the boardroom. They are often made by the company's team of executives. Senior executives may also be directors, but not necessarily as they often operate at a level below the formal structure of the board. Only the chief executive officer (CEO) may have a place on the board. The role of the CEO is to implement the strategic long-term policy decisions of the board: to coordinate, manage and control the company on a practical, hands-on, day-to-day basis. The position of non-director executives within the company is contractual in nature - they are often employed on a fixed-term contract. Their legal standing flows from the fact that the board of directors may delegate its powers to manage the company to a team of managers and vice presidents. If executives under-perform or are regarded as incompetent, they can be fired. They exist, however, on the edge, if not outside, the traditional corporate model. They owe contractual duties to the company . Yet the highest level of management, the directors, owe their duties solely to the shareholders as a body, to the exclusion of the company's workforce and executive management team.

## 1.5 Others

Persons (and external factors), other than the shareholders and directors, may influence corporate decision making in a modern context, to ensure proper governance. These include auditors, regulators and the company's customers. Public companies incorporated onshore must have their annual accounts audited. The auditors are thus able to monitor and exert influence over corporate policy, particularly in relation to the financial organisation of the company. The management of a business is also subject to considerable regulation, particularly onshore, where significant mandatory and public regulation and self-regulation exists alongside the companies legislation. 41 Finally, the company's customers ought not to be ignored in the established structure: the persons who transact business with the company and buy goods and/or services from it. The laws of supply and demand determine that there is little point in producing products unless there is a demand for them at a price that provides a satisfactory return. Customers are therefore a major influence in laissez-faire commerce. They do not, however, exercise any direct influence upon the organisation and proper operation of a company.

## 1.6 Summary of corporate governance

Notwithstanding the notional rights and interests of the persons and other factors identified above, the traditional corporate model that developed in 19th-century England recognises just two organs of corporate governance, namely:

- the shareholders, who supply the share capital to the enterprise and act by taking part in the company's general meetings

- the board of directors, who are appointed by the company - i.e., by the shareholders in general meeting - and are empowered to run the business as a committee for the ultimate benefit of the shareholders.

The law relating to corporate governance has been modernised in the US and UK. For instance, in England, see s.171 of the current Companies Act 2006. 42  But this type of duty is not imposed upon directors of offshore companies. Legislators in offshore jurisdictions have used the traditional corporate model as a template when drafting and enacting their companies legislation, as have professionals engaged in offshore company management when drafting and registering articles of association for companies incorporated in those jurisdictions. The modern corporate model may reflect the needs and requirements of a large, public, widely held, operating company. But for a private, closely held, asset-holding company that follows the traditional model, there are only two (or three) main participants that matter, namely the shareholders and board of directors (as well as the regulator).

## 2. The division of power between directors and shareholders

The division of power between the board of directors and the shareholders is essentially governed by the articles of the company rather than prescribed by legislation.

The articles are originally drafted by (or on the instructions of) the promoter/ subscriber(s) who invariably become(s) the shareholder(s). They may be changed by a resolution of the shareholders in general meeting. Hence the division of power is effectively controlled by the shareholders.

Having said that, it is standard practice to provide that the business of a company shall be conducted by the directors, who may (subject to any articles or legislation to the contrary) exercise all the powers of the company. This type of provision is found:

- in applicable permissive companies legislation, or
- in model articles that may be adopted by a company from a schedule to, or regulations made under, the companies legislation, or
- typically, in an express, tailor-made article of a company incorporated for offshore asset-holding purposes.

## 2.1 Companies legislation

Companies legislation anticipates that the power to manage the business of a company will be conferred upon the board of directors, although this is subject to any provisions in the articles to the contrary.

Section 109 of the BVI BCA 2004 states:

'The business and affairs of a company shall be managed by, or under the direction or supervision of, the directors of the company.'

(a) The likely consequences of any decision in the long term

(b) The interests of the company's employees

(c) The need to foster the company's business relationships with suppliers, customers and others

(d) The impact of the company's operations on the community and the environment

(e) The desirability of the company maintaining a reputation for high standards of business and

(f ) The need to act fairly as between members of the company' .

Section 91 of the Manx Companies Act 2006 and s.134 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008 are identical to the BVI legislation.

Section 40 IBCA 2000 of the Bahamas is typical of the IBC legislation, which states:

'Subject to the limitations or provisions to the contrary in its memorandum or articles, the business and affairs of a company incorporated under this Act shall be managed by a board of directors that consists of one or more persons who may be individuals or companies.' 43

## 2.2 Model articles

Model articles set out as a schedule to, or regulations made under, the companies legislation of a number of offshore jurisdictions - including the Cayman Islands, 44 the Bahamas, 45  Hong Kong, 46  and Singapore 47  - contain a specimen article based upon art.80 of the model articles found in Table A of the UK's Companies Act 1948. The latter provides that:

the business of the company shall be managed by the directors who may... exercise all such powers of the company as are not by the [companies legislation] or by these articles, required to be exercised by the company in general meeting.

The specimen articles reproduced in Appendix 1B of the online Study Resources contain a similar provision at para.19.1. It essentially says that the directors have a general residual power to manage the business of the company on a day-to-day basis and exercise the various powers of the company unless either:

- a. tthe companies legislation specifically confers a particular power upon the shareholders in general meeting; or
- b. the company's articles contain a contrary provision.

The interpretation of this particular article has been the subject of much debate and litigation in England. It has been held that, where art.80 (or an equivalent provision) has been adopted by a company in its articles, the power to manage the company vests in the directors. The directors cannot be made to obey a resolution of the shareholders if it is contrary to their own decision. In other words, the shareholders cannot make the directors do something they do not want to do (except by formally changing the articles).

In Quin &amp; Axtens Ltd v Salmon , 48  a company appointed five directors to its board (Directors A-E). The company adopted articles that contained a provision similar to art.80 above, but which went on to state that any resolution of the directors to acquire property for the company was not valid if either one of Directors A or B dissented. The directors passed a resolution by four votes to one to acquire certain property; however, A was the dissenting director. Accordingly, the shareholders convened a general meeting of the company, and passed a resolution instructing the board to acquire the property and authorised the directors to proceed with the transaction. The transaction was challenged and the judge observed that the general power to run a company should be exercised by the directors, and other identified powers may be exercised by the shareholders in general meeting, but when a power is expressly conferred upon the directors in the articles, then only the directors may exercise that power. The shareholders could control the directors in the exercise of their powers only by altering the articles. It was held that the shareholders cannot usurp the powers given to the directors by the articles by, for example, giving them binding instructions.

As a result of this interpretation of art.80, Table A of the Companies Act 1948 (or, rather, its predecessor, which was in similar terms) was replaced in the UK by a new model article found in art.70 of a new Table A in the Companies Act 1985, 49  which provides:

'Subject to the provisions of the [Companies] Act, the memorandum and the articles, and to any directions given by special resolution , the business of the company shall be managed by the directors who may exercise all the powers of the company.'

Article 64 of the model articles published in Jersey in the Companies (Standard Table) (Jersey) Order 1992 contains an identical provision. 50

This new, alternative article, therefore, authorises shareholders (in particular, those who control a large enough percentage of votes to pass a special resolution) to give instructions to the board and thereby manage the company through the directors. The shareholders may, by special resolution under this article, give the directors binding directions on an ad hoc basis: for instance, to enter into or refrain from entering into a particular deal, contract or transaction.

The current model articles in England are set out in the Companies (Model Articles) Regulations 2008, and have been reproduced in Appendix 1C of the online Study Resources. Articles 3 and 4 essentially replicate art.70 of Table A of the  Companies Act 1985, but in plainer style.

An example of the exercise by the shareholders of their indirect powers of management conferred by articles similar to the alternative art.19.1 of Appendix 1B, or art.4.1 of Appendix 1C, is illustrated in Appendix 21G of the online Study Resources.

Company management service providers must therefore analyse the particular article that confers a general power of management on the directors in order to ascertain whether it is absolute or can be compromised by directions given by a special resolution of the shareholders. The issue may be regarded as academic if the shareholders and directors are one and the same person, or are controlled by one and the same person. But the issue of beneficial owner control is of the utmost importance to beneficial owners who have appointed independent directors to the boards of their managed companies. Which article would you prefer if you were the beneficial owner? No doubt the modern one which gives beneficial owners, through their nominee shareholders, a right to instruct the board to act, or refrain from acting, in a particular manner. The standard article based upon art.80 of the 1948 Act referred to above, giving the directors all the powers to manage the company that are not required to be exercised by the company in general meeting, begs the question: precisely which decisions must be made by the shareholders at general meetings of the company?

The answer depends upon the provisions of the articles concerned and applicable companies legislation.

As a general rule (which does not always apply to IBCs, BVI BCs and other special purpose vehicles), the following matters may be resolved only by the shareholders in general meeting, rather than by the directors:

- matters affecting the constitution of the company, such as an alteration to the memorandum (name, domicile address, objects or capital clause) or articles
- matters affecting the company's capital, such as a reduction of capital, a redemption or buy-back of its shares out of capital, a variation of the rights conferred upon holders of a particular class of shares, reconstructions and company arrangements
- matters relating to the removal and appointment of directors
- in many cases, the approval of accounts, appointment of auditors and the declaration of dividends
- matters relating to the voluntary winding-up of the company.

## 3. Appointment of directors

## 3.1 Eligibility

Usually, any person who has the legal and mental capacity to own property may be appointed as a director of a company. Children, persons of unsound mind, undischarged bankrupts and those who have been disqualified 51  from acting are not, generally, eligible for appointment.

The residence of a person may have a bearing on whether he or she can be appointed as a director. Some jurisdictions, such as Bermuda and Singapore, 52  require at least one of the directors to reside in the local centre.

According to model Table A articles in the 1948 Act, the directors are required to hold a minimum number of shares in the company. Failure to comply can mean that the director has to vacate office. A director's shareholding qualification is, however, not usual in offshore companies. 53

Corporate directors are permitted in most offshore jurisdictions provided the corporate director is licensed by the local regulator to conduct 'company management business' . 54

In order to ensure minimum standards of competence and integrity, notwithstanding concerns about cost and 'level playing fields' , a number of international initiatives around the new millennium recommended that offshore financial centres introduce a licensing requirement for firms that carry out company management business. 55 The wide definition of 'company management business' includes not only the administration of asset-holding companies, but also the provision of directorships. These recommendations led to the introduction of a wide-ranging licence requirement for firms that provide directorships as well as incorporations and corporate administration. Following the recommendations in the Edwards Report, the Crown Dependencies introduced a new regulatory environment for both trustees and company management service providers.

- Guernsey enacted the Fiduciary and Administration Business Law 2000, amended 2003, requiring trust and corporate services providers to be licensed.
- Jersey enacted the Financial Services (Jersey) Law 1998 and the Financial Services (Extension) (Jersey) Law 2000, requiring trust and corporate service providers to be licensed.
- The Isle of Man enacted the Corporate Service Providers Act 2000, requiring corporate service providers to be licensed, with similar regulation for trust services, the Fiduciary Services Act 2005.

As a result of the recommendations in the KPMG Report, the following legislation has been passed by the British Overseas Territories.

- The Bahamas enacted the Financial and Corporate Service Providers Act 2000, which regulates anyone engaged in the provision of financial or corporate services for profit. The Banks and Trust Companies Regulation Act 2000 relates to the provision of banking and trust services.
- In the BVI the licensing and supervision of company management business is regulated by the Company Management Act 1990, amended 2004. Fiduciaries are regulated by the Banks and Trust Companies Act 1990.

- The Cayman Islands enacted the Companies Management Law 1999, now 2018 Revision, and enlarged the powers of the regulator, the Cayman Islands Monetary Authority (CIMA).
- Bermuda enacted the Trusts (Regulation of Trust Business) Act 2001, amended 2005, the Corporate Service Providers Business Act 2012, and strengthened the supervisory role of its regulator, the Bermuda Monetary Authority.
- In the Turks and Caicos Islands a professional trustee must be licensed under the Trustee Licensing Ordinance, and company management service providers similarly under the Company Management (Licensing) Ordinance 1999 as amended. In addition, a Financial Services Commission was established to regulate the provision of financial services, including company management services.

Other offshore jurisdictions not covered by either the Edwards or the KPMG Reports have passed similar regulation.

- In Gibraltar, the Financial Services Ordinance established a commission, giving licensing and supervisory powers for the control of financial services, including trust and company management services.
- In Mauritius, trustees and corporate service providers must be licensed by the Financial Services Commission under the Financial Services Development Act 2001, as amended.
- In Singapore, trustees must be licensed by the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) under the Trust Companies Act, 2005, and corporate services must be licensed as 'filing agents' under the Accounting and Corporate Regulatory Authority (Amendment) Act 2015.
- In Hong Kong, legislation came later, but in accordance with the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance with effect from 2018, a person or firm that carries out trust or company services business in or from Hong Kong is required to hold a licence from the registrar of companies.

## 3.2 Number of directors

Companies legislation usually prescribes that a company must have a minimum number of directors. For instance, in the UK the minimum used to be two but is now one, in line with EU practice. Most, if not all, offshore jurisdictions allow companies incorporated therein to have a sole director. 56

While there is a minimum number prescribed by statute, the actual number of directors required for any particular company, together with the quorum required to transact business, is set out in the articles. A traditional Table A 1948 Act article allows the number to be increased or reduced, but not below the statutory minimum, by the shareholders passing an ordinary resolution in general meeting - see art.15.2 of Appendix 1B. It has not been thought necessary to reproduce art.15.2 in the modern articles reproduced in Appendix 1C.

## 3.3 Method of appointing the directors

## 3.3.1 Initial director(s)

The first directors are those persons who consent to act as directors upon incorporation and appointed as part of the incorporation process. The actual mechanics of the appointment of the initial board varies between jurisdictions, but most companies legislation states that the subscribers to the memorandum must appoint the first directors. For example, the IBC legislation such as that enacted in the Bahamas (s.42(1) of the IBCA 2000) states:

'The first directors of a company incorporated under this Act shall be elected by the subscribers to the memorandum ... .'

In these circumstances, an organisational meeting of the subscribers may be held to nominate candidates, discuss and vote on their appointment to the board and record the resolution as a minute of a meeting. Alternatively, as the subscribers are service providers, it is much more likely that a formal meeting is dispensed with and the directors are appointed by a written resolution.

In jurisdictions where the companies legislation says nothing about the method of appointing the first directors, it is usual for the articles to set out the method of appointment. For example, the Companies Law 2022 Revision of the Cayman Islands is silent about the method of appointing the first directors; however, art.63 of Table A (and para.15.1 of Appendix 1B) provides that:

'The number of the directors and the names of the first directors shall be determined in writing by a majority of the subscribers of the memorandum.'

Accordingly, in the same manner as for an IBC, the subscribers must hold an organisational meeting, or pass a written resolution, to appoint the first directors.

For BVI BCs, the registered agent must appoint the first director(s) within six months of incorporation. Alternatively, a more roundabout statutory procedure may have to be followed. By way of example, ss.69 and 70 Companies Act, 1981 of Bermuda provide that:

1. The persons whose names are subscribed to the memorandum shall be the provisional directors of the company ...

Having been appointed as a corporate director, some firms prefer to record detailed terms and conditions in a directors' service agreement (DSA). A specimen DSA is illustrated in Appendix 21C of the online Study Resources. Finally, in most jurisdictions, the company must file the register of directors, or particulars of the board members, with the registrar, usually within a specified number of days of incorporation. 57

Whatever precise method is followed to appoint the initial director(s) of a company, typical requirements are:

- a. to obtain the prior written agreement of the director(s) to act, by a simple document similar to Appendix 21A of the online Study Resources;
- b. to pass a written resolution of the subscriber(s), similar to the resolution illustrated in Appendix 21B of the online Study Resources;
- c. the company may enter into a directors' service agreement (DSA) with the newly appointed director to record the relevant terms of their relationship, including exonerations and indemnities - see the example in Appendix 21C;
- d. for the corporate services provider engaged in the incorporation to send notice to the registrar of companies giving the director(s) name(s) and other prescribed particulars; there may be a prescribed form of notice for this purpose; and
- e. for the names of the directors to be entered in the company's register of directors.

## 3.3.2  Subsequent directors

Typical legislation states that subsequent directors, i.e., those other than the first directors appointed upon incorporation, may be appointed by ordinary resolution of the members in general meeting or, where permitted by the articles, by the directors.

This creates a default position that, when the articles are silent, the power to appoint subsequent directors is conferred upon, and therefore requires a resolution of, the shareholders. This default is, however, subject to any other provision contained in the articles. A power to appoint new or replacement directors will, almost always, be dealt with in the articles. Generally, the rules on appointment of the directors are dealt with in a company's articles.

Unfortunately, here lies a dichotomy. On the one hand, old-fashioned articles (i.e., those based upon the 1948 or 1985 Table A, such as those illustrated in Appendix 1B) assume that a company will hold an AGM of shareholders following the retirement of board members by rotation. On the other hand, more modern articles (such as those illustrated in Appendix 1C) permit the appointment of new or replacement directors by resolution of existing board members; this avoids having to convene and hold a general meeting of shareholders of a company. Let us examine these different approaches in a little detail.

a. Traditional articles : Appendix 1B (references to articles in this subsection are to Appendix 1B)
  - Articles 16.1 and 16.2 require one third of the directors to retire each year, but, if their number is not three or a multiple of three, then the number nearest one third should retire from the board each year, starting with those who have been longest in office; if they so wish, the directors who have retired may offer themselves for re-election at the AGM.
  - Article 16.4 provides that in the event of an unexpected vacancy on the board, for example if a director dies, retires or suffers an unexpected disability, the remaining directors can appoint a new board member. But such new director only serves until the next AGM, when a permanent board member can be appointed by the shareholders.
  - Article 15.4 confers a power to appoint new or replacement directors upon shareholders in general meeting. This power would typically be exercised at the company's AGM, convened and held in accordance with arts 10.1 and 10.2.

An example of minutes of a general meeting of a company at which a single resolution is passed to appoint a new director is illustrated in Appendix 21D of the online Study Resources.

In conclusion, the directors of a typical private company are, therefore, appointed by either an ordinary resolution of the shareholders or by board resolution. This means that a shareholder controlling 50% plus one of the voting shares can control the composition of the entire board. England and the bulk of the common law jurisdictions have retained what is known as a statutory straight voting system, rather than adopting the US concept of cumulative voting, which ensures that the minority shareholders are guaranteed at least one seat on the board. Straight and cumulative voting are easier to explain by way of an example.

Assume that a company has issued 100 shares: X owns 51, Y owns 49. Three directors must be appointed. Typically, in England, there is a straight vote for each appointment. Therefore, for Director 1, if X votes for Candidate A, and Y votes for Candidate B, X wins and A is appointed. For Director 2, if X votes for Candidate C, and Y votes for Candidate D, X wins again and C is appointed. Ditto for Candidate 3; X will always outvote Y, so none of Y's choices of candidate may ever be elected to the board.

Cumulative voting protects the interests of a minority shareholder. In the straight voting procedure, X had 3 x 51 votes, i.e. 153 votes, and Y had 49 x 3 = 147 votes. Under cumulative voting a shareholder is allowed to accumulate their votes, so Y can decide how to divide these 147 votes. Also, instead of each appointment being voted on individually, all candidates run together and the three candidates with the highest number of votes are elected. Accordingly, to guarantee at least one director on the board, Y can vote all 147 votes for one candidate. So, for instance, the ballot consists of all candidates, A, B, C, D, E and F to fill three vacancies. The result could be:

- Candidate A : 148 votes cast by X - X wins
- Candidate B : 147 votes cast by Y - Y wins
- Candidate C : five remaining votes cast by X - X wins
- Candidates D, E, F : no votes - not appointed.

## 4. Resignation, removal and disqualification of directors

A director cannot hold office for ever. Statutory provisions and express articles allow a director to:

- resign or retire , by rotation or otherwise
- vacate office (with cause)
- be removed (with or without cause)
- be disqualified from holding office.

For example, s.113(6) of the BVI BCA 2004 states:

'A director holds office until his successor takes office, or until his earlier death, resignation or removal.'

As with many aspects of corporate governance, the source of the regulations that apply in relation to the vacation of office by a director, by resignation, removal or disqualification, can be found in two places. Companies legislation usually provides rules of general application that are subject to any contrary provision in the articles of a particular company. Detailed provisions may then be found in the company's articles.

## 4.1 Resignation

The articles logically permit a director to resign voluntarily. The typical procedure is that a director's resignation must be in writing and served upon the board at the registered office of the company, subject to reasonable or fixed notice. The procedure is set out in art.17.3 of Appendix 1B and art.18(e) of Appendix 1C, a sample letter of resignation is illustrated in Appendix 21F, and a shareholder's resolution accepting such resignation is illustrated at Resolution 1 of Appendix 21D and a similar resolution of the board is illustrated at Resolution 2 of Appendix 21E of the Online Study Resources.

## 4.2 Vacating office

The articles of a company may also contain more detailed provision to the effect that a director shall cease to hold office on giving cause, for instance, if the director:

- i. becomes prohibited from being a director by a disqualification order made by a court of law under applicable companies legislation; 59

- ii. is adjudicated bankrupt (or makes a formal arrangement with creditors on the basis of insolvency and inability to pay all personal debts in full);
- iii. becomes of unsound mind or mentally incapable of performing the functions of a director
- iv. shall, for more than, say, six months, have been absent from board meetings held within that period, without the consent of the other directors;
- v. is convicted of an indictable offence (and/or is imprisoned); or
- vi. receives a request in writing from the other directors, being not fewer than, say, three in number.

This type of provision is illustrated in art.17.2 of Appendix 1B, and art.18 of Appendix 1C. In some jurisdictions, these provisions are referred to as disqualifying conditions. It may be preferable to describe them as conditions that provide for the mandatory vacation of office, in order to distinguish them from disqualification orders made by a court of law in the circumstances set out in section 4.4 of this module.

## 4.3 Removal

A director may usually be removed from the board by resolution of the shareholders, who are not obliged to give reasons or show any just cause. The shareholders can simply vote a director out of office.

The current UK statutory provisions 60  provide that the company in general meeting may, by ordinary resolution, remove a director from the board. This provision did not exist in English law until s.184 of the Companies Act 1948. Until 1948, any power to remove a director without cause (or justification) had to be contained in the articles, otherwise it did not exist. Accordingly, it became standard practice to include express provision in the articles, similar to art.17.1 of Appendix 1B:

- 'i. The company may by ordinary [or special] resolution remove any director before the expiration of his period of office notwithstanding anything in these articles or in any agreement between the company and such director.
- ii. The company may by ordinary resolution appoint another person in place of the director removed from office under the preceding article ... .'

As with the statutory provisions for the appointment of directors, some jurisdictions have included a statutory provision similar to that contained in the 1948 Act, allowing the company, by its shareholders in general meeting, to remove directors without cause. Other jurisdictions have elected not to follow the 1948 Act in this regard, and therefore leave it to the articles to provide for the removal of directors without cause, if it is considered appropriate. It is not unusual for the articles of offshore, one-person, asset-holding companies such as IBCs and BVI BCs to state that directors can be removed and new ones appointed by resolution of shareholders or by a resolution of directors.

## 4.4 Disqualification

In order to protect the public from the actions of a rogue director, modern companies legislation, as found in some jurisdictions, contains provisions that enable a court to make a disqualification order against a person, who is thereby prevented from becoming a director or being otherwise involved in the formation or management of companies.

If a person is considered to be unfit to act as a director owing to general (or specific) misconduct, the court may disqualify that person from acting as a director, on the grounds that it is in the public interest. For example, the person might have been providing corporate services in a manner that is considered to be in serious contravention of accepted standards of 'best practice' , or might have been involved in an arrangement that has been ruled to be dishonest or in contravention of local regulations (such as those relating to investment business).

Some jurisdictions do not confer statutory power upon the court to make a disqualification order against a director. Nonetheless, the issue is moot because the financial services regulator can prevent a person from becoming a director of a managed company by rejecting their application for a company management licence or, if such licence has already been issued, revoking the same.
