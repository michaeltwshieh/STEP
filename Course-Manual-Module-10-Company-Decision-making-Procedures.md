## Module 10: Company Decision-making Procedures

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- explain the difference between the decision-making powers of the board of directors and the powers of shareholders of a company in general meetings
- review the procedures for convening and conducting general meetings of shareholders
- review the procedures for convening and conducting board meeting
- summarise and illustrate other decision-making procedures
- develop your drafting skills, with particular emphasis on drafting notices, resolutions and minutes of meetings.

## Introduction

A company is unable to make decisions for itself. Instead, it requires the thought processes and actions of its members and directors.

In this module, we consider ways in which the corporate decision-making process is performed. Particular emphasis is placed on practical issues with which those who administer offshore companies should be familiar, such as the preparation of different types of resolution and the drafting of notices and minutes of meetings. After an introduction to the subject of company decision making, in particular a review of the division of power between shareholders and directors (in section 1), we deal with the procedural requirements for meetings of shareholders (section 2) and directors (section 3). In the final section, we consider the procedures for carrying out specific corporate actions.

## 1. The division of power between the directors and the members

A company has two power bases:

- i. the board of directors, who are generally given decision-making powers to enable them to manage the affairs of the company on a day-to-day basis, and whose collective decisions and actions are binding on the company; and
- ii. the members, who are the owners of the company.

As discussed in Modules 7 and 8, the actions of the board of directors should always benefit the company. In other words, their actions should ultimately benefit all the shareholders. In view of this, the members of a company have certain rights and powers designed for supervision and oversight and to hold the directors to account.

Owing to the division of power within a company, there are certain decisions that can be taken only by the shareholders, and others that should be taken only by the board of directors. Those who administer offshore companies should, therefore, have a sound working knowledge of the division in the decision-making process and understand who can make which decisions on behalf of a company.

Before we look in detail at the decision-making processes of the members and the directors, please note that we can cover only general issues in this section. This is because a company's internal rules and regulations are determined (within the broad framework of applicable companies legislation) by the constitution of the particular company concerned, i.e. its memorandum and articles. There are many variations. Accordingly, before proceeding with any action on behalf of a company, one should first determine whether the proposed action is within the laws of the centre concerned, and then consult the company's memorandum and articles of association in so far as they affect or relate to the proposed action.

## 2. General meetings of shareholders

## 2.1 Types of general meeting

A 'general meeting' is the term applied to meetings held by the shareholders of a company whose names are listed in the register of members. 155  When the articles follow the traditional onshore company model, such as Appendix 1B of the online Study Resources, there are two types of general meeting. Note, however, that the statutory requirement to hold an AGM has been abandoned for private companies incorporated in England and in most offshore jurisdictions, where it is optional.

## 2.1.1  Extraordinary general meetings (EGMs)

An 'EGM' is basically any type of general meeting of the company apart from the annual general meeting. 156  An EGM is often convened to discuss business that cannot be deferred until the next annual meeting. The directors usually have the power to convene an EGM, 157  although the members also have the power to requisition such a meeting, provided that a specified number of shareholders are party to the requisition (see section 2.2.1 below). 158

## 2.1.2  Annual general meetings (AGMs)

Companies have historically been required to convene and hold a meeting of the shareholders on an annual basis. The AGM is no longer compulsory in most jurisdictions. But traditional articles adopted by old or individual special-purpose companies may impose an obligation to hold one, in which case an AGM must be held. In these circumstances, the timing of the first annual meeting is usually within 18 months of incorporation. Subsequent annual meetings are usually held once every calendar year and not more than 15 months apart. 159

The business usually conducted at an AGM includes: 160

- consideration of the annual accounts and reports, if applicable
- the declaration of a dividend, if applicable
- retirement and appointment of directors if retirement is by rotation or otherwise applicable
- appointment and remuneration of the auditors, if applicable.

Depending on the provisions contained in the articles, notice of a general meeting (EGM or AGM) must be sent to every member. 161  If there are members who do not have voting rights, suitable wording should be included in the notice, confirming that it is being sent to them for information purposes only. In such cases, it is advisable to state that receipt of the notice does not confer any right to attend and vote.

In view of the nature of the business usually conducted at the AGM, and in order to streamline administration procedures, most offshore jurisdictions allow a company to dispense with the requirement to hold an AGM. 162

## 2.2 Convening a general meeting

## 2.2.1  Who can convene a general meeting?

Each company has its own rules set out in its articles, governing how and when a general meeting should be held and who is able to call such a meeting.

Standard articles provide that the directors have the power to convene a general meeting. This is done simply by passing a board resolution (a specimen is illustrated in Appendix 25A of the online Study Resources) and instructing the secretary or corporate service provider to issue a notice of the intended meeting. The members also often have the right to make the directors call a meeting in certain circumstances, such as when at least 10% of the members with voting rights want an EGM to be held. 163 A specimen request of shareholders addressed to the board to convene a general meeting is illustrated in Appendix 25B of the online Study Resources.

The articles typically require proper notice of a proposed general meeting to be given to the shareholders.

## 2.2.2  Length of notice period

The articles usually specify the notice period that is required for certain types of business. 164 For instance, it could be seven, 10, 14 or 21 days. The articles may state that the period of notice must be measured by 'clear days' . In calculating 'clear days' , the date of service of the notice and the date of the proposed meeting are not included.

## 2.2.3  Short notice

In practice, most general meetings of offshore companies are held with little or no notice period, because nominee shareholders are usually provided. Even if nominee shareholders are not provided, however, shorter notice than that specified in the articles is often permitted by standard articles that provide that, if all (or a specified proportion of shareholders, such as 95%) agree to waive or shorten the notice period, such notice (and therefore business transacted at the meeting) is valid. 165 A form of consent to short notice is illustrated in Appendix 25C of the online Study Resources.

If short notice is sanctioned, or notice is waived, the minutes of the meeting should refer to the fact that the necessary consents were given for the notice period to be shortened or waived altogether.

## 2.2.4 Service of notice

A copy of the notice should be sent to all members entitled to attend and vote at the proposed meeting, and in some circumstances, when auditors have been appointed, a copy must also be sent to them.

The notice of a general meeting must be in writing and standard articles specify how notices are to be served. 166

## i. Notice by post

Notices may be sent by post to the registered addresses of the members stated in the register. This may create problems if a notice becomes lost in the postal system. Accordingly, to combat this, it is customary for the articles to state that a notice that has been properly addressed, stamped and posted is deemed to have been received by the member. 167  The actual date upon which a member is deemed to have received notice is another issue. The articles of onshore companies often assume that safe delivery is effected within 48 hours of posting, whereas companies located in offshore centres need to take into account overseas postal times, which vary from country to country. 168

## ii. Special delivery

To combat arguments caused by posting notices, many service providers prefer to send notices by special delivery, registered post or by a recognised courier agent. Not only will the date of dispatch be recorded but also the date of delivery.

## iii. Notice by fax or email

These are popular alternative, modern, methods of service, although the articles must specifically authorise this method to be used 169  (as there may be arguments over whether the fax or email has been safely received in legible format).

## 2.2.5  Contents of a notice

Companies legislation does not prescribe any particular style for the notice of a general meeting. It is common practice, however, for the articles to state that the following must be included:

- the name of the company
- the type of general meeting to be convened, e.g. AGM or EGM
- the place, date and time of the proposed meeting
- a summary of the topics to be discussed at the proposed meeting (a typical article 170 provides that the notice should include '... in the case of special business, the general nature of that business' - 'special business' being any business other than the matters usually dealt with at the AGM, and listed in section 2.1.2 above).

The notice should be signed, usually by the company secretary, who has prepared it on the instructions of the directors.

A specimen notice for an AGM is illustrated in Appendix 25D of the online Study Resources, and for a general meeting, other than an AGM, sometimes called an EGM, is illustrated in Appendix 25E of the online Study Resources.

## 2.2.6 Failure to give notice

Depending on any contrary provisions in the articles, failure to give proper notice to all those who are entitled to receive such notice invalidates the proceedings of the meeting. A standard article 171  provides that accidental omission to give notice does not, however, invalidate matters.

Unfortunately, it is not always clear what constitutes 'accidental' . In addition, it may generally be assumed that a shareholder who attends a meeting, either in person or by proxy, has received valid notice.

Many offshore service providers do not give written notice of general meetings to shareholders of those companies for which they provide the registered shareholders. In such cases, notice is deemed to have been served and received, and paperwork is therefore kept to a minimum. Notice is usually given in all other cases.

## 2.3 The agenda

An agenda is basically an aide-memoire for use by the chairman of the meeting, as well as those entitled to attend. The notice summarises the main items of business to be discussed at a general meeting. An agenda provides a list, in correct order, of all matters to be covered at the meeting, not just the main business. The order is often determined by the complexity of the matters to be discussed - usually items that require little or no discussion are covered first. The order can be altered, however, and new items added if necessary.

The following items are usually included in an agenda for a general meeting:

- receipt of apologies from those unable to attend
- confirmation of who is present by proxy
- a review of the minutes of the previous meeting, a discussion of any matters arising from those minutes, and the adoption of those minutes, amended if appropriate
- the business covered in the notice
- any other business
- the proposed date of the next meeting.

An agenda is not always considered necessary for members' meetings of offshore companies where the registered shareholders are provided by the offshore service provider.

## 2.4 Appointment of a proxy

Typical articles provide that a member who is unable to attend and vote at a general meeting may appoint someone to attend and vote on their behalf. 172  This person is called a proxy and may be the chairman of the meeting, another member who can attend or some other person who is not connected with the company.

Usually, proxies have the right to speak at the meeting for which they have been appointed. The articles of some companies provide that a proxy may vote on a poll only; they cannot vote on a show of hands. 173 Proxies are expected to vote in accordance with the instructions of the registered shareholders they represent, although there may be occasions when a proxy is given discretion to decide what is appropriate. An example of a so-called open proxy is illustrated in Appendix 25F of the online Study Resources, and a specific proxy, instructing the proxy how to vote, in Appendix 25G of the online Study Resources.

If the registered shareholder is a corporate entity owned by a service provider, it is common practice for the corporate entity to appoint an individual to act as its proxy at general meetings of the companies for which it acts. These individuals tend to be senior officers of the service provider. Forms of proxy are required for each company concerned.

Alternatively, a corporate shareholder may appoint a representative to act on its behalf. This circumvents the need for it to appoint proxies for each meeting of client companies. 174  An example of a resolution of the board of a corporate shareholder appointing a corporate representative to attend a specific shareholders' meeting, or generally, is illustrated in Appendix 25H of the online Study Resources. A notice of the appointment of a representative of the corporate shareholder to the company in which the corporate shareholder holds shares is illustrated in Appendix 25J of the online Study Resources.

## 2.5 Quorum

In order for the decisions made at a general meeting of the shareholders of a company to be valid, the meeting must be properly convened and constituted. The notice requirements determine whether a meeting has been properly convened. In order for a meeting to be properly constituted, a certain minimum number of people, the quorum, must be present either in person or by proxy. In other words, the meeting must be quorate before any business can be transacted. 175

## 2.5.1  Usual number

The minimum number of members of a company is prescribed by applicable companies legislation. It used to be two, but a sole shareholder is now allowed in most jurisdictions. Nonetheless, the articles may fix the quorum at a higher number, such as three or more. Whether a person who attends a meeting by telephone or by video conferencing facilities is considered to be 'present' depends on the specific provisions of the articles. 176

A member who attends a meeting on their own behalf while also acting as a proxy for another member counts only as one person present.

## 2.5.2  Inquorate meetings

If a meeting is held and there are insufficient members present, the meeting is said to be inquorate and should be adjourned. The business, if any, conducted at an inquorate meeting is invalid. 177  If the meeting becomes inquorate during the course of the meeting, perhaps as a result of a member having to leave midway through the proceedings, only those matters addressed after the member's departure are invalid.

## 2.5.3  Single member companies

Single member companies do not have general meetings. Instead, written resolutions are passed by the single member. Although it is illogical for a single shareholder to have a 'meeting' with only one person being present, for company law purposes, unless the articles provide otherwise, this is a valid 'meeting' .

Some service providers record decisions taken by the single member in the form of minutes of a general meeting. This is acceptable provided the minutes contain details of the resolutions passed and have ideally been signed by the single member. But, arguably, a written resolution is more appropriate, such as the ones illustrated in various Appendices.

## 2.6 Role of the chairman

For a meeting to be properly constituted, typical articles provide that a chairman must be appointed. 178  The chairman of a general meeting may be the chairman of the board of directors, although in offshore companies it is usual practice for the members to appoint someone from their ranks to act as chairman.

The usual role undertaken by the chairman is:

- to confirm that the meeting has been properly convened
- to confirm that a quorum is present
- to preserve order
- to ensure that the business as set out in the notice or agenda is covered
- to ensure decisions are taken if required
- to ensure that minutes of the meeting are kept.

## 2.7 Role of the secretary

When a company has a secretary, the secretary is usually responsible for:

- preparing and circulating the notice and agenda before a meeting
- organising the venue
- collating or preparing any supporting paperwork, such as reports that may be required for the meeting.

The company secretary normally attends the meeting to assist the chairman by taking notes, and may eventually be required to prepare the minutes and circulate copies to those entitled to receive them.

It is not necessary for an appointment of a secretary to be made in order for the meeting to be properly constituted. In jurisdictions that do not require a company to have a secretary, and the board of the company have decided not to have one, the functions of the secretary are delegated to the corporate service provider.

## 2.8 Motions and resolutions

A motion is essentially a proposal raised at a meeting for consideration. It may be recorded by stating 'It was suggested that ...' or '[name] moves that ...' . Once a motion has been passed, it becomes a resolution. A resolution is, therefore, a decision that has been reached by the company in general meeting.

There are, following traditional English companies legislation, two types of resolution that may be passed in a general meeting: an ordinary resolution or a special resolution.

Whether an ordinary or a special resolution is required depends upon the importance of the motion proposed and is determined by either the provisions of applicable companies legislation or by the provisions of the articles. The rule is this: ordinary business of the type routinely conducted at the AGM requires an ordinary resolution; special business requires a special resolution.

Ordinary resolutions require a simple majority of votes in favour of the motion.

To be passed, special resolutions require a supermajority in their favour. They are required in order to transact business of significance, other than general business. A special resolution is usually required to deal with major decisions affecting the company, such as an alteration to the memorandum or articles of association, a reduction of share capital or a change of name, to migrate, to place a company into voluntary liquidation or to vary the rights of a class of shareholders. This type of resolution usually requires a two-thirds or three-quarters majority, depending upon the provisions of applicable companies legislation. 179 Copies of special resolutions have to be filed with the registrar of companies.

Note that IBCs, BVI BCs and the New Manx Vehicle do not distinguish between ordinary or special resolutions (unless specifically provided for in a company's articles). A resolution of the shareholders of these companies in general meeting can be passed by a simple majority. 180

## 2.9 Voting at general meetings

According to typical articles of companies across all jurisdictions, there are two ways in which voting can be conducted and votes counted: either by a show of hands or by a poll.

## 2.9.1  Show of hands

Those members who are present vote by raising their hands either in support of or against a motion. Typical articles 181  state that one vote is allocated to each member present and proxies are not able to vote. A simple majority of votes cast determines the outcome of ordinary resolutions.

If there is a tie, the chair typically will have a casting vote. 182

The articles usually contain provisions that deal with how voting should be conducted where there are joint holders, in which case it is usually the first-named person on the register of members who is able to vote. 183

## 2.9.2 Poll

Typical articles 184  provide that the chair or members commanding a sufficient proportion of votes may request that, instead of a show of hands, a vote may be carried out by a poll. In a poll, each member is allocated a number of votes in accordance with the number of shares owned. Voting by a poll is usually undertaken by ballot in writing, and ballot papers are used to record the votes cast. Proxy votes count on a poll.

## 2.10  Adjournments

It is possible for a meeting to be halted before all the business mentioned in the notice or agenda has been covered. This is called an adjournment and may be required, for example, if order during the meeting has been lost and cannot be restored, or if further information is required to enable discussions to continue. Those present may ask the chair to adjourn the meeting, or in some cases, the chair may have the power to make this decision. 185

If a meeting is adjourned, it should be resumed in due course. The new meeting is considered to be a continuation of the one that was halted. As such, only business that had not been discussed and resolved should be covered at a reconvened meeting following an adjournment. Notice of the resumed meeting is often required. 186

## 2.11  Format of the minutes of a meeting

A permanent record should be prepared of matters that have been discussed and resolved at a general meeting. This record traditionally takes the form of minutes of the meeting. There is no statutory requirement as to the form of the minutes of a general meeting, although the following matters are often covered:

- The type, date and place of the meeting are generally used as the main heading of the minutes.
- The persons present (either in person or represented by proxy) are listed, as are those persons who have submitted apologies for absence.
- The chair is elected.
- The person taking the minutes, as secretary of the meeting, may be referred to (although this is not necessary).
- It is confirmed that the required period of notice for the meeting has been given (or that a shorter notice period has been accepted).
- It is confirmed that a quorum is present.
- The business, as set out in the notice or agenda, is then dealt with item by item. Once a decision has been reached, the term 'It was resolved' (or 'Resolved') is generally used ahead of an accurate written description of the decision reached.
- Once all the itemised business has been dealt with, the minutes cover any other business raised by those present.
- Once the other business has been covered, if there is any, the chair brings the meeting to a close.
- Finally, the minutes are usually signed by the chairman of the meeting or by the chair of the following general meeting. Signing the minutes in this way signifies that the minutes represent a true record of what was discussed and resolved.

## 2.12  Filing

Some types of special resolution should be filed with the registrar, such as a resolution to change the company's name, or alter its constitutive documents, as well as being recorded in the company's minutes. A form of resolution for filing with the registrar is illustrated in Appendix 25L of the online Study Resources.

Ordinary resolutions, where there is no requirement to file with the registrar, should merely be filed in the company's minute book.

## 3. Directors' meetings

The day-to-day management of a company, in almost all cases, rests with the directors. From time to time, the directors meet to discuss items of business that require a decision. Many of the principles that apply to the conduct of general meetings of shareholders also apply to meetings of the board of directors, but there are a few differences.

## 3.1 Convening a directors' meeting

The laws of some offshore centres used to stipulate that a directors' meeting must be held at least once a year. 187  This statutory requirement has now been dispensed with and the timing of meetings is generally not stipulated. A meeting is required whenever the directors are faced with a business decision, and therefore the frequency of board meetings tends to be determined by the level of activity of the company. 188  Usually, any single director can call for a board meeting to be held. 189

## 3.2 Notice

Although the articles prescribe the notice requirements for a members' meeting, in most cases, for the period and method of notice for a directors' meeting, it is left to the directors to decide what is appropriate in the circumstances. The result is often that short or reasonable notice only is required, which may be communicated verbally rather than in writing. 190

## 3.3 The agenda

Although not a requirement, the preparation and circulation of an agenda can be useful for board meetings if the business to be discussed requires reasoned consideration before the meeting is held.

## 3.4 Appointment of an alternate

A director who is unable to attend and vote at a meeting of the directors may be allowed, by the articles, to appoint someone else to attend instead; this person is called an alternate director . 191  Once appointed, the alternate director is bound by the same duties and responsibilities as the other directors. An alternate director should only make decisions that they believe to be in the interests of the company and should not act slavishly in accordance with the instructions or wishes of the director for whom they are acting. In England the new Model Articles introduced in 2008 do not contain provisions for the appointment of an alternate director, so special articles are needed when alternate directors are desirable.

## 3.5 Quorum at a directors' meeting

A quorum of directors is required before they may dispatch business. The actual number depends on the articles. At least two directors or a majority of directors present, or represented by an alternate, usually constitute a quorum, 192 although it may be one if a sole director is appointed.

## 3.6 Conduct of board meetings

A meeting of the directors can be an informal affair, because most companies allow their directors to hold and conduct meetings as they consider appropriate. Even so, it is usual for every meeting to have a chair. 193

## 3.7 Resolutions

There is only one type of resolution that can be passed by the directors. These are decided by majority vote. 194  The articles typically confer upon the chairman a second, casting vote in the event of a tie. 195

## 3.8 Form of minutes and resolutions

The general form of directors' minutes and resolutions is much the same as that applicable to members' minutes and resolutions.

## 4. Written resolutions

Instead of holding a meeting to discuss business and pass resolutions, it is nowadays possible for a written resolution to be circulated to the members and/or directors, who may sign it if they are in agreement. 196

A shareholders' written resolution must be circulated to all members who have the right to attend and vote at a general meeting and, in order for the resolution to be passed, it must be signed by all such members. The date the last signature is obtained is usually treated as the date on which the resolution is passed.

Usually, notice of the intention to circulate a written resolution must be given to the auditors (if appointed), who may decide that a meeting, rather than a written resolution, is necessary to cover the business proposed.

Depending on the provisions in the articles, it is usually possible for written resolutions to be passed by the directors along the same lines as for members. Written resolutions, whether they are resolutions of the members or of the directors, are popular ways of transacting business. They are convenient and extremely useful where a decision is required, but it is not possible to convene a meeting owing to the absence or unavailability of one or more of the people required to attend.

## 5. Corporate procedures

This final section provides concise summaries of some of the more common corporate procedures, sometimes known as corporate actions, which require resolutions to be passed.

## 5.1 Procedure for annual general meetings

Depending upon the type of business to be transacted and the terms of applicable companies legislation and the articles of the company under scrutiny, it may be necessary to follow the procedure below for a company's AGM.

- Prepare draft financial statements (and director's report, if required or deemed necessary); and obtain the approval of the company's auditors in the unusual circumstances when they have been appointed.
- Convene and hold a meeting of directors to resolve to convene an AGM, approve the financial statements (and directors' report) and recommend the payment of a dividend.
- Convene the AGM and serve proper notice.
- Hold the AGM, obtain forms of appointment of corporate representatives and forms of proxy, and transact the usual business by passing all appropriate resolutions.

- Draft minutes of the meeting for signature by the chairman and file in the company's minute book.

Clearly not all these steps are necessary for a company under the management of offshore service providers (indeed, an AGM may well be waived altogether). Nonetheless, it is illuminating to realise the hoops that even private companies have to jump through to comply with traditional onshore requirements.

## 5.2 Change of directors

Subject to the provisos mentioned in the introduction to section 5.1 above, it may be necessary to follow the procedures below to effect a change of directors.

- Check the articles to ascertain the provisions relating to the retirement of directors; for instance, whether a notice period is required, or whether the resignation has to be in writing.
- Obtain the proposed new director's written consent to take up office.
- Convene a meeting of the board of directors, or the shareholders, as appropriate, depending upon who has the power to appoint subsequent directors, and pass an appropriate resolution.
- Prepare minutes for signature by the chair.
- Update the register of directors and officers.
- The new director may enter into a formal DSA with the company.
- File notification with the registrar of companies giving details of the resignation and appointment of a new director.

## 5.3 Transfer of shares

Subject to the usual provisos and, in particular, on the assumption that all compliance requirements have been met in relation to AML, counter financing of terrorism (CFT), FATCA and CRS, it is necessary to follow the corporate procedures below to transfer shares.

- Check the articles to ascertain whether any pre-emption rights have been observed (or whether the shareholders have waived their rights).
- Company secretary/administrator receives the share transfer form, duly executed by the transferor and transferee, together with the transferee's share certificate, which has to be surrendered.
- Convene and hold a board meeting to resolve to approve the transfer.
- Update the register of members and register of beneficial owner, or equivalent.
- Cancel the old certificate (by writing 'cancelled' across it), prepare and issue a new certificate to the transferee.
- [Make a return to the registrar of companies, if necessary.]

If it is only the beneficial ownership of the shares that is being transferred, all that is necessary is an irrevocable written instruction to be given by the beneficial owner (similar to the one illustrated in Appendix 26 of the online Study Resources) for the service provider to execute a new shareholder nominee agreement (Appendix 27A of the online Study Resources) or declaration (Appendix 27B of the online Study Resources) and update the register of beneficial owners, or equivalent.

## 5.4 Change of registered office

Subject to the aforesaid provisos, it is usual to follow the procedures below to change the registered office of the company to another address situated within the same jurisdiction.

- The director(s) pass a board resolution to change the address of the company's registered office.
- The secretary/service provider makes a return to the registrar notifying the registrar of the change.
- The secretary/service provider amends company stationery, name plate, etc.
- Secretary/service provider transfers all statutory registers to the new address and notifies the company's bankers, brokers and other interested parties.

## 5.5 Increase authorised capital

Subject to the usual provisos, typical procedures for increasing the authorised share capital of a company involve the following steps.

- Convene and hold a board meeting to recommend the proposed increase in share capital.
- Convene and hold an [extraordinary] general meeting of shareholders and pass a resolution to increase the authorised capital; in those jurisdictions that follow the 1948 Act model, a special resolution is required.
- File a copy of the [special] resolution with the registrar of companies, notifying the registrar of the resolution.
- Endorse the amendment upon the company's memorandum of association.

## 5.6 Change of company name

Subject to the usual provisos, typical procedures for changing the company's name involve the following steps.

- Seek approval of the proposed new name from the registrar.
- Convene and hold a meeting of directors to recommend that the company's name be changed.
- Convene and hold a shareholders' meeting to resolve a change of name of the company which, according to the 1948 model, requires a special resolution.
- File a notification with the registrar, advising them of the name change.
- Endorse details of the change of name on the memorandum and articles of association.
- Amend company stationery and name plate at the registered office, etc.
- Notify the company's bankers, brokers, creditors, insurers, customers and other relevant third parties.
- Obtain a new company seal, if necessary.

## 5.7 Dividends from companies with traditional articles

A company incorporated in jurisdictions that follow the 1948 Act model and that has adopted traditional articles similar to those illustrated in Appendix 1B may declare a dividend for the shareholders at its AGM. When an AGM is dispensed with, or distributions are to be made otherwise than at the AGM, the following procedure should be followed.

- The directors review the financial condition of the company and establish whether there are distributable profits.
- If so, the directors pass a resolution to convene a general meeting and recommend the payment of a dividend.
- The shareholders meet to consider the recommendation of the board and vote on whether to declare the dividend by ordinary resolution.
- If the resolution is passed, the board instructs the secretary, or service provider, to make the payment by bank transfer. Note that nominee shareholders typically assign all dividends payable to the beneficial owner by terms of a typical nominee shareholder agreement (see para.3 of Appendix 27A).

Streamlined articles may confer on the directors the power to pay not only interim dividends but also a final dividend.

## 5.8 Distributions from BVI BCs (or Bahamian IBCs)

In practice, transfers of cash to the beneficial owner of asset-holding companies are often made far more frequently than once a year and, accordingly, a streamlined procedure is required. For this reason the old BVI IBCA, mirrored by the Bahamian IBCA, allowed IBCs to declare and pay dividends and distributions by resolution of the directors. 197

The streamlined procedure is carried over into the new BVI BCA 2004, but subject to new requirements and terminology. When the company under management is a BVI BC then ' distributions '  (as defined in s.56(b)) should be ' authorised '  rather than ' dividends declared ' . Under s.57(1), the directors, rather than shareholders, may authorise a distribution and, under s.57(2), the resolution of the directors must contain a statement that, in the opinion of the board, the company satisfies the statutory 'solvency test' (as defined in s.56(b) - see Module 6 section 7.3). In these circumstances, the following procedure is followed.

- The directors review the company's accounting records to ascertain whether there are distributable funds and that the company is solvent and will remain so after the distribution.
- Convene a board meeting or pass a written resolution to:
- a. confirm the company is solvent and satisfies the statutory solvency test; and
- b. make the distribution.
- The administrator arranges to make the payment, as directed by the company's nominee shareholder(s), to the beneficial owner by bank transfer.

## 5.9 Procedures for allotting and issuing new shares

According to articles similar to art.2.3 of Appendix 1B, a company should follow these corporate procedures for allotting and issuing new shares.

- The corporate service provider should check the articles to ascertain whether the directors have power to allot and issue shares. (If the directors lack authority, then convene a general meeting (or have the board pass a written resolution), for the purposes of passing a resolution to change the articles to confer authority upon the directors to issue shares.)
- The board of directors negotiates with the prospective investor and, when terms are agreed, the secretary/service provider will typically receive an application to subscribe for shares in accordance with the agreed terms.
- The service provider, on behalf of the board, conducts the required know your client (KYC) procedures for compliance with anti money laundering (AML), combating the financing of terrorism (CFT), Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) and Common Reporting Standard (CRS) requirements.
- Convene a board meeting to pass a resolution (or draft a written resolution) to approve the application to subscribe for shares and resolve to allot and issue the new shares, and seal and deliver a new share certificate.
- The board instructs the company secretary/administrator to prepare and execute a new share certificate and deliver it to the new shareholder in exchange for the receipt of payment of the agreed consideration.
- Secretary/administrator updates the register of members and register of beneficial ownership.
- If necessary, notify the registrar of companies and relevant competent authority [by reporting date].

## 5.10  Procedures for auditing the company's accounting records and financial statements

According to typical articles such as those illustrated in Appendix 1B, the following procedures should be followed when the shareholders wish to have the company's accounts audited.

- Convene a meeting of shareholders (or the proposal may be made at the company's AGM) to consider whether to have the company's accounting records and financial statements audited.
- Pass ordinary resolutions to:
- a. have the company's financial statements audited;
- b. appoint a selected auditing firm; and
- c. instruct such firm to conduct the desired level of audit.
- iii. Engage the services of the selected auditor and execute an engagement letter.

## 5.11  Borrowing and security

The articles typically confer upon the board of directors a power to borrow money on behalf of the company. The power may be unrestricted as to amount or be subject to a maximum, beyond which the approval of the shareholders is required. 198

A typical form of borrowing is a bank loan. When the bank allows the company to overdraw its current account, the British refer to this as an overdraft facility; when a company overdraws its checking account the Americans call it a line of credit facility. The borrowing may be unsecured or secured by way of mortgage (often referred to as a 'charge') over the assets of the company. The charge may be fixed and or floating: see the explanation in Module 12 section 1.1.

The corporate procedures for borrowing in this way are as follows.

- The directors resolve to borrow by way of loan agreement (or bank overdraft facility agreement) and to mortgage the company's assets by way of fixed and/or floating charge. A specimen resolution of the board is illustrated in Appendix 25M of the online Study Resources.
- The required documentation is executed and delivered.
- Particulars of the charge must be entered into the company's register of charges. This is a statutory requirement in some jurisdictions, but optional in others: see Module 3 section 4.1.4.

## 5.12  Complex borrowing transactions

Borrowing from more than one lender can create complexities, particularly when security is given and one lender has priority over the other(s).

The corporate procedures are similar to those described in section 5.11 above, but the resolutions require careful drafting. A further illustration appears in Appendix 25N of the online Study Resources. Clearly, in these circumstances, the company will engage attorneys to act for the company, so it may be a good idea to instruct them not only to approve the mortgage documentation, but also to draft the resolutions.
