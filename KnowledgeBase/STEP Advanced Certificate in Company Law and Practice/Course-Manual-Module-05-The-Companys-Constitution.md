## Module 5:

## The Company's Constitution

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- review the historical development of the corporate constitution
- discuss the principles behind modern rules and their original basis
- examine the functions and typical content of the memorandum and articles of association and determine how they can be changed to meet the requirements of the client
- analyse corporate capacity and trace the evolution of the ultra vires doctrine
- examine whether, and if so how, the memorandum may be altered
- examine how the articles may be altered.

## Historical introduction

We have already established that the legal structure of a limited company is a product of the needs of industry and commerce. Rules were developed from the 16th century to regulate the constitution of business undertakings at common law in England. But the common law concepts were considered to be somewhat inadequate and unsatisfactory. Accordingly, the legislature intervened to create statutory rules to encourage enterprise and stimulate a laissez-faire economy.

The initial companies legislation enacted in England was the Joint Stock Companies Act 1844, followed by the introduction of limited liability by the Limited Liability Act 1855, with each statute being consolidated into the Joint Stock Companies Act of 1856.

The basic principles underlying the traditional corporate structure are found in these statutes. The 1856 Act introduced the current English model company, used throughout the common law offshore jurisdictions. 83  The 1856 Act, for example, introduced the procedure for the incorporation of corporate entities by simple registration with the registrar of companies. The procedure involved the filing of a memorandum of association setting out the constitution of the company, which, in 1856, had to be signed by seven subscribers. 84

The internal rules and regulations of the new form of registered company were, by the mid-19th century, a matter for the company itself, and therefore did not have to be registered. The constitution of the company consisted of two documents: the memorandum and the articles.

In most jurisdictions of the world, the constitution of a company still follows this model, comprising:

- a memorandum (which must be filed with the registrar upon incorporation), and
- the articles (which may be filed upon incorporation or at a later date).

Having said that, England has modernised matters in the current Companies Act 2006 by transferring much of what would traditionally have been set out in the memorandum to a prescribed application form, IN01, leaving the memorandum merely to identify the promoters and state that they agree, upon incorporation, to take a stated number of shares in the company. Hong Kong and Singapore have also essentially abandoned the memorandum.

## 1. The constitution of a company

'Constitution' is a word generally used to indicate a form of supreme government. Where this is established by a written document (as, for example, in the US) the written document is called 'The Constitution' . 85

In the context of company law it means the documents and rules that govern a company. Historically, the constitution of a company comprises two documents.

- i. the function of the memorandum (occasionally known as its charter or, in Delaware, its certificate of incorporation ) is to govern the relationship between the company and third parties in the outside world; and
- ii. the function of the articles (occasionally known as the by-laws ) is to govern the internal organisation and affairs of the company.

In summary, the corporate constitution regulates the distribution of profit, risk and control within a company. 86  The memorandum and articles generally state:

- that the profits of the company are to be distributed in accordance with the rights conferred upon shareholders
- that the liability of members for corporate acts, defaults and liabilities is limited (by shares or by guarantee)
- that the responsibility for the conduct of the business of the company is allocated between the shareholders (in general meeting) and the directors (by democratic board resolution) in accordance with detailed provisions set out in the articles.

It cannot have escaped your attention that, over recent years, there has been considerable public debate in relation to the constitution of a traditional company. Much discussion has concentrated upon what is now termed 'corporate governance' . This has arisen because of the separation of ownership from control in large, public, often multinational companies where the directors and executives who manage the company do so on behalf of and for the exclusive benefit of a large and widely diverse body of shareholders.

It is often said that the board of directors is accountable to the shareholders. In other words, the board manages the business of the company in order to maximise the profits enjoyed by shareholders (to the exclusion of others involved in the undertaking). Yet as the Labour Party 87  in England has recognised, and the US author Brian Cheffins 88 points out, the typical constitution of a company totally ignores the interests and expectations of other key participants in a corporate undertaking, namely its employees, creditors and customers, and the public at large.

Directors and corporate executives owe a duty of care to the company, which effectively means the shareholders. They owe no such duty, under the Anglo-American model company structure, to the company's employees, customers and the community. This may have serious implications in the onshore jurisdictions but, as we shall see, little impact offshore.

The broad corporate constitution, comprising the memorandum and articles, is used for all forms of company, incorporated for many different purposes: public/private ownership, onshore/offshore, large/small, trading/passive asset-holding. Nonetheless, corporate regulation permits considerable flexibility. You would not expect the constitution of a large, publicly owned, onshore trading company to be the same as that of a small, private, single-member offshore asset-holding company. For example, BVI, Cayman Islands and Jersey companies law is flexible enough to permit, on the one hand, promoters of a company to structure its constitution to satisfy the most stringent corporate governance and onerous listing requirements of the world's largest and most prestigious stock exchanges and, on the other hand, to cater for a single shareholder, sole director, one-person, asset-holding vehicle. 89

We are clearly concerned with the latter. We therefore concentrate in this text upon the requirements of a typical private company incorporated offshore. As with most aspects of company law, however, it will help you to develop an understanding of the subject if you can appreciate the principles behind the current rules. You need to bear in mind that the constitution of an offshore company is based upon the corporate model developed in England, to cater principally for an actively trading, publicly owned operating company, rather than one that exists merely to hold assets for a single investor.

## 2. The memorandum

The memorandum was originally drafted as a deed, executed by each of the subscribers who, upon incorporation, went on to become the members of the company. Essentially, it contained promises by each subscriber/member to observe its provisions. A deed is no longer used, but the memorandum's legal effect is the same.

Typical companies legislation provides that the memorandum and articles:

'binds the company and its members ... as if each member had subscribed his name and fixed his seal thereto and as if there were contained therein ... a covenant [i.e. a promise] by each member (and his heirs) to observe the same.' 90

The memorandum must be signed by each subscriber, and their signatures witnessed (but in the case of (I)BCs, signed by the registered agent). Each subscriber must take at least one share in the company (except (I)BCs). The minimum number of subscribers differs between jurisdictions and types of company. It used to be two, but most jurisdictions have modernised their laws to permit a sole subscriber.

The content of the memorandum depends upon:

- the minimum requirements of the companies legislation in the jurisdiction of incorporation
- the individual needs and requirements of the client, i.e., the promoter, who must clearly comply with the above minimum requirements, but who is given considerable scope to mould the company into the type of structure that best suits its purpose.

In most common law offshore jurisdictions, the minimum requirement for a private limited company is for the memorandum to contain five or six basic clauses dealing with:

- the name of the company
- its registered office
- the liability of its members
- the objects of the company
- the capital of the company and its division into shares
- other jurisdiction-specific requirements.

The new companies legislation in Hong Kong (Cap 622, 2014) and Singapore (Cap 20, 2017) has essentially abandoned the memorandum as a separate document, and so the provisions listed above are found at the beginning of the articles. As stated above, in England under the Companies Act 2006, the above information is now stated in the prescribed application to incorporate, Form IN01, so that the memorandum merely states that: 'Each subscriber to this memorandum of association wishes to form a company under the Act and agrees to become a member of the company and to take at least one share' . The exact number and type of shares and the amount paid is set out in the application form.

## 2.1 Name

The first clause of the memorandum generally states the name of the company. See Appendix 1A cl.1. There are statutory restrictions and requirements in respect of the choice of name, which were reviewed in Module 3 section 3.3.

## 2.2 Registered office

The second clause generally states the jurisdiction (seldom the street address within the jurisdiction) of the company's registered office. See cl.2 of Appendix 1A. In these circumstances the registrar has to be notified separately upon incorporation of the precise location of the company's registered office.

A company is required to have a physical address for a number of reasons:

- to facilitate formal communications with the company
- to establish the nationality of the company
- to maintain its corporate records (and allow inspection thereof by members, and of its public records by the public).

A registered office is required to enable the outside world to have a means of contacting the company. This is particularly important for creditors of the company, as they must have an address to send payment requests and, if there is any subsequent conflict, final demands or legal process. Documents are deemed properly served upon a company if delivered to or sent by post to the address of its registered office.

A statement in the memorandum that the company's registered office is situated in State A establishes the nationality of the company. A company formed under the companies legislation of State A with a registered office in State A has State A nationality. Under traditional principles, a company retains this nationality throughout its life. As we saw in Module 4, however, modern offshore statutory provisions entitle a company to migrate from one jurisdiction to another, provided it complies with certain conditions.

The nationality of a company is not the same thing as its residence for tax purposes. A company incorporated and having its registered office in State A may be deemed to be resident in State B for tax purposes if its central management and control abide in State B. These international conflict of laws rules are dealt with in the STEP Advanced Certificate in Principles of International Taxation, AML and Compliance.

The registered office is also the place where certain company records must be kept.

For example:

- the register of members
- the register of beneficial ownership, or equivalent
- the register of directors
- the register of company secretary and other officers such as the registered agent
- the register of charges
- the accounting records
- the common seal, if applicable
- the minute book containing a record of the meetings of the members, and meetings of its directors.

## 2.3 Liability of members

The memorandum must state whether the liability of the company's shareholders is limited. See cl.3 of Appendix 1A.

If it is, the memorandum must state whether such liability is limited by shares or by guarantee. If limited by shares, the capital clause (often cl.5 - see section 2.5) must state the company's authorised capital, etc. If limited by guarantee, the capital clause must state the amount of the guarantee, i.e. a specified sum that each member promises to pay to discharge the company's debts on winding up. If the company is of unlimited liability, cll.3 and 5 are omitted.

This clause serves, at least in theory, as a warning to persons doing business with the company. They are forewarned to be careful as the liability of the members is limited!

## 2.4 The objects

The fourth clause deals with the capacity of the company to transact business by stating its objects. See cl.4 of Appendix 1A.

## 2.4.1  Introduction to corporate capacity

The doctrine of capacity relates to whether a person has the lawful power or authority to carry out an act or enter into a transaction or, for instance, is suffering from some kind of legal disability, e.g. is a minor, or mentally incompetent, or a bankrupt.

In the context of company law, 'capacity' relates to whether a company has power or authority to carry out an act or enter into a particular transaction - a transaction, moreover, that is intended to achieve an end result, i.e. an object: hence the name of this clause - the objects clause.

The concept of corporate capacity has now been largely reformed by modern companies legislation, but some vestiges remain. Once again, in order to understand the current rules, it is necessary to trace their origins.

## 2.4.2  The ultra vires doctrine

' Ultra vires '  is essentially an expression meaning that a person has exceeded their legal authority. In the context of company law it means that a company has acted in a manner that is outside or has exceeded its lawful objects. The act is therefore ultra vires , and void.

A company may exercise its powers to transact business only for a lawful object, i.e., one set out as an objective of the company in its objects clause.

The often quoted case of Ashbury Railway Carriage Co. Ltd v Riche 92 illustrates the application of the old rule (and how it can result in unfairness or loss to third parties who have entered into transactions with the company). Ashbury Railway Carriage Co. Ltd was incorporated in accordance with applicable companies legislation. Its objects, as stated in its memorandum, were to 'make, sell, lend on hire and repair railway carriages and wagons' . The directors then decided to expand the company's business and they entered into a contract with a third party to build a railway system in Belgium. The court held that the contract was void as the company had capacity only to carry out acts in pursuit of the objects specified in its objects clause. The company had acted ultra vires . The contract was therefore void and of no legal effect and was unenforceable, either by the company or by the third party. Neither party could sue in the event of the other's breach.

## What is the rationale behind the rule?

Under the traditional onshore Anglo-American corporate model, the shareholders invest money in a company (in exchange for shares) for the directors and executives of the company to use the capital raised in a certain manner, i.e. to pursue the company's objectives. You will recall that corporate assets belong to the company, but are managed by the directors. The directors ought not to be able to use the capital raised by the issue of shares in whatever way they feel is appropriate. There are constraints: the directors must use the capital only in pursuit of the stated objectives of the company. Accordingly, the ultra vires doctrine developed as a form of investor protection - to prevent directors going off on a frolic of their own and using corporate assets for purposes wholly different from those intended and contemplated by the shareholders when they invested in the company.

Notwithstanding the worthy principles behind the ultra vires doctrine, however, it subsequently became (as Gower puts it 93 ) 'a nuisance to the company and a trap for unwary third parties' .

It was a nuisance to the company as the subscribers (and their advisers) had to think of every conceivable object that the company might wish to undertake during its lifetime and ensure that it was included in the objects clause. If they were not included, the company could not enforce contracts entered into in pursuit of an ultra vires object.

The objects clause of the memorandum therefore became exceedingly long, cumbersome and vague. Instead of specifying the main objective of the company, it became buried in verbose draftership containing every conceivable type of activity that any company might wish to undertake. This was frowned upon by the courts, 94 which would interpret an objects clause by trying to discover the company's main objects and relegate all others as ancillary to these.

The ultra vires doctrine was a trap for unwary third parties because an apparently valid contract, voluntarily negotiated and entered into by a company and third party, might turn out to be unenforceable and of no legal effect if it was found that the company had acted ultra vires . This meant that neither the company nor the third party could sue the other for breach. This was a particular problem when companies, in their haste to enter into new lines of business, inadvertently failed to check their objects first; or when third parties, eager to obtain new business, failed to ensure, by checking the memorandum of the other contracting party, that it was indeed acting intra vires .

## 2.4.3  Reform of the ultra vires doctrine

The ultra vires doctrine was clearly unsatisfactory. It has therefore been reformed by modern companies legislation. The extent of the reform varies between jurisdictions, but the way in which it has been tackled follows one or more of three English templates or its complete abolition. When the English templates are followed, an ultra vires transaction which falls outside one of the company's lawful objects is valid and enforceable; however, the doctrine still exists to enable the company, through its shareholders, to sue the directors either to prevent them entering into an ultra vires transaction or, alternatively, for compensation for consequential loss. When the doctrine has been abolished it simply no longer applies insofar as a company has unrestricted capacity to conduct any type of business activity. The reform statutory templates are identified below.

- Section 35 Companies Act 1985 states that: 'In favour of a person dealing with a company in good faith, any transaction shall be deemed to be within the capacity of the company and … free from any limitations in the company's [objects clause in its] memorandum' . Note the proviso: a transaction that is not covered by the objects clause is nonetheless enforceable provided the other contracting party acted in 'good faith' , namely did not know that the company had entered into an ultra vires transaction.
- Section 35 was subsequently amended in 1989, so that the new s.35 reads:

'The validity of an act done by the company shall not be called into question on the grounds of lack of capacity by reason of anything in the company's [objects clause in its] memorandum.'

This removes the requirement of good faith, so that neither the company nor the other contracting party can invoke the ultra vires doctrine to wriggle out of a transaction entered into by the company but which was outside the company's objects. Nevertheless, it still exists to protect shareholders, who can bring proceedings against the directors who use or deal with corporate assets for purposes that are not covered by the objects clause.

<!-- image -->

- Section 31 of the Companies Act 2006 states: 'Unless a company's [constitution] restricts the objects of a company, its objects are unrestricted.' This makes the objects clause optional. If the company adopts an objects clause the doctrine still applies as in para.(ii) above; if the company does not have an objects clause then it has full capacity to conduct any business activity. Most jurisdictions have reformed the doctrine in this way. In these jurisdictions very few companies elect to have a restrictive objects clause. Instead, they have an unrestricted objects clause similar to the one illustrated in cl.4 of Appendix 1A, which states that:

'The Company may engage in any act, activity of business that is not contrary to law.'

- Some jurisdictions have simply repealed the ultra vires doctrine altogether, so all companies automatically have capacity to conduct any business activity. An objects clause is therefore completely redundant.

## 2.5 Share capital

The capital clause is often the fifth clause found in a company's memorandum. When the company is limited by shares and has a share capital, the clause states:

- the authorised (or nominal) capital of the company, i.e. the maximum amount of capital that the company is authorised to raise by issuing shares
- how the capital is divided up into shares
- the nominal (or par) value of each share (except for (I)BCs and other companies that are permitted to issue shares without a par value).

For example, as in cl.5 of Appendix 1A, the share capital clause may state that:

'The share capital of the company is $50,000 divided into 5,000 shares of $10 each.'

The company may then issue up to a maximum of 5,000 shares and thus raise $50,000 by way of share capital. The nominal (or par) value of each share is $10. Under traditional company law theory, a shareholder must pay at least $10 for each share allotted to him, otherwise the shares are sold at a 'discount' (in the US this is described as 'watered stock'). The company may sell its shares at any price above $10 that the market for those shares may determine. The actual price at which the share is sold is the 'issue price' , and the difference between par and the issue price is known as the 'premium' .

The figures set out in the capital clause are critical when determining the treatment of capital in the books and financial statements of the company and in establishing which funds can be distributed to shareholders in the future. The capital clause is designed as part of a package of measures that exists for the protection of persons who transact business with, and become creditors of, the company. The share capital, as set out in the memorandum (or at least that part of the share capital that has been issued), constitutes a separate fund that the company must keep intact in the balance sheet and not, under any circumstances, except on a winding-up, return to the shareholders. In theory, the capital fund should always be available to pay creditors upon the winding-up of the company. This is the so-called capital maintenance doctrine, to which we shall return in Module 6.

Alternatively, the capital clause may state that:

'The company's capital is divided into 10,000 ordinary shares [of common stock] of $10 each and 5,000 preference shares [preferred stock] of $25 each.'

The authorised capital of such a company is therefore (100,000 + 125,000 =) $225,000. The memorandum does not, however, describe the various rights that attach to the different classes of shares. The rights of holders of the ordinary shares and rights of holders of the preference shares are usually set out in the articles, or in the terms of the issue of the shares: for example, in the prospectus that the company may be required to publish and make available to prospective investors before the issue of new shares.

Private companies do not have a statutory minimum share capital, but public companies usually do.

The concept of share capital and the capital maintenance rules have been liberalised and simplified over recent years in a number of offshore financial centres. Companies incorporated in these jurisdictions may now elect not to have an authorised share capital and par value for their shares. Instead, the capital clause simply states that the company may issue up to a maximum number of shares that have no par value. This affects the company's capital structure, which is reviewed in the next module.

By way of example, the BVI BCA 2004 has essentially abolished the concept of authorised share capital and par value shares and these do not have to be referred to in the memorandum. Instead, the memorandum of a BVI BC must simply state the maximum number of shares it is authorised to issue, or that the company is authorised to issue an unrestricted number of shares. 96

## 3. Alteration of the memorandum

The constitution of a company is imposed upon the company on incorporation, within the legislative framework, by its promoters and subscribers, the latter becoming its first members. It is therefore logical that the members of a company have the right to alter its constitution.

Historically, once incorporated, the constitution of a company could not be changed. If, for instance, a company wished to expand into a line of business not covered by the objects clause, it could not seek to amend its objects - it would have to act as a promoter and incorporate a subsidiary with a new and appropriate objects clause.

It has been possible, for over a century, however, to change the constitution of a company after incorporation. The procedure, according to the 1948 Act model, requires a supermajority vote for a special resolution of the members of the company in general meeting. 97  Some offshore jurisdictions do not distinguish between an ordinary and a special resolution of shareholders and in these circumstances a simple majority is sufficient. Those jurisdictions with IBC legislation allow the directors to pass the resolution provided it is authorised in the articles.

The following is a summary of how changes to the memorandum may usually be made, although before making any alterations one should refer to the applicable companies legislation and also to the articles of the company, as these may set out specific requirements.

Note that any changes made to the memorandum must be notified to the registrar of companies in the offshore centre where the company is incorporated. Notification must be given within a certain period of time, usually 14 days or one month, from the date of the resolution.

## 3.1 Company name

According to the 1948 Act model, the name of a company may be changed by a special resolution of the members. Most jurisdictions follow suit, save for the IBC jurisdictions that do not differentiate between an ordinary and a special resolution to change the name of the company. 98  Section 12(1) of the BVI BCA, as amended 2005, states that the shareholders, by majority vote, may resolve to change the name of the company, although s.12(4) states that the memorandum may authorise the directors to do so. A specimen resolution to change the name of the company is illustrated in Appendix 11 of the online Study Resources.

Any new name must undergo the same checks and be subjected to the same criteria as the original name selected on incorporation (see Module 3 section 3.3).

Notification of the change of name must be filed with the registrar together with payment of the prescribed fee. The registrar will then amend the register of companies and issue an amended certificate of incorporation to reflect the change of name. Section 18(4) confirms that a change of name 'shall not affect any rights or obligations of the company nor render defective any legal proceedings brought by, or against, the company'.

Post change of name matters to attend to include such things as: an alteration to the name displayed at the registered office, on websites and notepaper; a new seal (if the company has adopted one) will be required; and interested persons, such as bankers, brokers, insurers, customers and other third parties, should be notified.

## 3.2 Registered office

The 1948 Act model does not contain any specific statutory procedure for moving the registered office of a company within the state of incorporation. Section 107 merely states that when it is moved the company must notify the registrar within 14 days. In these circumstances, it is widely accepted that a decision to change the location of the registered office is a matter for the directors. 99 Other jurisdictions follow this model; some, such as the IBC jurisdictions, specifically state that the directors may change the registered office by board resolution; the BVI BCA states that the directors may change it when the power to do so is conferred upon them by the articles. 100

The change of location of the company's registered office is not an alteration to the memorandum, which typically only states in the second clause that the registered office is located within the state of incorporation; there is therefore no legal or practical requirement to re-print the memorandum. The corporate procedure is to:

- a. pass an appropriate board resolution (a specimen of which is illustrated in Appendix 12 of the online Study Resources), and
- b. notify the registrar within 14 days (there may be a prescribed form as in England, 101 but otherwise a letter enclosing the board resolution will suffice, together with payment of the prescribed fee).

Note that the nationality (country of incorporation, and jurisdiction of the registered office) cannot be altered in this manner, save by formal migration. This is dealt with in Module 4 section 2 and typically requires a special resolution of the shareholders of the company in general meeting.

## 3.3 Nature of the company

The members can usually resolve by special resolution to alter a public company to a private company, and vice versa; a limited company to an unlimited company and vice versa; an ordinary company to a protected cell/segregated portfolio company and vice versa.

Any re-registration will require an overhaul of the constitutive documents of the company. In these circumstances it may be easier simply to incorporate a new company. But if it is decided to, for example, re-register a public company as a private company, the following steps are required. A board resolution should be passed to approve and confirm the re-registration, recommend a revision of the articles and convene a meeting of shareholders to consider the matter; a special resolution of shareholders should be passed to re-register the company and make consequential alterations to the articles; the special resolution and new articles must be filed with the registrar together with a statement of compliance and prescribed fee. Specimen resolutions are illustrated in Appendix 13 of the online Study Resources; note, however, that the specimen resolutions illustrate a re-registration from a public to a private company, and that this procedure is redundant in those jurisdictions that have abolished the distinction - see Module 3 section 3.2.1.

If the application is granted, the registrar will issue a certificate of incorporation on re-registration. Post re-registration matters to attend to include things such as: an alteration to the name displayed at the registered office, on websites and notepaper; a new seal may be required; and interested persons, including bankers, brokers, insurers and other third parties, should be notified, as for a change of name (section 3.1 above).

## 3.4 Liability of the members

The members can usually resolve by special resolution to alter their liability from unlimited to limited, but all members must unanimously consent to a change from limited to unlimited.

## 3.5 Share capital

Shares may usually be issued by resolution of the directors although, if the authorised capital has to be altered, the members must resolve to make such a change. 102 Typical companies legislation states that, subject to any contrary provision in the articles, this must be carried out by special resolution of the shareholders in general meeting.

## 4. The articles of association

## 4.1 Purpose of articles

The articles are the regulations of the company, governing its internal organisation, management and administration. Whereas the purpose of the memorandum is to set out the external or public aspects of the company for the benefit of third parties and creditors, the purpose of the articles is to regulate internal administration: to govern the directors, officers, shareholders and other insiders.

Upon incorporation, the promoters, or the subscriber(s), who become(s) the first member(s), may make such regulations for the internal governance of the company as they think fit, provided they comply with the broad statutory requirements prescribed by applicable companies legislation.

## 4.2 Model articles

The content of the articles depends upon the requirements of the client promoter and the preferences of their advisers. The form of the articles is not prescribed by statute. That said, in most jurisdictions model articles are provided, which may be adopted by the company, either in whole or in part.

Model articles in the basic form that we still find today were initially published as Schedules to the Joint Stock Companies Act of 1856. These model articles were still found in English companies legislation, albeit with modified language; they included:

- Table A , containing model articles for a company limited by shares
- Table B , containing further model articles for a company limited by shares (now repealed)
- Table C , containing model articles for a company limited by guarantee without share capital
- Table D , containing model articles for a company limited by guarantee with share capital
- Table E , containing model articles for an unlimited liability company.

The current English Companies Act 2006 has three forms of 'model articles' (rather than 'tables'), which are found in the Companies (Model Articles) Regulations 2008: the first is designed for a private company limited by shares (Sch.1); the second for a private company limited by guarantee (Sch.2); and the last for a public company (Sch.3).

In the common law offshore financial centres the promoters of a company may elect:

- to prepare their own, tailor-made articles
- to adopt a standard set of articles made available by the local companies legislation, where these exist, either wholly or making amendments to them as they see fit. 104

A number of trust companies and offshore corporate service providers use the statutory model articles (which typically still go by the name of 'Table A') as the basis for the articles of companies that they incorporate and manage on behalf of clients. Others have found, by trial and error, that certain alterations to the standard articles serve their clients' needs. Where possible, however, all providers try to adopt uniform articles for all their managed companies, so that the 'coal-face' company manager is comfortable and intimately familiar with their terms and can conduct day-to-day administration without (m)any surprises.

## 4.3 Usual contents

Rules and regulations found in standard articles endeavour to cover all aspects of internal organisation, management and administration, including the following points.

## 4.3.1 Share capital

- the power to issue shares and the rights attaching to different classes of share

- matters involving a company's lien on shares (when shares have been issued to shareholders but not fully paid for)

- procedures and other matters relating to the distribution of dividends to shareholders

## 5. Alteration of the articles

In those jurisdictions that follow the 1948 Act model, a company may, by special resolution of its shareholders, amend, restate, or add to its articles. The IBC jurisdictions, including the BVI, require a simple majority of shareholders to pass a resolution to amend the articles and the memo or articles may authorise the directors to do so. 105 It is unlawful to include a provision in the articles to the effect that they cannot be altered. 106

The procedure to follow in circumstances where the company has multiple shareholders is shown below. The procedure can be streamlined in the case of a company with a sole shareholder, when a written resolution can be passed and, for IBCs, note that an amendment can be made by resolution of the board when such power is conferred by the articles.

- The directors resolve to recommend an alteration to, or adopt new, articles (referred to in the US as a re-statement) and to convene a general meeting to consider a motion to pass a special resolution: see Appendix 15A of the online Study Resources.
- A general meeting is held and the shareholders pass a special resolution by super-majority vote to alter, or adopt new, articles (Appendix 15B of the online Study Resources).

- Where the amendments are significant it is often simpler to adopt a complete new set of articles by way of re-statement, rather than amend the existing articles piecemeal. In any case, it is the industry practice to prepare and print a new and complete copy of the articles as altered (see Appendix 15C of the online Study Resources).
- The secretary/registered agent/corporate service provider must notify the registrar, within a specified number of days, by forwarding a copy of the special resolution together with a copy of the new articles, and pay the prescribed fee, if any.

The general rule that the articles can be amended by a supermajority of the members is, however, subject to an implied provision that the power must be exercised by the shareholders in a bona fide manner for the benefit of the company as a whole. This condition is implied as a protective measure to prevent the majority shareholders from exercising their voting power to change the articles in some manner in their favour or for their advantage, to the detriment of the minority.

If an alteration to the articles is proposed by the majority shareholder(s) that:

- is oppressive or prejudicial to the minority
- would give the majority an advantage not available to the minority

the minority shareholder(s) may complain to the court, and a judge who finds the change contrary to the interests of the company as a whole has power to prevent the alteration from proceeding.

So, for example, in Greenhalgh v Arderne Cinemas Ltd 107 the broad facts were that a company had two shareholders: A with 67% of the shares and B with 33%. A, the majority shareholder, wanted to sell his shares to a third party, X. The articles contained a pre-emption clause which gave B the right to buy A's shares if he decided to sell them but A wanted to sell to X as he would get a better price. He therefore convened a meeting of shareholders and proposed a resolution to alter the articles by removing the pre-emption clause. B complained to the court to prevent the proposed alteration, on the grounds that the majority shareholder was acting in a manner that was oppressive and prejudicial to the minority. 108

In Dafen Tinplate Co. Ltd v Llannelly Steel Co. , 109  the majority shareholder proposed a resolution to change the articles to enable a shareholder with a certain proportion of shares to buy the shares of the minority 'at a fair price' . The court held that this was not a bona fide exercise of the shareholders' powers in the interests of the company as a whole. The result would probably be the same if the majority shareholder had promised to inject further capital into the company provided he was allowed to buy out the minority, 110 or offered a price for the minority shares above their market value. 111
