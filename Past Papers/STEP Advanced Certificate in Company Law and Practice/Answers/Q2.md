**2.1**

The jurisdiction chosen for this answer is the British Virgin Islands. Company Ltd ('Company') is treated throughout as a BVI business company incorporated under the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 ('BCA'), administered from the BVI by Corporate Services Provider, which also supplies the Company's registered agent (an office every BVI business company must fill, under s.91(1) BCA). The articles attached to this paper are the Company's articles, and every article reference below is to them.

Begin with who is actually giving the instruction. Mr AB's name appears nowhere in the register of members. The two issued shares stand in the name of Corporate Services Provider (Nominees) Ltd, and Mr AB's rights are those of a beneficial owner under a nominee shareholder agreement, not those of a member. He is not a director either. So the news that Mr AB "has arranged" a $5 million borrowing is a proposal and nothing more, and it arrives second-hand, through a relationship manager. Article 19.1 follows the 1948 Table A pattern: the business of the Company is managed by the directors, and nothing in these articles lets the members direct the board to take a specified step. The sole director must therefore make up its own mind, in good faith, on what it judges to be in the Company's interests. There is no such thing in law as a nominee director, whatever the client believes he is paying for.

Before anything else, refresh the client due diligence. A $5 million loan and the purchase of a business jet are a long way from the passive holding of assets, and they change the Company's risk profile. The administrator must satisfy himself as to the bona fides of Mr AB, the provenance of the funds coming into the structure (the deposit, the fees, and whatever will service the interest), and the purpose for which the Company is now to be used. Screen XYZ Bank and the aircraft vendor. Revisit the Company's FATCA and CRS classification and its economic substance position, and confirm that the Company is in good standing and its fees are paid. If the source of funds cannot be evidenced, the transaction stops there.

Capacity is quickly disposed of. Section 28 BCA gives a BVI business company full unrestricted capacity to carry on any business or activity, subject to its memorandum, and only a restricted purposes company under s.10 has its objects cut down. The old ultra vires doctrine of Ashbury Railway Carriage Co Ltd v Riche, under which a transaction outside the objects was void and unenforceable by either side, has been reformed out of the way. Still, read the memorandum. If it does restrict the Company's purposes, the members must amend it before the Company contracts.

Now the point on which the whole transaction turns. Article 19.3 confers on the directors the power to borrow and to mortgage the Company's property, but it is a power with a ceiling: the amount for the time being remaining undischarged of money borrowed by the directors may not exceed $1 million without the previous sanction of an ordinary resolution of the Company. The loan is $5 million. The board therefore cannot borrow this money on its own authority. The word "previous" carries weight. The members' sanction has to be obtained first, not tidied up afterwards.

Suppose the board went ahead anyway. The facts of Royal British Bank v Turquand were close to identical: the articles allowed borrowing only with the authority of the shareholders in general meeting, the directors borrowed without obtaining it, executed a mortgage under seal, and the company then collapsed and its liquidator argued that the loan did not bind the company. The argument failed. An outsider dealing in good faith, without notice of the internal irregularity, may enforce the transaction, and s.31 BCA now puts the same protection on a statutory footing for BVI business companies. XYZ Bank would very probably be paid. That is cold comfort for the director, because the rule protects the third party, not the board. Internally the director would have acted outside the authority the articles gave it and would answer to the Company for any loss flowing from the breach. In practice the bank's lawyers will demand the members' resolution as a condition precedent, so the omission is likelier to stall the drawdown than to sink the loan. It remains a breach of duty.

The definitions article makes an ordinary resolution one passed by a simple majority of the votes of the members entitled to vote. There is one member. Article 12.2 provides that where a company has a sole shareholder the quorum is one and the sole shareholder can pass a written resolution under art.12.11, so a written resolution signed by Corporate Services Provider (Nominees) Ltd is the obvious route. An extraordinary general meeting would also work, on 14 clear days' notice under art.11.1, or on short notice with the member's consent under art.11.2, with the nominee attending through a representative authorised by resolution of its own board under art.14. The written resolution is quicker and leaves a cleaner paper trail.

But the nominee cannot simply vote as it pleases. Under a standard nominee shareholder agreement it holds the shares as nominee, does not deal with them except as directed, and exercises the voting rights as the beneficial owner directs. So the administrator needs a written, signed instruction from Mr AB telling the nominee to vote in favour of sanctioning the borrowing, and a relationship manager's account of a telephone conversation will not do. That instruction goes on the file. Being a company, the nominee itself acts either by a resolution of its board or through an officer authorised to sign on its behalf.

The sole director acts the same way. Corporate Services Provider (Directorships) Ltd is a corporation and has no mind of its own; its own board must meet, or pass a written resolution, to consider the proposal, to resolve that the Company enter into it, and to authorise named officers to sign for the Company. Responsibility runs down the chain: those sitting on the board of a corporate director owe fiduciary duties and a duty of care to the corporate director, and through it to every managed company for which it acts. At the level of the Company, art.20.4 deems a sole director to form a quorum where the number of directors has been fixed at one, and art.20.5 lets a sole director exercise all the powers conferred on the directors by resolution in writing signed by him, art.20.15 saying much the same for written resolutions of directors generally. The board decision is accordingly taken as written resolutions, signed for and on behalf of the corporate director by its authorised signatories.

Then the conflict. Everything in this structure sits inside one group. The director, the member and the registered agent are all subsidiaries of Corporate Services Provider, which is paid for the work. The counterparties, XYZ Bank and the vendor of the aircraft, are strangers to the group, so on a narrow view the director has no interest in the loan or the mortgage at all. The articles are nonetheless drawn severely. Article 17.1(e) vacates the office of a director who is directly or indirectly interested in any contract with the Company. Article 17.2 draws that sting, provided the director declares the nature of his interest at the first board meeting held after he became interested and does not vote on the contract, and art.18.2 makes the same bargain for a contract or arrangement with the Company: disclose at the board meeting at which the contract is determined, then abstain. Applied to a company with one director, the arithmetic is uncomfortable. If the director must abstain there is no disinterested quorum and the board cannot act. The orthodox solution is to take the matter up to the members and obtain their approval by ordinary resolution, and any transaction fee or arrangement fee payable to the group should be disclosed in the resolution as part of the declaration. A second benefit follows. Prior approval by the members releases the director from liability for what might otherwise be a breach of duty, which matters here, because a jet bought for the beneficial owner's use is a transaction whose commercial benefit to the Company is not self-evident. The release is worth less than it looks when the approving member is the director's sister company, and that is precisely why Mr AB's own written instruction, and an indemnity from him under the company management agreement, do the real protective work.

Instruct counsel. BVI counsel should review the facility letter, the aircraft purchase agreement and the mortgage; US and Isle of Man advice will be needed on title to the aircraft and on the registries. Execution then follows the 1948 Act template reproduced across the offshore centres: a transaction that between individuals would have to be in writing and under seal must be sealed with the company's common seal, or executed by a director expressing an intention that the document take effect as a deed. A BVI business company is required to have a common seal, kept at the office of the registered agent. Article 22 permits the seal to be affixed only under the authority of a resolution of the board and in the presence of a director and the secretary or such other person as the directors appoint, both of whom must sign the instrument sealed in their presence. Two traps sit in art.21. The Company may not appoint as secretary its sole director, or a corporation whose sole director is the Company's sole director, or the sole director of the corporate director (art.21.2); and where the articles require a thing to be done by a director and the secretary, one person cannot satisfy that by wearing both hats (art.21.3). The individual who signs as director for the corporate director must therefore not be the individual who attests the seal.

Completion is largely a matter of assembling what the lender asks for. Expect its conditions precedent list to call for certified copies of the memorandum and articles, the certificate of incorporation, a certificate of good standing from the Registrar, the register of members and the register of directors, certified copies of both the members' ordinary resolution and the directors' written resolutions, a certificate of incumbency with specimen signatures, and the bank's own due diligence on the director, the nominee and Mr AB. Drawdown, payment of the price to the vendor, transfer of title, and the mortgage dated and delivered.

Post-completion, particulars of the mortgage must be entered in the Company's register of charges, which s.162 BCA makes compulsory for a BVI business company, recording the lender and its address, the type of charge, the property charged, and the date of the charge and of its recording. This is not housekeeping to be left for a quiet week. Failure to register does not invalidate the charge, but priority as between successive chargees may be determined by the date of registration rather than the date of the instrument creating the charge. Update the register of beneficial owners, make any filing the BCA requires of the registered agent, and keep the minutes, resolutions and underlying records at the registered agent's office or wherever else the directors determine.

The move of the aircraft from the FAA register to the Isle of Man Aircraft Registry is a question of aviation law, not company law, and specialist aviation counsel must handle it. What the Company has to do is short. The board resolves to apply for de-registration and re-registration, appoints an agent or attorney for that purpose under art.19.2, and obtains the lender's consent, since no mortgagee will let its collateral move between registries without first controlling the perfection of its security on the new one.

One last commercial point the board should not skip past. A company with two shares in issue is taking on $5 million of debt and an asset that will cost real money to insure, crew, hangar and maintain. If Mr AB is to fund the shortfall or the running costs, that funding should be documented as a shareholder loan, evidenced by a promissory note, rather than allowed to drift through the accounts.

So the order of business is this: due diligence and source of funds; check the memorandum for any restriction on purposes; obtain Mr AB's written instruction to the nominee; a resolution of the corporate director's own board authorising its officers to act; a written ordinary resolution of the sole member giving the previous sanction art.19.3 requires for borrowing above $1 million, approving the transaction and approving the director's interest in it; written resolutions of the sole director approving the documents and authorising their execution under seal; completion against the bank's conditions precedent; and then the register of charges, the filings, and the re-registration of the aircraft. Leave out the members' resolution and the bank will still very likely be paid. The director will not be forgiven.

1. **The trap is art.19.3 and it is the marks.** The $1m cap means an ordinary resolution of the members must be passed BEFORE the board borrows $5m. If your answer does not carry that point through both 2.1 and 2.2, it fails.
2. Aircraft registration (FAA to Isle of Man Aircraft Registry) is **not covered anywhere in the course materials**. The answer deliberately treats it as an aviation-law matter for specialist counsel, and confines itself to the company law steps (board resolution, agent/attorney under art.19.2, mortgagee's consent). Do not add invented aviation law.
3. The Company is described as having a secretary in art.21 and a registered agent (BVI). The paper does not say a secretary has been appointed. The sealing resolution names the registered agent as the second signatory, which art.22 permits ("such other person as the directors may appoint"). If you prefer, substitute the secretary, but watch arts 21.2 and 21.3, which stop the same individual acting in both capacities.
4. Whether the loan/mortgage counts as a contract "in which the director is interested" for arts 17.1(e), 17.2 and 18.2 is arguable, because the lender and the vendor are outside the group. The answer takes the cautious line (declare the interest, get members' approval anyway). That is defensible and gains marks; it is not the only defensible line.
5. Document A (the corporate director's own board resolution) is included because a corporate director can only act through its own board. Some model answers would draft only two resolutions. Keeping A costs little and shows the point.
6. The paper's articles are 1948 Table A in form while the chosen jurisdiction is the BVI. That combination is what the question invites (a BVI BC may adopt these articles), but keep the two consistent: BVI statute for capacity, registered agent, register of charges and Turquand; the paper's articles for borrowing power, quorum, written resolutions, conflicts and sealing.
