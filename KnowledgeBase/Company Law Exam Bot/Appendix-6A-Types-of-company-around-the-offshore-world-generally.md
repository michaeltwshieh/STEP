# Appendix 6A: Types of company around the offshore world, generally

## Appendix 6A:

## Types of company around the offshore world, generally

## 1. Resident companies

Resident companies (sometimes referred to as local or domestic ordinary companies, depending upon the jurisdiction and context in which the phrase is used) are usually the only type of corporate entity that is permitted to conduct business locally, within the local economy. They are required to meet local filing and regulatory requirements that are usually more onerous than those imposed on other types of company which conduct business overseas rather than locally.

## 2. Tax-exempt companies

Companies incorporated in certain low-tax Crown Dependencies were able to relinquish their local resident status and instead apply to become exempt from tax in the local centre. When a company receives its new tax status, it is generally referred to as a tax-exempt company. These tax-exempt companies were abolished when low-tax jurisdictions introduced a zero rate of corporate tax - see Appendix 4A in respect of Jersey, Guernsey, Isle of Man and Gibraltar.

## 3. International companies

These should not be confused with IBCs, despite the use of the name 'International' in the title. International companies were an extension of the principle of the tax-exempt company and were available in Alderney, Gibraltar, Guernsey, Jersey and the Isle of Man. They were similar in nature to tax-exempt companies in that a locally incorporated company was able to relinquish its resident status in favour of an alternative tax treatment. The requirements to be met in order for a company to obtain international status were similar to those that apply to exempt companies.

The essential difference between tax-exempt and international companies was in relation to the amount of tax that an international company could elect to pay. Instead of paying a nominal amount of duty, the extent of which usually equates to the amount of the exempt company tax, an international company may instead elect to pay tax locally at a fixed rate, usually somewhere between 0.5% and 35%.

Why would an offshore company voluntarily elect to pay tax? And, if it could choose the rate of tax, what rate should it choose?

The answer to these questions are found in the complex world of double tax treaties. Dividends paid by international companies are generally deemed to have been made after the deduction of the standard rate of corporate tax that applies in the centre concerned. In addition, international companies are not required to withhold tax from distributions made to non-residents. An onshore taxpayer can, in these circumstances, take advantage of the tax credit under model double-taxation treaties.

These companies have been abolished following OECD and EU pressure - see Appendix 4A.

## 4. Non-resident companies

In recent years a few offshore centres have offered what have been termed non resident companies . These were companies incorporated locally but that, rather than take the residence of their place of incorporation, applied to be treated as non resident in the centre concerned.

In the offshore financial centres where this type of company was initially offered (the Isle of Man and Ireland are the most commonly quoted examples), non-resident companies were not assessed to tax on their income and profits, unless received from local sources (bank interest excepted). In return for this favourable treatment, a nonresident company was required to pay a nominal annual duty to the local revenue authorities, although the duty is usually at a rate higher than the duty payable by tax-exempt companies in the same centre. The key feature of a non-resident company was that management and control had to take place outside the offshore centre. This usually meant that local directors were not appointed and, instead, the client, or nominees of the client, would manage the company. The knock-on effect of this was that often the affairs of a non-resident company would not be known to the service provider and there would be no one locally who could be held accountable for the actions of the company.

This, of course, developed into a regulator's nightmare as, perhaps not unexpectedly, incidents began to materialise where a non-resident company was involved in fraudulent or criminal use. In the Isle of Man and Ireland non-resident companies threatened the reputation of those centres and steps were taken to reduce, and eventually outlaw, the use of this type of vehicle.

The Manx and Irish non-resident company should not be confused with the Caymanian variety. The Cayman ordinary non-resident company is a private limited company but one that cannot carry on business within the Cayman Islands and is therefore not subject to local shareholding requirements. It is nevertheless managed and controlled within the Cayman Islands.

## 5. International Business Companies ('IBCs')

The IBC is very common in offshore financial centres located in the Caribbean and other 'nil-tax' centres. Examples of the centres that offer IBCs include Antigua, Anguilla, the Bahamas, Barbados, Belize, British Virgin Islands (now called BVI BCs), Niue, St. Vincent and the Seychelles.

In some centres, such as the Cook Islands, Mauritius and Samoa, corporate vehicles that have characteristics similar to those of the IBCs are known as International Companies. In Bermuda, the Cayman Islands and Turks and Caicos Islands there is a similar vehicle known as an exempted company. In this context 'exempted' refers to the fact that they are exempt from the requirement to be majority owned by Caymanian citizens.

The following are features of these vehicles.

- Single and corporate directors are allowed.
- Single members are allowed, subscribers do not have to take up any shares, and shareholder resolutions are passed by majority vote.
- A company secretary is not required (but if one is voluntarily appointed it may be a corporate secretary).
- Directors need not be resident in the offshore centre of incorporation and there are no rules regarding frequency and location of director's meetings.
- Annual general meetings of shareholders are not required, nor are accounts required to be laid before the members at an AGM.
- An annual return is not required, but an annual duty is payable to the Registrar of Companies.
- A registered agent is required, that is, an agent who must reside in the jurisdiction of incorporation and who is responsible for ensuring that the local company law requirements are adhered to.
- Limited information is required by the Registrar of Companies (memorandum and articles upon incorporation together with the registered office and record of shareholding). Even less information is public.
- They are not permitted to trade in the offshore centre, although this prohibition is being relaxed under OECD pressure - see Module 3, section 3.2.4.
- IBCs enjoy modern flexible capital maintenance provisions that enable the company to distribute capital surplus (what the British call share premiums).

## 6. Limited Duration Companies ('LDCs')

This type of company had its origins in the company laws of the US. The Internal Revenue Service has issued favourable tax rulings stating that an LDC should be treated as a partnership for tax purposes. This provides tax planning opportunities for US citizens and corporations.

An LDC enjoys the usual characteristics of a registered company but has a limited duration, generally 30 years or until the completion of a specified task or activity set out in the company's articles of association.

It is usual for the members of an LDC to manage the affairs of the company, and any income or realised gains are assessed on the members, rather than on the company itself. This transparency for tax purposes can create a number of possible financial planning opportunities as, unlike a partnership, an LDC is a separate legal entity with limited liability for its members. However, as the gains and income are assessed on the individual members, their personal reliefs and allowances, which might otherwise have gone unused, are available to set off against the taxes payable.

## 7. Companies limited by guarantee

A guarantee company is one that does not have share capital. Instead, persons are elected to membership and are asked to contribute to the assets of the company in the event that the company is wound up. The members pay an entry subscription that comprises the initial capital of the company and may be asked to pay further subscriptions if required in the future. As indicated in Module 3, this type of company is often used for undertakings that do not expect to distribute any profits. They are available in most offshore jurisdictions.

## 8. Hybrid companies

A hybrid company is an extension of the guarantee company, as it is one that is limited by both shares and guarantee.

The hybrid has a board of directors and members. However, the members are required to contribute to the capital of the company and, in return, they acquire rights that are pro rata to the contributions they have made. They are issued with shares but are required to contribute further funds should the company go into liquidation as a result of insolvency.

In recent years, hybrid companies have increased in popularity particularly as a form of a 'corporate trust' or quasi foundation. This particular use of a hybrid company appeals to clients from civil law countries, where the trust concept is not widely accepted but the use of companies is well known and understood.

The following is an outline of how a hybrid company may be structured so as to take on some of the features of a trust.

- First, the articles of association are drafted in such a way that the management and control of the company rests with the directors as well as those members who have voting shares. Neither the directors nor the shareholders have the right to receive distributions from the company, although the shareholders have wide voting powers.
- Secondly, the rights of the guarantee members are such that, under the articles, they are entitled to receive dividends and loan payments from the company (provided, of course, that dividends are declared or loans agreed). The guarantee members, however, have limited voting rights, if any at all.

By structuring the company in this manner, the directors and voting shareholders have roles that are similar in nature to those of trustees of a trust. Similarly, the guarantee members occupy a position similar to that of the beneficiaries of a discretionary trust, as they receive benefits whenever the directors exercise their dispositive powers in their favour. The similarity to a trust can be extended further if the articles provide for the appointment of a person whose role is to oversee the actions of the directors. This person acts in a similar way to a protector and the articles should specify whether he owes fiduciary duties or not.

Although this type of arrangement assumes many of the key features of a trust, it should be remembered that the hybrid is nevertheless a company and therefore enjoys the special characteristics of a company, such as perpetual succession, separate personality and, of course, limited liability.

## 9. Protected cell companies ('PCCs')

This type of company is a relatively new concept, having developed in Guernsey under the provisions of the Protected Cell Companies Ordinance 1997 in response to the requirements of the local mutual fund industry and certain tax planning opportunities. They are now also available in other jurisdictions. 26

PCCs have many of the usual features and characteristics of a limited company but the main difference is that their capital is divided up into separate parts, or cells, which are designed to segregate and protect the assets of the company from attachment by creditors and other claimants.

The theory is that, if the assets held in one cell are attached by creditors of the company, the assets in the other cells are protected because of the segregation of the capital. This can create benefits for those who create offshore mutual funds or other types of collective investment schemes, as the capital may be divided into separate cells. Each cell can then be operated as a separate sub-fund within the collective investment fund, thus enabling parts of the fund to invest in high-risk assets while preserving the protection of the other parts of the fund where increased risk is not acceptable.

These companies can also be used to avoid onshore controlled foreign company anti tax avoidance legislation (but it is impossible, in practice, to obtain a definitive opinion upon their effectiveness).

## 10. Companies in high-tax centres

Some companies incorporated in certain high-tax centres may also provide financial planning opportunities for non-residents. The following is a summary of the features of certain types of corporate entity that may be incorporated in Luxembourg, Switzerland and Delaware.

## 10.1 Luxembourg 1929 Holding Companies

The two most common types of corporate entity in Luxembourg are the Société Anonyme ('SA') and the Société à Responsabilité Limitée ('SARL'). However, the most common type of company for non-residents is the 1929 Holding Company, which can either be an SA or a SARL (although most are SAs).

A 1929 Holding Company must fulfil certain requirements, including a minimum paid-up capital of FLUX 1,000,000 (or the Euro equivalent). In addition, the sole object of the company must be to hold participating interests in other undertakings. Provided these requirements are met, the company is not liable to taxes in Luxembourg, nor are distributions subject to withholding taxes.

This type of company can be used for a variety of purposes, including holding investment portfolios, patents and trademarks, gold and precious metals, foreign currencies or interests in companies that own real estate.

Holding companies can also be used to act as the central holding company in a financial planning structure and a finance company for a group of companies.

## 10.2 Swiss companies

There are three main types of Swiss company that may be formed for use as a taxefficient asset-holding vehicle.

- A company that has a registered office in Switzerland but that does not conduct any commercial activities, nor have any permanent office staff in Switzerland, is classified as a domiciliary company . As such it is only liable to pay tax at the Federal level (and not at the Cantonal and Communal levels that apply to most other types of Swiss company).
- Service companies (companies with an administrative office in Switzerland that may provide a service to other companies within its group) are granted taxation benefits in so far as they are usually allocated a concessionary rate of tax payable upon 'service income' .
- Auxilliary companies are companies that have a presence in Switzerland but do not engage in commercial activities within the geographical jurisdiction of Switzerland. This type of company is also granted favourable tax benefits.

## 10.3 Delaware Limited Liability Company ('LLC')

In summary, a company is a separate legal entity, owned by its shareholders, managed by its directors, and which is liable to pay corporation tax in its state of incorporation. A US LLC is a statutory variation, first enacted in Wyoming in 1977 based upon the German GmbH. LLCs are available in a number of states in the US, but most are incorporated in the state of Delaware. An LLC is a separate legal entity offering its shareholders limited liability, but it is managed and taxed in a completely different way. There is no equivalent corporate structure in the UK or elsewhere in the offshore world. The closest comparable entity is the limited partnership.

## Management

An LLC does not have a board of directors. The company is managed by its shareholders, called members , of which there are two types: the ordinary members and the managing member. The managing member is appointed by the members and is given all powers of management of the business of the company on a day-to-day basis. The LLC has Articles of Organisation but, in addition, it is typical for the members and managing member to enter into an Operating Agreement - this is an agreement that sets out internal rules governing such matters as membership rights, management powers and duties, operating procedures, matters relating to distribution of profits, etc.

## Taxation

When formed the LLC can elect to be taxed for US tax purposes as a C Corporation (and become liable to US corporation tax upon its worldwide profits from every source) or an S Corporation (and be treated for US tax purposes as a partnership). Most holding structures elect to be taxed as an S Corporation. In these circumstances the company files a Tax Return that reports profits, but is treated as pass-through , i.e. the company is not subject to corporation tax in the US and all annual profits are attributed to the members.
