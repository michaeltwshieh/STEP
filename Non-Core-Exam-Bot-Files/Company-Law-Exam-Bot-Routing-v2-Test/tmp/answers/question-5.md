5.1

For the offshore money-box company in the question, the difficulty begins with Re Exchange Banking Co., Flitcroft's Case (1882) 21 Ch D 519. The rule is that a company may not pay a dividend, or make another distribution to its shareholders as shareholders, out of capital. Capital subscribed for shares must remain credited to the capital account. A return can normally be made only from profits, whether current trading profits or profits accumulated as retained earnings. The rule therefore separates a shareholder's claim to profit from the amount contributed for shares. The latter amount cannot be handed back under the label of a dividend. Trevor v Whitworth (1887) 12 App Cas 409 applied the same capital-maintenance reasoning to a company's acquisition of its own shares.

The rationale followed from limited liability. Once shareholders ceased to bear unlimited responsibility for company debts, the risk of business failure shifted towards creditors. Capital maintenance was the quid pro quo. Proper value had to enter the company on an issue of shares, as illustrated by the prohibition on an issue at a discount in Ooregum Gold Mining Co of India v Roper (1892) AC 125. The resulting capital fund was then meant to stay available to meet creditor claims rather than be stripped out for the shareholders' benefit. A distribution out of capital reduces the assets supporting those claims while rewarding the people whom the creditor-protection rule was meant to restrain.

Flitcroft's Case did not mean that every asset had to remain untouched. A trading company could spend cash represented by capital in carrying on its business, and a genuine profit could be distributed. Much of the practical difficulty lay in deciding what counted as profit. Ammonia Soda Co Ltd v Chamberlain (1918) 1 Ch 266 permitted a nimble dividend from the current year's trading profit despite losses in earlier years. Dimbula Valley (Ceylon) Tea Co Ltd v Laurie (1961) Ch 353 treated a realised gain on sale, and even an unrealised gain on revaluation of a capital asset, as capable of supporting a dividend. Drown v Gaumont-British Picture Corp Ltd (1937) Ch 402 allowed the premium received above the par value of shares to be treated as available under the common-law approach. Each route left the nominal capital entry intact, but each could reduce the real cushion on which creditors might have expected to rely.

Later companies legislation based on the Companies Act 1948 tightened those openings. Distributable profits were tied to accounts prepared on accepted accounting principles and audited. Prior losses had to be brought into the calculation, unrealised gains were excluded and the share premium account was treated like share capital for distribution purposes, section 56 of the Companies Act 1948 being the relevant share-premium restriction. The broad statutory formula became accumulated realised profits over the company's lifetime, less distributions already made. A further net-assets restriction prevented a dividend that would leave net assets below issued capital and undistributable reserves.

Even the tighter version achieved creditor protection only to a modest extent. Authorised capital said little about the fund actually contributed: a memorandum might state $1 million of authorised capital while the company issued only two $1 shares, leaving actual share capital of $2. There was no general minimum capital requirement. Nor did the doctrine place particular cash or property in a sealed fund for creditors. It required an accounting entry and prohibited specified returns to members, but the company could still consume the corresponding assets through honest trading losses. The rule did stop a direct distribution of subscribed capital and later legislation shut several common-law routes around it. In practice, though, it preserved a legal figure more reliably than a pool of assets, and the absence of minimum capital meant that the protected figure could be trivial from the outset.

5.2

For part 5.2(i), the chosen jurisdiction is the British Virgin Islands (BVI), applying the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 to a BVI business company throughout. The BVI route is distinct from the older Bahamian IBC capital-and-surplus model and from English-style distributable-profit rules.

(i)

The BVI legislation largely removes the Flitcroft restriction instead of trying to refine the meaning of profit. Under the BVI Business Companies Act 2004, a BVI business company need not maintain the traditional division between share capital and share premium. It may issue up to the maximum number of shares authorised, and the consideration is credited to capital without the old allocation between par capital and premium. Section 45 permits the directors to issue shares, subject to the Act and the memorandum and articles, at the time, to the persons and for the consideration and terms they determine. Section 47 allows consideration in forms including money, a promissory note, property, services already rendered or a contract for future services, although a par-value share cannot be issued below par.

For money leaving the company, section 57 replaces the declaration of a dividend from identified profits with the authorisation of a distribution. Subject to the memorandum and articles, the directors may authorise a distribution to members at the time and in the amount they think fit. The source account is no longer the controlling question. The directors must instead be satisfied on reasonable grounds that, immediately after payment, the company will pass both limbs of the solvency test in section 56: its assets will exceed its liabilities and it will be able to pay its debts as they fall due. Their resolution must record their opinion that the post-distribution test will be met.

The statutory definition also fits the money-box company more closely than a rule confined to cash dividends. A distribution includes a direct or indirect transfer of an asset to or for the benefit of a member. It is wide enough to cover cash, another corporate asset, a dividend, a redemption or acquisition of shares and the purchase of an asset for a member's benefit. In practice, the directors review the accounting records and the company's position after the proposed transfer, then approve the amount at a board meeting or by written resolution with the required solvency statement. The administrator makes the bank transfer on the nominee shareholder's direction. Creditors receive a live balance-sheet and cash-flow test at the point of payment, while the beneficial owner obtains the timing and amount flexibility described in the question.

(ii)

Administrators developed a separate financing technique which avoids treating most of the owner's contribution as protected capital at all. The nominee subscribers take only one or two shares, commonly at a par value of $1 each. The beneficial owner introduces the balance of the cash or other assets as a shareholder loan. Thus, on the course example, only $2 is share capital and the remaining $1 million is company debt. The directors acknowledge receipt of the loan and authorise a loan agreement or promissory note. The usual offshore form is interest-free and repayable on demand, although commercial interest or a fixed repayment date may be agreed.

That classification changes the capacity in which money is returned. The beneficial owner demands repayment as a creditor, in whole or by a fixed partial amount, rather than receiving a distribution as a member. The company records an on-demand loan as a current liability, or a fixed-term loan as a long-term liability, and the directors resolve that the payment is a repayment of the outstanding debt. Further contributions are added to the loan and each withdrawal reduces it, so a running account must record the balance. Repayment cannot exceed the debt or ignore the agreed terms. The method remains available for a BVI company even though sections 56 and 57 now provide a more direct distribution route.

Taken together, the Flitcroft rule offered creditors a formal capital floor, but its size and economic value could be slight. The BVI solvency test protects creditors at the moment value leaves the company, while the shareholder-loan structure keeps most initial funding outside capital maintenance from the beginning. Those two routes give the money-box company lawful flexibility, subject in each case to the post-payment solvency position or the genuine outstanding loan balance.
