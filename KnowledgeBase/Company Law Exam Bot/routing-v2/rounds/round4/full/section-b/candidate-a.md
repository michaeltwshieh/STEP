### 1.1

COMPANY LTD (Company [number])

Note: A member of the Company who is entitled to attend and vote is also entitled to appoint a proxy, who need not be a member of the Company, to attend, speak and vote instead of him.

### 1.2

The notice should be sent to C, D and E as the registered shareholders. It should also be sent to A and B because each is a director who is not stated to be a shareholder. If a person has become entitled to a share because of a death or bankruptcy, that person would also receive notice. An auditor should receive a copy if one has been appointed and the applicable requirements call for it. On the facts, no other person has a right to notice.

The notice must be in writing. Article 27.1 permits personal service, posting to the member's registered address or email to the registered email address supplied to the Company. A tracked or special-delivery service is sensible because it records dispatch and delivery, but the article's requirements must still be met. Email is deemed served on sending. A posted notice is deemed served on the fourth day after it is properly addressed, prepaid and posted.

Article 11.1 requires at least 14 clear days. Neither the service day nor 28 August is counted. For a personal delivery or email, service should therefore take place by 13 August [year], leaving 14 to 27 August as the clear days. For post, the letter should be posted by 9 August [year] so that the deemed service date is 13 August. The notice should also draw attention to the proxy procedure. A proxy instrument and any supporting authority must reach the registered office at least 48 hours before the meeting or the proxy will not be valid.

### 1.3

The starting position is that failure to give proper notice to a person entitled to it can invalidate the proceedings. Article 11.3 qualifies that result: an accidental omission to give notice, or a member's non-receipt of a notice, does not invalidate the meeting. A member who attends in person or by proxy will ordinarily be treated as having received valid notice. The omission must therefore be genuinely accidental; deliberately leaving C, D, E, A or B out would put the proceedings at risk. The members may also agree to shorter notice, but Article 11.2 requires the consent of all members entitled to receive notice.

### 1.4

At the appointed meeting C is only one person present. Article 12.2 requires two shareholders personally present unless the Company has a sole shareholder. There is therefore no quorum and no resolution can validly be passed at that meeting.

Assuming this was the directors' meeting convened under Article 10.4 rather than a meeting requisitioned by members, Article 12.3 requires the meeting to stand adjourned to the same day in the following week, at the same time and place. If it was a members' requisitioned meeting, it would instead be dissolved. At the adjourned meeting, if no second shareholder attends within half an hour, the members present constitute the quorum. C could then vote. His 70 per cent holding is above the two-thirds super-majority stated in Article 1, so, on the facts given, he could pass the special resolution at that adjourned meeting. He cannot do so at the original meeting on 28 August.

### 1.5

On a show of hands each member present has one vote. C therefore has one vote in favour, while D and E have two votes against. The resolution is lost on the show of hands, even though C holds 70 per cent of the issued share capital.

C should demand a poll before, or at the latest when, the chairman declares the result. Article 12.6 permits one member holding at least 15 per cent of the paid-up capital to demand a poll, and C holds 70 per cent. On the poll each member has one vote for each share held. C would have 70 per cent of the votes and D and E together 30 per cent. C would therefore obtain the two-thirds majority required by Article 1 and the special resolution would pass. If no poll is demanded in time, the chairman's declaration and the corresponding entry in the minute book are conclusive evidence of the show-of-hands result under Article 12.7.

The result shows why Article 12.6 matters in a company where a two-member majority on a show of hands can oppose a much larger shareholding.

## Question 3

The jurisdiction selected for this answer is the British Virgin Islands. Company is treated as a BVI business company with the articles attached to the examination. International Trust Company is treated as the registered shareholder holding the shares under the Redhouse Trust, while Ms Avril Nath and the trust officer are the two directors.

### 3.1

Company shows the usual split in a compact form. International Trust Company holds the shares and therefore owns the membership interest in Company. The board, consisting of Ms Nath and the trust officer, manages the business and decides how Company's assets and Midcity Bank account are dealt with. The members supervise the board through the powers reserved to them by the Companies Legislation and the articles, including the power to change the constitution and to appoint or remove directors. Depending on the articles, they may also approve accounts, auditors, dividends or a voluntary winding-up. The board's collective decisions bind Company in its dealings with outsiders.

The BVI statutory formulation places the management of the business and affairs of a company in, or under the direction or supervision of, its directors. Article 19.1 of the attached articles follows the older Table A model: the directors may exercise Company's powers except powers which the Companies Legislation or the articles reserve to a general meeting. The board is therefore the operating decision-maker, while the shareholder body supplies capital and exercises constitutional and supervisory control.

The arrangement arose in nineteenth-century company practice because a company brought together two groups with different functions. Entrepreneurs had the time and skill to run the undertaking. Capitalists supplied funds but, particularly in a large public company, were too numerous and dispersed to manage its daily affairs. Limited liability made the investment attractive because a shareholder could lose the investment without assuming the Company's debts personally. Transferable shares also gave an unhappy investor an exit by sale rather than a right to take over management. Directors could provide continuity when shareholders changed, and shareholders could hold them to account by controlling appointments and removals. Those four pressures, expertise, capital, continuity and limited liability, explain why ownership and management came to be separated.

The same model remains useful even though Company is an underlying asset-holding vehicle. The trust officer and Ms Nath can make informed board decisions about assets held through Company, whereas International Trust Company can use its shareholder vote to change the board or the articles. That is the practical division of power inherited from the traditional corporate model.

### 3.2

Article 19.1 makes the division concrete. It gives the directors the management of Company's business and the residual power to exercise Company's powers, subject to a power being reserved expressly to a general meeting by the Companies Legislation or the articles. The day-to-day handling of the assets and the Midcity Bank account consequently belongs to the board. International Trust Company's status as shareholder does not by itself give it title to those corporate assets or a right to give the board binding instructions about each transaction.

This wording is based on Article 80 of the Table A model under the Companies Act 1948. In Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon (1909), a similar management provision meant that shareholders could not usurp a power placed in the directors by the articles by passing an instruction at a general meeting. A shareholder who wishes to change that allocation must change the articles. The position differs from Article 70 of the Companies Act 1985 and the 2008 English model, which allow directions by special resolution. Article 19.1 contains no such reserved power for an ad hoc shareholder direction.

The qualification matters. International Trust Company can act as member on matters that Article 19.1 leaves to the general meeting, such as an alteration to the articles or the appointment and removal of directors. It can also use a valid constitutional alteration to move the boundary between the two organs. Until that occurs, a recommendation from the trustee may be considered by the board, but it is not a board resolution and does not replace the directors' own judgment.

### 3.3

The trust structure changes who holds the shares, not who owns Company's assets in law. International Trust Company has legal title to the shares as trustee. Company remains a separate legal person holding the worldwide assets and the Midcity Bank account. The trust beneficiaries therefore cannot treat those assets as personal property, and International Trust Company's shareholder title does not turn the bank account into a trust account operated outside the board's authority.

The trustee can exercise the rights attached to the shares. It can vote on matters reserved to members, replace the board and, subject to the required procedure, alter the articles. It can make recommendations about investments or distributions. Article 19.1 prevents it from converting those shareholder rights into routine management instructions to the two directors. A different result could follow if the members first amended the articles to a form that permits binding directions by special resolution, but that constitutional step is absent here.

The trust officer's appointment as a director creates a separate obligation. The officer and Ms Nath owe fiduciary duties and duties of care and skill to Company. There is no legal category of a nominee director who can avoid those duties by saying that the trustee gave the instruction. If the trustee asks the board to move funds from Midcity Bank, sell an asset or enter a transaction, the directors must understand the proposal, consider its effect on Company and decide whether it is in Company's interests. They can rely on proper information and professional advice where the circumstances justify it, but a direction from the trustee is not a substitute for inquiry.

The control risk is particularly marked in an asset-holding structure. If the trustee or a beneficiary operates the bank account, gives a general power of attorney or makes all high-level decisions from another state, the place of Company's management and control may be challenged for tax purposes. The service provider and its directors may also face liability if the control arrangement causes loss or permits unlawful use of corporate funds. A narrow, written special power for an identified transaction is safer than a general power of attorney. The board should retain control of the account, keep minutes and records, and refuse an instruction that is unlawful or that the directors cannot support as being in Company's interests. Shareholder approval can release liability for some otherwise ratifiable acts, but it does not turn a dishonest or fraudulent diversion of Company's property into a proper act.

The structure therefore leaves the trustee with real shareholder influence, while the board retains the legal management function. That separation protects the corporate form and gives the directors a workable basis for declining a request that conflicts with Company's interests.

Article 19.1 is demanding for a trustee that expects direct control, but it is the provision that keeps the trust's shareholding from swallowing the Company's separate decision-making organs.

### 3.4

Article 15.3 permits Company to remove a director before the end of the director's period in office by ordinary resolution, notwithstanding anything in the articles or in an agreement between Company and the director. No reason or finding of fault is required. If International Trust Company is, as the facts indicate, the only registered shareholder, it can use a written resolution under Article 12.11 signed by the member entitled to notice and to vote. If there is more than one member, the same decision can be taken at a properly convened general meeting by the required ordinary majority.

Removal by vote is separate from an automatic vacation of office. Article 17.1 states that office is vacated in the listed cases, including bankruptcy, unsoundness of mind, resignation, holding another profitable office without the required consent and a direct or indirect interest in a Company contract. Article 17.2 preserves the office where the interest arises only because the director is a member of a corporation contracting with Company, provided the interest is declared at the first subsequent board meeting and the director does not vote on the contract or related matter. A disqualification order under applicable legislation is another possible route, but it is not the same as the member's discretionary removal under Article 15.3.

Article 15.4 allows the members, by ordinary resolution, to appoint another person in place of the director removed under Article 15.3. The replacement's retirement date is treated as if the replacement had become a director on the day the removed director was last elected. The incoming director should first give written consent to act. Company should then record the resolution, update the register of directors and make any required filing with the Registrar. A directors' service agreement may be entered into, but it does not prevent removal under Article 15.3.

The attached articles do not give the board a general power to appoint a replacement on these facts. A directorship supplied by an offshore service provider may carry a fee and contractual protection, but the office still brings the ordinary exposure of a director. The risk comes from using corporate powers, failing to supervise, allowing a conflict or continuing to incur debts when the company cannot pay them. The protection is practical as well as contractual: understand the company's documents, make a real board decision, record it and refuse an act that cannot properly be defended as being in the company's interests.

The articles are the starting point for authority. A general management article gives the board power to run the company, often alongside particular powers to borrow, grant security, issue shares, appoint agents and operate bank accounts. The specimen Article 19.1 gives a residual power, while Article 19.3 requires previous ordinary member sanction before borrowing above the stated ceiling. If a director ignores that limit, the transaction may still bind an honest lender. Royal British Bank v Turquand (1856) protects a third party dealing in good faith against an internal irregularity, and section 31(1) of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 states the same protection in more explicit terms. That protection belongs to the outsider. It does not release the director from liability to the company for acting without authority. A director reduces the risk by checking the memorandum and articles, identifying any member approval, holding a properly constituted board meeting or signing a valid written resolution, and arranging the correct deed, signature, seal and delivery formalities.

The fiduciary duty is broader than formal authority. The director must act honestly and in what the director believes to be the best interests of the company, and must use a power for its proper purpose. Hutton v West Cork Railway Co (1883) shows why a sincere belief is not enough if company money is used for an irrational purpose unrelated to the business. Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver (1942) shows the personal cost of using a directorship to obtain an opportunity or profit that should have belonged to the company. A fee under a director's service agreement does not change that position. A director who uses an asset-holding company's funds for the beneficial owner's private expenditure, or arranges a purchase mainly to benefit an associate, has a problem even if the company later suffers no easily measured loss.

Loyalty creates separate danger points. A director must avoid self-dealing and other conflicts, must not make an unauthorised profit or take a corporate opportunity, and must not keep a gift or advantage from a third party connected with the office. Aberdeen Railway v Blaikie Bros (1854) illustrates the rule against a contract in which a director has an interest. Cook v Deeks (1916) and O'Donnell v Shanahan (2009) show the same concern where a director diverts a company opportunity. Under Article 18.2 of the specimen articles, the interested director must disclose the nature of the interest at the board meeting and must not vote on the contract or related matter. The BVI statutory disclosure rule is in section 124 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004. The director should make full disclosure, leave the quorum and vote where the governing rule requires it, and obtain approval from a disinterested board or the members before proceeding. Without that protection, the company may set the transaction aside, require the profit to be handed over or claim compensation.

Care, skill and diligence produce a different kind of exposure. The old subjective approach in Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co (1925) was tightened by Dorchester Finance Co Ltd v Stebbing (1989) and Norman v Theodore Goddard (1991): the modern comparison is with a reasonably competent director in the circumstances. Section 122 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 refers to the nature of the company, the nature of the decision and the position and responsibilities undertaken by the director. That still requires active oversight of a passive company. In Re Barings plc (No.5) (2000), delegation did not remove the need to supervise. In Bishopsgate Investment Management (In Liquidation) v Maxwell (No.2) (1994), signing documents presented by a powerful third party without asking what they did caused liability. Signing blank cheques is the same warning in Dorchester Finance.

The director may rely on an employee, another director or a professional adviser, but only within the limits stated by section 123 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004. The director must act in good faith, make the inquiry indicated by the circumstances and have no knowledge that the reliance is misplaced. A reliable accountant's routine information may be accepted; an unexplained transfer, unusual account or request from an outsider calls for questions. A director should read the facility, mortgage, account or tax document before signing it, ask for records, obtain independent legal or accounting advice where the subject is outside the director's competence, and record the reasons for the decision. An honest, informed business judgment that later loses money is not by itself a breach.

An offshore service-provider director also faces the special risk of being treated as a nominee. There is no legal office of nominee director. A director who says that the beneficial owner, trustee or relationship manager gave the instruction has not answered a claim that the director failed to act for the company. A general power of attorney or unrestricted control of the company's bank account can shift management and control to the client, create a tax-residence problem and expose the provider and directors to unlawful-use and anti-money-laundering concerns. The safer response is to keep account signing with the directors, use a narrow special power for an identified transaction if one is needed, check the client's purpose and records, and refuse a direction that is unlawful or cannot be justified as a company decision. If the pressure cannot be managed, resignation is safer than signing blindly, although resignation does not erase an accrued breach.

Insolvency adds exposure to creditors. Under the Companies Act 1948 model, fraudulent trading requires a person to be knowingly party to carrying on the company's business with intent to defraud creditors. The mental element is subjective and is often inferred where directors knowingly incur fresh debts with no realistic prospect of payment. The BVI route places fraudulent-trading liability in section 55 of the Insolvency Act 2003, applied through section 211 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004. Wrongful trading is easier to establish: under section 256 of the BVI Insolvency Act 2003, the question is whether the director knew or ought to have known that there was no prospect of avoiding an insolvent liquidation. A liquidator can investigate the period before winding-up and seek a contribution to the company's assets. Articles, an indemnity and insurance do not excuse deliberate fraud or dishonest conduct. Directors should monitor cash flow and asset values, take advice when liabilities exceed assets, stop creating new credit when the objective test is met and consider an available insolvency or winding-up process. Proper minutes matter because they show what was known and what was considered.

The company is normally the proper plaintiff for a breach of duty under Foss v Harbottle (1843), acting through the required shareholder decision. On an insolvency, the liquidator can bring the claim for the company and use any recovery for creditors. A minority shareholder may proceed in the limited fraud exception, as the course cases illustrate. This makes the risk real even where a majority client appears willing to approve what the director has done.

There are several ways to reduce financial exposure after the conduct has been addressed. A director may obtain prior approval or later ratification by ordinary resolution after full disclosure. That can release liability for a disclosed conflict, an unauthorised benefit or negligent management. It cannot validate a dishonest expropriation that harms the company, minority members or creditors. The distinction matters where the beneficial owner also controls the voting shares.

In a jurisdiction that permits them, the articles may contain a narrow exoneration and indemnity. Article 29.1 of the specimen articles protects directors and officers, in the absence of bad faith, fraud or dishonesty, against loss arising from an error, oversight or mistake. Article 29.2 deals with indemnification of lawful liabilities and costs. A director's service agreement may contain similar provisions in clauses 9.1 and 9.2 of Appendix 21C, with insurance in clause 12. The course identifies the BVI statutory provisions in sections 132 and 133 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004. Those clauses do not make a director's conduct proper; they allocate the financial consequences within the limits of the law.

The company management agreement supplies a further, narrower layer. Clauses 7.1 and 7.2 of Appendix 22 deal principally with the administrator's exclusion and the client's indemnity for third-party claims. They do not automatically exonerate each director or prevent a third party from suing. Rae v Meek (1889), West v Lazard Brothers (1993) and Roywest Trust Corp (Bahamas) Ltd v Savannah NV (1987) show why an exclusion must be confined to the permitted error or negligence, drawn clearly to the client's attention and accepted with knowledge of its effect. Directors' and officers' insurance can cover negligent or inadvertent liability where the policy allows it. It will not pay for fraud, dishonesty or deliberate default, and the policy wording, limits and exclusions must be read before reliance is placed on it.

The better protection is a defensible decision trail. A director who checks authority, discloses an interest, asks the questions indicated by the accounts and declines an unlawful client instruction has reduced the chance of a claim before the indemnity or policy is needed. Contractual protection and insurance can meet some resulting costs, but the director remains exposed where the conduct was dishonest, wilful or outside the protection permitted by the chosen jurisdiction.

### 5.1

A members' voluntary winding-up is intended for a solvent company. The directors first review the finances and sign a declaration of solvency stating that the company can pay its debts in full. The members then pass the required special resolution to wind up the company and normally appoint the liquidator in the same resolution. The liquidator takes control, pays every debt, prepares the final account, presents it at the final meeting and files it with the Registrar before dissolution. A members' voluntary winding-up therefore leaves a surplus for members only after all creditors have been paid.

A creditors' voluntary winding-up is the route for an insolvent company. The directors cannot honestly sign the declaration of solvency. They convene the members to pass the winding-up resolution, file and publish it as required, and then convene the creditors. The directors present the company's assets and liabilities and the creditors choose the liquidator by a majority of those present in person or by proxy. The liquidator then collects and sells the assets, pays creditors in the statutory order, holds the later members' and creditors' meetings and files the final account.

Company is insolvent and Blackacre is subject to a fixed legal charge of $2.4 million but is worth only $1.7 million. That fact alone makes a declaration that all debts will be paid in full untenable. The appropriate process is therefore a creditors' voluntary winding-up. It permits the creditors, whose recovery is at stake, to participate in choosing the liquidator and in the later conduct of the liquidation. A compulsory winding-up remains a possible creditor-driven alternative, but it is not needed merely because Company is insolvent when the voluntary creditors' route is available.

The fixed-charge rule is hard on the unsecured creditors here: Mortgagee takes the whole $1.7 million realisation and its $700,000 deficiency then shares with the other ordinary claims.
