The chosen jurisdiction is the British Virgin Islands. The proposed company would be incorporated as a BVI Business Company under the BVI Business Companies Act 2004, and the BVI position is applied throughout this response.

1. Name

None of the three names should be treated as approved. Each must first be searched and reserved with the BVI Registrar, since it cannot duplicate or be confusingly similar to an existing name. "SA" is an acceptable limited-liability ending in principle, but the substance of each name presents a problem. "Royal Offshore Investments SA" uses the controlled words "Royal" and "Investments"; "Royal Banking Services SA" also uses "Royal" and suggests regulated banking business; and "Offshore Investments SA" uses "Investments". The second choice is the weakest because nothing supplied shows that the company will be licensed to conduct banking business. The banker should either provide the regulatory consents needed for the controlled words or propose alternatives which do not use them. No stationery, seal or incorporation document should be prepared until a name is approved.

2. Type of company

A BVI Business Company limited by shares is recommended. Mr X wants a profit-making investment-holding vehicle, for which shares provide ownership, voting and distribution rights while limiting his liability to any unpaid issue price. A guarantee company is ordinarily used where profits are retained for a non-profit purpose and there is no share ownership, which does not fit these instructions. The BVI no longer separates an offshore IBC from a local company: the single BVI BC form can hold foreign investments or conduct business, subject to any separate regulatory restriction.

3. Capital

The reference to "authorised capital" should be recast for a BVI BC. Its memorandum need not state an authorised dollar capital, par value or currency; it may state a maximum number of shares, or permit an unrestricted number. The cost-sensitive answer in the course materials is 50,000 shares, because a BVI BC authorised to issue 50,000 shares or fewer pays a $350 incorporation fee, while more than 50,000 raises that fee to $1,100. The better course is therefore to authorise 50,000 ordinary shares of no par value. An unrestricted number would cost more without any stated gain.

Only issued shares create Mr X's economic interest. Subject to confirmation of the intended funding, one ordinary no-par-value share could initially be issued for $1 to the CSP's nominee, to hold for Mr X, leaving 49,999 unissued shares available. The banker must confirm the number, class, issue price and consideration before the directors allot any further shares. The register of members records the nominee as legal holder and the beneficial-owner records must identify Mr X.

4. Shareholders and beneficial owner

"Client details on file in New York" is insufficient. The CSP itself must identify and verify Mr X and his bona fides, the source of the funds or investments, the purpose of the company and its expected activity. It should receive suitable identification and address evidence, together with Mr X's residential address, occupation, nationality, date and place of birth. His tax residence, taxpayer identification number and the appropriate FATCA and Common Reporting Standard self-certifications are also required. The New York banker's file cannot replace the CSP's own statutory due diligence.

The requested nominee shareholder can be provided after that work is complete. A written nominee agreement should record that the nominee holds the registered title for Mr X, follows lawful voting and transfer instructions and accounts to him for distributions. Mr X remains the disclosed beneficial owner for the BVI beneficial-owner records and reporting obligations. Nominee ownership offers an administrative arrangement; it does not conceal Mr X from the CSP or competent authorities.

5. Directors

One director is sufficient under section 109(4) of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004, and a single director is proportionate for this one-owner investment-holding company. Corporate directors are permitted in the BVI, so the CSP may offer a licensed corporate director if its engagement criteria, fees and risk review are met. The term "nominee director" is misleading: whoever accepts office owes the company the ordinary fiduciary duties and duty of care and cannot act as Mr X's puppet.

The banker must confirm whether the CSP is to provide the director or whether Mr X proposes another candidate. Consent, identity and eligibility evidence will be needed. Under section 113(1), the registered agent appoints the first director or directors, and the course materials state that this must occur within six months after incorporation. The articles should permit a sole director and a quorum of one if that is the chosen arrangement, with the appointment entered in the register of directors and the required filing made.

6. Secretary, registered agent and resident representative

A secretary is optional for a BVI BC. Unless Mr X wants a separate administrative officer, there is little reason to add one because the CSP can perform the corporate administration. If appointed, the secretary would maintain registers and records, arrange board and member decisions, take minutes, issue share certificates, handle correspondence and make filings within the required periods. The secretary acts under powers given by the articles or delegated by the board.

A registered agent is mandatory under section 91(1) of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004; failure to have one is an offence under section 91A. The agent must be located in the BVI and licensed there, and only a licensed registered agent can carry out the incorporation. Its name and address appear in the memorandum. It keeps specified constitutional and corporate records, receives formal service, deals with Registry filings and acts on lawful board instructions. A separate New York resident representative does not satisfy this BVI requirement.

7. Objects

An ordinary BVI BC has full capacity under section 28 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004, subject to its memorandum, so a long list of every conceivable investment activity is unnecessary. A broad clause allowing any lawful business or activity will suit an ordinary investment-holding company. A restricted-purposes company is different: section 10 requires its restricted purposes to be stated in the memorandum.

The banker must still describe what Mr X proposes to hold and do. The CSP needs to know whether the company will hold a passive portfolio, trade securities, own real estate, borrow, give security or conduct any regulated investment activity, and in which countries. Those particulars drive the AML review, economic-substance analysis and any licensing question even though the memorandum can confer broad capacity.

8. Constitution and articles

The CSP can use its standard BVI investment-holding articles as a starting point, but the BVI legislation supplies no statutory model articles. The final form should fit the answers still outstanding. In particular, it should deal with a sole director and quorum of one, issue and transfer of shares, the nominee shareholding, member and board written resolutions, distributions, execution and the powers to appoint advisers and bankers. Any special veto, pre-emption right, different share class or direction right wanted by Mr X must be stated now. On these facts, standard articles can be prepared once those choices are confirmed.

9. Registered office

The New York office cannot be the registered office of a BVI company. Every company must have its registered office in its jurisdiction of incorporation, so the CSP should provide a BVI address, normally connected with the licensed registered agent. That address provides a place for service and inspection or retention of the prescribed records. The New York office may remain a correspondence address, but nothing turns on that for the statutory BVI office.

10. Financial matters

No statutory audit is required for an ordinary BVI BC on the course materials, so the answer "No, unless necessary" can stand unless the articles, a bank, an investor or a transaction later requires one. Section 99 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 still requires records sufficient to explain the company's transactions and permit its financial position to be determined with reasonable accuracy. The CSP should agree who will keep those records. If an audit is later chosen, the members can approve it and appoint an independent auditor.

"Morgan's, New York" is not enough to appoint an investment adviser. The banker should supply its full legal name and address, regulatory status, qualifications, proposed services, fees and whether it will advise or exercise discretion. After incorporation, the board should approve the appointment and written terms. A discretionary investment manager raises a greater management and control issue than an adviser, so that distinction matters here.

The absence of a tax adviser should be reconsidered. Mr X is using a New York banker and proposes a New York adviser, while his residence and tax status remain unknown. Independent advice should cover his home jurisdiction, the proposed investments, FATCA, CRS and where management and control will be exercised. The CSP cannot give that external tax advice from the questionnaire. A 31 December financial year end is acceptable and should be fixed by the first board decision.

11. Bank accounts

Deferring the bank arrangements until after incorporation is acceptable, but an account cannot be opened on the present blanks. The board will need the bank's name, account type, currency and completed mandate, including the human signatories, signing combinations and any payment limits. The directors should select the bank and approve the mandate in writing; the bank will then conduct its own due diligence. If Mr X seeks signing power, the board should retain control, preferably by requiring a director to co-sign. A brokerage or custody account may also be needed for the proposed investments and should be separately approved.

The incorporation should therefore pause while the banker supplies Mr X's due-diligence and tax particulars, the investment activity, the initial share instructions, the director choice and revised or cleared names. The CSP can then reserve the name, prepare tailored BVI documents and complete the statutory filings. On these facts, the sound structure is a BVI BC limited by shares, authorised to issue 50,000 no-par-value shares, with one initially issued share, one director, a mandatory BVI registered agent and no secretary or auditor unless the final instructions create a reason for either appointment.
