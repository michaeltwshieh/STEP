## Examinable material

The entire syllabus is examinable and examination questions may be set on material contained in any of the course materials provided to you.

Examination questions will not be set on law that is less than six months old.

You will not be penalised for basing your answer on law that is out of date if it is presented as the current position in the course material.

Equally, you will not be penalised for referring to new legislation not covered in the course material in your examination answers.

## Examination Approach

Where the examination question relates to a legal problem a good standard approach to use is as follows.

1. State the relevant principles of law accurately.
2. Apply these principles of law to the particular legal issues raised by the question set.
3. Use relevant authorities e.g. cases or statutes to support the argument in relation to the question set (see below).
4. Make informal and critical comment on the law.
5. Ask yourself the following questions:
- Have I addressed all the issues?
- Have I given all the necessary information?
- Have I stated the law fully enough?
- Have I applied the law to the facts of the problem in this question?
- Have I dealt with all possible interpretations of the facts?

## Use of language in examinations

An examiner will  always be impressed by clear, coherent and well-written examination answers. General points that examiners look for are as follows:

## · Coherency

- Logical overall structure of the examination answer
- Coherent sentence structure (including spelling, length)
- Unambiguous meaning

## · Accuracy

Attention should be paid to particular aspects of the language used, such as:

- tenses
- form of verbs
- use of plural/singular
- punctuation
- use of a/the

## · Appropriateness

The appropriate form of language for an essay, assignment or problem is to strike a balance between  not  being  formal  and  not  being  too  informal.  Avoid  expressing your own views in terms such as 'I think that', but use expressions such as 'a better view' or 'it is suggested that'.

When asked to write a letter to a client as part of an answer to a question, ensure that appropriate language as well as format is used, as a formal letter is still an important form of communication.

## Practices to avoid

The following is a list of common mistakes found in examination answers that should be avoided.

- Typing the question out, or repeating the question
- Long introductions
- Irrelevance or ambiguity
- Absence of authority, too much authority or irrelevant authority
- Telling too much of the story of the cases you use for authority
- Jumping to a conclusion
- No conclusion or inappropriate conclusion
- Illogical order, repeating information
- Incoherent /lack of paragraphs or sentences
- Inaccurate grammar -splitting infinitives; mixing singular and plural
- Inappropriate tone, use of slang or jargon
- Abbreviations without a full definition

## Citation and referencing

All assessments are checked using plagiarism software that checks for text found in the STEP/CLTI course materials and elsewhere across billions of sources including Internet pages and student assessments. It also checks to see if it can detect use of artificial intelligence (AI) software. Our plagiarism software is extremely effective at identifying plagiarism and AI academic malpractice.

You should ensure that any content that is not in your own wording has been appropriately referenced, this includes any materials provided as part of the course, or examination papers you have previously submitted (self-plagiarism).  Limited marks are awarded where answers are over-reliant on the course material, and you are expected to show application of your own knowledge.

Submissions that have a significant proportion of non-original content will be investigated through our Academic Malpractice procedures and could result in a mark of 0 being achieved or a deduction made to your result.

Cases and statute should be used to support any reasoning or facts given in answer to a question. They show you are 'well read' and there will be credit for quoting the right cases or statutes, but they do not replace setting out the basic tenets of the law. Do not give chapter and verse regarding the background of the parties to the action, places, times etc. but the name of the case, (preferably) with the year of the law report, any relevant facts and the outcome.

Always relate the outcome to the point in question. Quoting from a judge's dicta,  whilst  often interesting, even amusing, is not necessary. Do not quote the same case for every point in the hope that it will be right for one of the answers.

With statutes, make sure you have the up to date position, especially where there are later statutes that are passed from time to time amending existing statutes such as the Trustee Delegation Act 1999 amending Trustee Act 1925. With statutes, it is necessary to quote the section, statute name in full and the year of the Act, e.g. section 31 Trustee Act 1925.

You do not need to repeat the Act when referring to sections if it is clear from the context or by the use of standard abbreviations, e.g. IHTA 1984, LPA 1925 etc.

## Latin terms

Only use Latin terms where there is no real alternative as they are a 'proper' name or they convey a special meaning, e.g. donatio mortis causa , sui juris .
