## 5.1 - 9.5/10

### Marks gained

**Core rule: full credit.** The answer clearly states that a company cannot pay a dividend or make another distribution to shareholders as shareholders out of capital, and that distributions are normally confined to profits. The distinction between a shareholder's claim to profit and the amount subscribed for shares is direct and responsive to the question. The formulation that subscribed capital cannot simply be returned under the label of a dividend captures the practical effect of *Re Flitcroft's Case*.

**Share premium: full substantive credit for a more nuanced account than the KAP.** The answer explains the common-law opening in *Drown v Gaumont-British Picture Corp Ltd* and then the statutory treatment of the share premium account as capital for distribution purposes. That is supported by Module 6, which itself contrasts the common-law position with the later statutory restriction. The answer should not be penalised merely because the KAP compresses the history into the proposition that share premium is treated as capital. The response ultimately gives the required proposition and explains how it developed.

**Rationale: full credit.** The answer squarely links the doctrine to limited liability and the transfer of insolvency risk from shareholders to creditors. Describing capital maintenance as the quid pro quo for limited liability closely matches the course's rationale. It also explains the mechanism: value must be introduced when shares are issued and the capital entry must not then be stripped out for members' benefit. The discussion of *Ooregum Gold Mining Co of India v Roper* usefully ties the issue-at-a-discount rule to the same creditor-protection purpose.

**Practical failure: almost full credit.** The response identifies all of the important weaknesses:

- authorised capital could be large while issued capital was only two $1 shares;
- there was no meaningful general minimum capital floor;
- the doctrine did not ring-fence particular cash or property;
- assets corresponding to the capital entry could be consumed through ordinary, honest trading losses; and
- the doctrine therefore preserved an accounting figure more reliably than an asset pool available to creditors.

This is a particularly effective answer to the evaluative command, “To what extent…?”. It does not merely list defects; it concludes that direct returns of subscribed capital were restrained but that creditor protection was modest in economic terms.

**Accurate extra depth, not a substitute for core marks.** *Trevor v Whitworth*, *Ooregum*, *Ammonia Soda*, *Dimbula Valley* and *Drown* are all relevant to the course's treatment of capital maintenance and distributable profits. They demonstrate strong knowledge, but the answer had already earned most available marks from the rule, rationale and practical evaluation. No separate bonus is available above 10, and the extra authorities increase examination-time cost.

### Marks lost

1. **Explicitness omission: 0.25 mark.** The answer illustrates the absence of a meaningful minimum by using two $1 shares, but it does not state as a separate proposition that there need be no minimum number of issued shares and no minimum par/nominal value per share. Those are distinct KAP points. They are strongly implied, so this is only a small loss.

2. **Legislative chronology/attribution precision: 0.25 mark.** The paragraph beginning “Later companies legislation based on the Companies Act 1948…” groups audited/GAAP accounts, cumulative realised-profit rules, exclusion of unrealised gains, the share-premium restriction and the net-assets test under a broad 1948-based description. Module 6 presents these as broad principles distilled from English companies legislation and specifically pinpoints section 56 of the 1948 Act for share premium. The answer's overall legal contrast is sound, but attributing the entire later formula and net-assets package so closely to the 1948 Act is more categorical than the course source safely supports.

### Technical-authority check

- *Re Exchange Banking Co, Flitcroft's Case* (1882) 21 Ch D 519: correctly used for the prohibition on distributions out of capital.
- *Trevor v Whitworth* (1887) 12 App Cas 409: relevant supporting capital-maintenance authority; not essential but not irrelevant.
- *Ooregum Gold Mining Co of India v Roper* [1892] AC 125: correctly used for proper value/no issue at a discount.
- *Ammonia Soda Co Ltd v Chamberlain* [1918] 1 Ch 266: correctly used for nimble dividends at common law.
- *Dimbula Valley (Ceylon) Tea Co Ltd v Laurie* [1961] Ch 353: consistent with the course treatment of realised or revaluation gains.
- *Drown v Gaumont-British Picture Corp Ltd* [1937] Ch 402: consistent with the course's common-law share-premium point.
- Companies Act 1948, section 56: correctly relevant to the share-premium restriction; the only concern is over-broad attribution of the whole later distributable-profit regime, not the section 56 citation itself.

## 5.2(i) - 5.5/6

### Marks gained

**Chosen jurisdiction and consistency: full credit.** The preliminary paragraph expressly chooses the BVI and applies the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 throughout. This complies exactly with the question's jurisdiction instruction.

**Modern BVI circumvention: full credit.** The answer correctly explains that the modern BVI legislation largely abandons the traditional source-of-profits inquiry and the old capital/share-premium allocation. It identifies the decisive point: a distribution is authorised by directors and does not need to be traced to realised distributable profits or a particular source account.

**Solvency protection: full credit.** The answer accurately gives both limbs of the statutory solvency test:

- assets must exceed liabilities; and
- the company must be able to pay debts as they fall due.

It also states the correct timing (immediately after the distribution), the reasonable-grounds requirement and the requirement for the directors' resolution to record their opinion. This is the central creditor-protection substitute for capital maintenance and is handled very well.

**Statutory mechanics and application: full credit.** Sections 56 and 57 are the key provisions and are applied correctly. The wide definition of distribution is accurately explained as covering direct or indirect transfers of assets to or for members' benefit, including cash, other property, dividends and share redemptions/acquisitions. The sequence of reviewing accounting records, passing a board or written resolution with the solvency statement, and arranging payment through the administrator is consistent with Module 10 section 5.8.

**Sections 45 and 47: accurate but low-value depth.** The discussion of issue timing, persons, consideration and the prohibition on issuing par-value shares below par is supported by Module 6's statutory extracts. It helps explain the modern capital structure, but it is not central to the requested distribution analysis and earns little once the core marks are secured.

### Marks lost

1. **Comparative substantive omission: 0.5 mark.** The answer says the BVI route is “distinct from the older Bahamian IBC capital-and-surplus model”, but it never explains that older route. A concise comparison should have stated that old BVI-IBC-derived legislation retained nominal capital and capital surplus/share premium, allowed dividends from surplus (including, where legislated, unrealised appreciation), required the nominal capital entry to remain, and subjected distributions to a statutory solvency test. The modern BVI route then goes further by abandoning source-account restrictions altogether. Because the question permitted a jurisdiction of choice and the current BVI route is fully answered, this is a limited loss rather than a major one; however, the KAP expressly identifies both reform models, so a bare label does not earn full comparative coverage.

- There is no technically dubious BVI statutory claim in the answer.
- The references to the memorandum and articles and to the directors being satisfied on reasonable grounds are important qualifications and were correctly retained.

## 5.2(ii) - 4/4

### Marks gained

**Financial structure: full credit.** The answer gives the classic offshore arrangement: nominee subscribers take only one or two nominal $1 shares, while the beneficial owner supplies the substantial balance as a shareholder loan. This directly answers what administrators do when capital is initially introduced.

**Legal character of withdrawal: full credit.** It correctly explains that repayment is made to the beneficial owner in the capacity of creditor rather than member. The repayment is therefore repayment of genuine debt under its terms, not a dividend or return of share capital.

**Documentation and accounting: full credit.** The response includes the loan agreement or promissory note, directors' acknowledgement/approval, written demand for full or partial repayment, board approval of repayment, appropriate current/long-term liability classification and maintenance of a running loan account.

**Limits and continued availability: full credit.** It accurately states that repayment cannot exceed the genuine outstanding indebtedness or disregard the agreed loan terms, and that the technique remains available even under modern BVI legislation. This is a helpful safeguard against presenting the arrangement as an unlimited label for withdrawals.

No marks are lost in this subpart. The answer is more developed than the KAP but remains relevant.
