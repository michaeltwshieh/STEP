1.2

The notice must be served on C, D and E because they are the registered members entitled to receive notice. Article 27.5 also requires notice to every director who is not a shareholder. On the facts A and B should therefore be served in their capacity as directors, unless the register shows that either is also a shareholder. The attached articles do not give an entitlement to any other person. An auditor should be included only if the applicable legislation gives an auditor a right to notice or the Company has an appointed auditor to whom notice must be given; no such fact is supplied.

Article 27.1 permits personal service, post to the registered address, or email to the registered email address. The prudent course is to send the notice by email and by recorded or special-delivery post, retaining the transmission and delivery evidence. Article 27.2 deems a posted notice served on the fourth day after posting and an email served when sent. Article 11.1 requires at least 14 clear days, excluding both the service day and 28 August. Accordingly, email or personal service should occur no later than 13 August [year], and a posted notice should be posted no later than 9 August [year] on the stated deeming rule. A shorter period is valid only if every member entitled to notice consents under Article 11.2, and the consent should be recorded with the minutes.

1.3

The ordinary rule is that failure to give proper notice to a person entitled to it invalidates the meeting's proceedings. Article 11.3 qualifies that rule by providing that an accidental omission to give notice to, or accidental non-receipt by, a member does not invalidate the proceedings. The qualification should not be treated as a licence to omit a member deliberately or to disregard a known service failure. A member who attends personally or by proxy will generally be taken to have received valid notice. The safest course is nevertheless to serve every person identified in Article 27.5 and retain proof.

1.4

The first meeting cannot transact business with only C present. Article 12.2 requires two shareholders personally present, and this is not a sole-shareholder company. Since the meeting was convened by the directors rather than on a members' requisition, Article 12.3 requires it to stand adjourned if a quorum is absent after half an hour, to the same day in the following week at the same time and place. If, at that adjourned meeting, a quorum is still absent after half an hour, the members present constitute the quorum. C could then transact the business alone.

On a show of hands C would cast the only vote. If a poll were demanded, C would have 70 votes out of the 100 shares. The statutory assumption in the question permits a lesser super-majority fixed by the articles. Article 1 defines a special resolution as requiring two thirds of the votes of shareholders entitled to vote, so C's 70 per cent exceeds that threshold. C could therefore pass the voluntary winding-up resolution at the properly adjourned meeting. C cannot avoid the initial quorum requirement by purporting to pass it at the first meeting, and C could not use the written-resolution route alone because Article 12.11 requires every shareholder entitled to notice and vote to sign.

1.5

The meeting is quorate when C, D and E attend. On a show of hands Article 13.1 gives each member present one vote. The result is therefore one vote for and two against, so the resolution is lost on the show of hands.

C should demand a poll before, or at the latest on, the chairman's declaration of that result. Article 12.6 permits one member holding at least 15 per cent of the paid-up capital to demand a poll, and C holds 70 per cent. On the poll Article 13.1 gives C 70 votes, while D and E together have 30. The resolution therefore carries by 70 per cent. On the assumption given in the question that Article 1's two-thirds super-majority is the permitted lesser threshold, it is passed as a special resolution. The chairman should record the demand and poll result in the minutes, because Article 12.8 makes the poll result the resolution of the meeting.

Question 3

The chosen jurisdiction for this answer is The Bahamas. Company Ltd is treated as a Bahamian company limited by shares governed by the attached traditional articles. International Trust Company is the registered shareholder holding the shares under the Redhouse Trust. The analysis therefore keeps the trustee's legal shareholder capacity separate from the Company's own legal personality and does not import IBC-only provisions.

3.1

The supplied Article 19.1 reflects the usual division. The board of directors manages the Company's business and may exercise the Company's powers except where the legislation or articles reserve a matter to the members. The members own the shares and exercise the reserved constitutional and supervisory powers. They appoint or remove directors, alter the constitution, decide matters that the legislation or articles reserve to a general meeting, and ordinarily approve major changes such as a voluntary winding-up. The board acts collectively, subject to the quorum and voting provisions, rather than each director being a separate owner of Company property.

That structure grew out of the nineteenth-century company used as a practical partnership between capitalists and entrepreneurs. The Joint Stock Companies Act 1844 made registration, separate personality and public share capital workable without a private Act or Royal Charter. The Limited Liability Act 1855 reduced the investor's exposure to corporate debts, while capital-maintenance and disclosure safeguards answered part of the resulting creditor risk. The Companies Act 1907 then recognised the smaller private company, for which public fundraising and the full machinery designed for a widely held company were unnecessary.

Those changes explain why ownership and management were separated. A public company could obtain capital from many investors who could sell their shares without taking over the business. Investors were not expected to have the expertise to run a railway, factory or other enterprise, and a change of membership should not interrupt the company's contracts or property. Directors therefore received the day-to-day management power, while members retained the ability to shape the board and decide the matters reserved to them. In return, directors owed the Company fiduciary duties of good faith and loyalty and a duty of care and skill. The arrangement is efficient, but its accountability depends on members using their appointment, removal and constitutional powers.

3.2

Article 19.1 gives the board a residual management power, subject only to a matter that the Companies Legislation or the articles require the Company in general meeting to exercise. It is materially the old Article 80 or 1948 Table A approach. The board therefore decides the ordinary management of Company, including dealings with its assets and bank account, while the member deals with the matters expressly reserved to it.

The article does not contain the later wording that makes the board subject to directions given by a special resolution of members. A special resolution of International Trust Company cannot therefore instruct the board to carry out a particular transaction merely because International Trust Company owns the shares. The members could alter the articles through the constitutional procedure, but that is a different exercise from giving an ad hoc direction under the existing Article 19.1. This is the point illustrated by Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon (1909): an express management power vested in directors cannot be usurped by a member resolution. The trustee can still use the powers that Article 19 and the rest of the articles reserve to the member, including appointing or removing directors, but the board remains the management organ under the articles as they stand.

3.3

The trust arrangement does not merge the Company with the Redhouse Trust. The Company is a separate legal person. Its worldwide assets and its account with Midcity Bank belong to Company, and the trustee, director and trust beneficiaries do not own those assets merely because International Trust Company owns the shares. The result is the same distinction recognised in Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd (1897) and in Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd (1925): the company may own property and operate a bank account in its own name, while a shareholder owns shares rather than the underlying property.

International Trust Company is nevertheless the registered member. It can exercise the member's voting rights and, through those rights, appoint or remove directors and alter the articles where the required resolution is obtained. The trust instrument may also give the trustee objectives or powers in relation to the shares. Those are sources of practical influence. They do not turn the trustee into the board or displace Article 19.1. If the trustee wants Company to buy, sell or encumber an asset, it may recommend the course and ask the member to use a reserved power, but the directors must decide a management transaction themselves unless the articles are first changed.

The position is particularly important because the question places a trust officer on the board. There is no separate legal category of nominee director. The co-directors owe fiduciary and care duties to Company, and the fact that one director also owes duties as a trust officer is no defence to following an instruction that is contrary to Company's interests. A director must consider the Company's assets, the Midcity account and the foreseeable consequences of a proposed transaction, record the decision and supervise any delegation. If the trust duties and Company duties create a conflict, the conflict must be disclosed and dealt with under the articles and applicable law; the course materials do not provide the detailed trust-law rules for resolving such a conflict.

The trustee should not be given an unrestricted general power of attorney or sole control of the bank account merely to make the structure convenient. The course materials identify that arrangement as capable of shifting management and control to the client, exposing the directors to loss for failing to safeguard Company assets and creating tax and anti-money-laundering concerns. A defined, transaction-specific authority with a time limit, or joint signing with directors, is safer. The board should retain real oversight of Company even though the underlying purpose is passive asset holding.

3.4

Article 15.3 permits the Company to remove a director before the end of the director's period of office by ordinary resolution, notwithstanding any agreement between the Company and that director. No additional ground for a formal removal is stated in the attached articles. As the registered member, International Trust Company may pass that ordinary resolution at a properly convened general meeting or, because it is the sole member on the facts, by the written-resolution procedure in Article 12.11. The notice, quorum and voting rules must be observed if a meeting is used. Any separate contractual issue under a director's service agreement does not remove the Company's article 15.3 power, although its precise consequences would depend on the agreement and applicable law.

This formal removal should be distinguished from an office being vacated under Article 17.1. Resignation by written notice, bankruptcy, unsound mind, or an unauthorised office of profit can vacate the office. A direct or indirect interest in a Company contract can also do so, subject to Article 17.2 where the interest was declared at the first subsequent board meeting and the director does not vote. Those events are not substitutes for the ordinary-resolution removal route where the member wishes to remove a director.

Article 15.4 allows the members by ordinary resolution to appoint a person in place of the removed director. The proposed incoming director should first give written consent and confirm eligibility. If one of the two directors is removed, the remaining director is below the ordinary two-person quorum in Article 20.4. Article 20.8 nevertheless permits the continuing director to act for the limited purpose of increasing the board to the required quorum or summoning a general meeting, so the continuing director should not use that provision to conduct unrelated business. International Trust Company can pass the replacement resolution, and the Company should then update the register of directors and make any required Registrar notification. A director's service agreement may be entered into after the appointment, but it cannot replace the consent, resolution or record updates.

Question 4

A directorship exposes a person to risk because the office combines authority over Company property with personal duties to the Company. The risk is present even in a passive offshore company. A director who signs a document for a bank, approves a transfer from a company account or accepts the beneficial owner's instruction must be able to show what power was used, why the decision benefited the Company and what checks were made.

The first problem is authority and internal procedure. Articles commonly give the board power to manage, borrow, mortgage assets, appoint agents and execute documents, but they can reserve a transaction to the members or impose a borrowing limit. If a director acts outside those limits, the Company may nevertheless be bound to an honest outsider under the rule in Royal British Bank v Turquand (1856), and in jurisdictions that codify it under section 40 of the Companies Act 2006 or equivalent legislation. That protection is for the outsider. It does not release the director from internal liability for using the power improperly or ignoring a required member sanction. Reading the articles, obtaining the right board or member resolution, using the correct signatory and keeping minutes are therefore risk controls, not clerical extras.

The central fiduciary risk is using the office for something other than the Company's interests. The director must act honestly, in good faith and for a proper purpose, and must remain loyal to Company. Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver (1942) shows the no-profit rule: a profit obtained through a corporate opportunity had to be accounted for when it should have belonged to the company. Lee Panavision Ltd v Lee Lighting Ltd (1992) illustrates the danger of arranging Company business on terms that favour an associated company. Re Lee, Behrens and Co Ltd (1932) shows why even a benevolent disposal of Company assets needs a genuine belief that it promotes the Company's prosperity. Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi (2005) adds the risk of diverting a customer to the director personally.

Conflicts and self-dealing require particular care. A director who buys from, sells to or otherwise contracts with the Company may be required to make full disclosure and obtain informed approval. An interested director should not vote or count in a disinterested quorum where the governing rule excludes him. Failure can lead to the transaction being set aside, an account of profit and an order to indemnify Company for loss. The no-benefits rule also catches advantages received from third parties because of the office. The practical protection is a written disclosure made before the decision, independent consideration by the unconflicted directors or members, and an accurate minute explaining the approval route.

The duty of care and skill creates a different exposure. Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co (1925) used a more forgiving, subjective standard, but Dorchester Finance Co Ltd v Stebbing (1989) and Norman v Theodore Goddard (1991) reflect the modern expectation of an ordinarily competent director. Re Barings plc (No.5) (2000) is a useful warning for a board that delegates trading or administration: delegation is permitted, but a director must inform himself about Company affairs and maintain monitoring. Lexi Holdings plc v Luqman (2009) shows that suspicious records and a family relationship can make reliance unreasonable, while Madoff Securities International Ltd v Raven (2013) shows that reliance can be justified for some directors where the circumstances and the expert advice support it. Bishopsgate Investment Management (In Liquidation) v Maxwell (1994) makes the point sharply: routinely signing documents presented by an influential third party without asking what they do can make the director liable for the resulting loss.

This is why the label nominee director is dangerous. There is no legal office in which a service-provider director can simply obey the beneficial owner. A client may recommend a transaction, but the director must consider the Company's records, assets, solvency and foreseeable consequences. The same risk arises where a client receives a general power of attorney or unrestricted control of the bank account. It can transfer practical management and control to the client, create tax and anti-money-laundering concerns, and leave the director responsible for losses caused by failure to safeguard corporate property. A narrow special power, joint bank signing and real board supervision reduce that exposure.

Insolvency adds personal risk to the ordinary duty owed to Company. A liquidator investigates the period before liquidation and may claim compensation for mismanagement. Section 332 of the Companies Act 1948 permits a court contribution order where persons knowingly carried on business with intent to defraud creditors. Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 goes further in England by imposing wrongful-trading liability where a director knew or ought to have known that insolvent liquidation could not be avoided. The materials record that the offshore position varies: fraudulent-trading provisions apply in The Bahamas, Bermuda, the BVI and other centres, while wrongful-trading liability exists in the BVI, Guernsey, Jersey and some other jurisdictions but not for a Bahamian IBC. The director must therefore check the incorporation statute as soon as the accounts show financial distress, stop incurring unjustified debts and consider a creditors' winding-up or other available insolvency process.

There are meaningful protections, but each has boundaries. A director can seek prior approval or later ratification by ordinary resolution after full disclosure. That can release liability for an interested transaction, a benefit or negligent management, but the non-ratifiable breach doctrine prevents majority directors from approving their own dishonest expropriation of Company assets. A properly drawn article or director's service agreement may exonerate negligent default and indemnify liabilities to third parties in jurisdictions that permit it. Article 29.1 and 29.2 of the attached traditional model, section 58 of the Bahamas International Business Companies Act 2000 and section 132 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 illustrate the offshore approach, subject to the express exclusion of fraud, dishonesty and wilful default. Section 232 of the Companies Act 2006 and the legislation of some other jurisdictions take the opposite view, so the local rule must be checked before relying on the clause.

A management agreement can give an indemnity from the beneficial owner, and the director's service agreement can contain exoneration, indemnity and insurance provisions. Rae v Meek (1889), followed in West v Lazard Brothers (1993) and Roywest Trust Corp (Bahamas) Ltd v Savannah NV (1987), supports the need for a clause drawn narrowly enough to cover negligence rather than every form of liability and brought specifically to the client's attention. The indemnity may reimburse the service provider, but it does not prevent a creditor or other third party suing the director. Directors' and officers' insurance is another sensible layer, but cover normally depends on good faith and excludes deliberate default, fraud and dishonesty.

The best reduction of exposure is disciplined independence: read the governing articles, obtain the required approval, disclose interests, ask questions where records or instructions are suspicious, take professional advice, supervise delegates and maintain complete minutes and accounting records. Re Barings is the critical reminder that a board cannot outsource oversight. Exoneration, indemnity and insurance can reduce the financial consequence of an honest mistake; a dishonest or wilfully defaulting director remains exposed. A director who combines the correct decision process with genuine attention to the Company's interests is in the strongest position the materials support.

Question 5

The chosen jurisdiction for this answer is The Bahamas. Company Ltd is treated as a Bahamian company limited by shares. The supplied course materials describe the general offshore winding-up and priority rules by reference to the Companies Act 1948 model, but they do not provide the current Bahamas section numbers for every creditors' voluntary winding-up or final-account step. The answer therefore applies that supplied model and leaves any Bahamas-specific filing form or deadline conditional.

5.1

A members' voluntary winding-up is intended for a solvent company. The directors first make a full enquiry into the Company's affairs and sign a declaration of solvency stating that the debts can be paid in full within 12 months from commencement, supported by a statement of assets and liabilities. The members then pass the required special resolution to wind up the Company and usually appoint the liquidator in the same resolution. The resolution is filed, the appointment is notified and published as required, and the liquidator takes control, collects assets, pays every debt in full, prepares the final account, lays it before the final meeting and requests dissolution. The members remain the principal decision-makers in that process.

A creditors' voluntary winding-up is used where the Company cannot pay all debts in full. The directors cannot honestly sign the declaration of solvency. They convene the members to pass the winding-up resolution, file and publish it as required, and convene a meeting of creditors. The directors present the financial position and the creditors choose the liquidator by the prescribed majority. The liquidator then takes control, realises assets, investigates the Company's affairs, pays creditors in statutory order and holds the required final meetings and filings. Creditors consequently have a much greater say than in a members' voluntary winding-up.

Company is insolvent. Its total stated assets are $2,605,000, but Blackacre is subject to a fixed legal charge securing $2,400,000 and is worth only $1,700,000. That leaves a $700,000 unsecured deficiency before the other debts are considered. The Company also has legal, accountancy, service-provider, tax, trade and shareholder-loan liabilities, with total stated liabilities of $3,375,000. A declaration of solvency would not be supportable. The appropriate process is therefore a creditors' voluntary winding-up, subject to any creditor choosing to seek a compulsory order instead.
