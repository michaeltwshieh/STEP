The jurisdiction chosen for this answer is the British Virgin Islands. International Trust Company is taken to be a BVI trust company, licensed to carry on trust business under the Banks and Trust Companies Act 1990 and to carry on company management business under the Company Management Act 1990. Company Ltd ('Company') is taken to be a BVI business company incorporated under the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 ('BCA'), which has adopted articles in the form attached to this paper. References to 'the Companies Legislation' below are references to the BCA, and all article numbers are those of the Company's articles.

**3.1**

A company is an artificial person. It cannot form an intention, sign a document or decide anything, so it borrows the minds of natural persons to act for it. Those minds are organised into two power bases: the members, who own the company and act collectively in general meeting, and the board of directors, who run it. The usual division is easily stated. Day-to-day management, and the wide commercial powers that go with it, sit with the board. A short list of reserved matters sits with the members: alterations to the constitution, alterations to the capital or to class rights, the appointment and removal of directors themselves, in many cases the approval of accounts and the declaration of a dividend, and the decision to wind the company up voluntarily. Beyond that list, the members delegate.

What matters for practice is where that division comes from. It is not, in the main, imposed by statute. Section 109 BCA provides that the business and affairs of a company are to be managed by, or under the direction or supervision of, its directors, but the real allocation is made by the articles, and the articles are the members' own document: they are settled by the subscribers on incorporation and can be rewritten later by the members alone. So the members choose how much power to give away, and can in principle take it back. The division of power is therefore self-imposed rather than legislated.

Once the members have made that choice, however, they are held to it. Where the articles commit a power to the board, the members cannot exercise it themselves, and they cannot instruct the board how to exercise it. That is the effect of *Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon* [1909] 1 Ch 311. The company's articles conferred general management on the board but provided that a resolution to acquire property was ineffective if either of two named directors dissented. The board voted four to one to buy property, the dissenting voice being one of the two named directors, and the members then met and passed a resolution telling the board to go ahead anyway. The transaction was struck down. Powers conferred on the board by the articles belong to the board, and a members' resolution cannot usurp them. If the members are unhappy, their remedy is to change the articles or change the directors, not to give orders.

Why did it develop this way? The answer lies in the shift from personal to impersonal capital. The medieval sole trader owned the business, managed it and bore its losses, and the partnership that followed only spread those functions across a handful of men who were still the same men. Capital and management sat in one pair of hands. The joint stock companies that grew out of the 16th and 17th century merchant ventures began to prise them apart, and after the collapse of the South Sea Company the Bubble Act 1720 stopped the process for over a century by outlawing the form altogether. Industrialisation forced the question back open. Railways and heavy manufacturing needed sums of capital that no individual or partnership could raise, and the Joint Stock Companies Act 1844 answered by allowing incorporation by simple registration, with a separate personality and freely transferable shares. The Limited Liability Act 1855 completed the design by capping what an investor could lose, on the reasoning that unlimited liability chilled investment and that cheap capital was worth the risk transferred to creditors.

The consequence was a new kind of owner. The Victorian shareholder was a passive supplier of money who neither wanted nor was equipped to run a railway, and the directors were the entrepreneurs who did. Four practical reasons were given for handing management to an elected board, and they still hold. The members are numerous and lack specialist knowledge, whereas directors are chosen for expertise. Separating ownership from control is what allows capital to be raised from strangers in the first place. Continuity requires that shares can change hands without the business grinding to a halt every time they do, which cannot happen if each new member expects a say in management. And limited liability, confirmed for the one-man company in *Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd* [1897] AC 22, means the member risks only the investment, so a member has little claim to run what the member does not stand behind. Article 80 of Table A to the Companies Act 1948 crystallised all this in a single sentence, and it is that sentence, in substance, which offshore drafters have copied ever since.

**3.2**

Article 19.1 is the 1948 formula. The business of the Company is to be managed by the directors, who may exercise all such powers of the Company as are not, by the Companies Legislation or the articles, required to be exercised in general meeting. Two things follow.

First, the board's authority is residual and it is general. Anything not carved out for the members belongs to the board by default, which for an asset-holding vehicle means effectively everything: opening and operating the account with Midcity Bank, buying and selling the world-wide assets, appointing agents under art.19.2, borrowing and giving security under art.19.3.

Second, and more to the point, art.19.1 is the *unqualified* version. It contains no words permitting the members to give directions by special resolution. That distinguishes it from art.70 of Table A to the Companies Act 1985 and from the current English model articles, both of which subject the board's management power to any directions given by special resolution, and so hand the members a reserved right to steer the board on an ad hoc basis. Under art.19.1 the members have no such right. *Quin & Axtens* applies with full force: International Trust Company, as sole member, cannot lawfully instruct the board to do or refrain from doing anything.

The carve-outs remain, and they should not be understated. The members alone alter the articles or the memorandum, increase or reduce capital (arts 9.1, 9.3), attach special rights to shares or vary class rights (arts 2.1, 4.1), declare a dividend on the board's recommendation (art.23.1), fix directors' remuneration (art.18.1), determine the number of directors (art.15.2), appoint and remove directors (arts 15.3, 15.4), sanction a distribution in specie on a winding-up (art.28), and, under art.19.3, must sanction by ordinary resolution any borrowing that would take the Company's outstanding borrowings above $1 million. That last point is a genuine limit on this board, not a formality, given the assets the Company holds.

Power can be shifted back to the members, but only by the routes the constitution allows. The articles may be amended by special resolution, which under the definitions article requires two thirds of the votes, and a sole member holding all the shares clears that threshold without effort. The obvious amendment would be to replace art.19.1 with the 1985 formula and reserve a power of direction. The blunter route is art.15.3. On these articles the division of power is real as a matter of law, and thin as a matter of fact.

**3.3**

Here the two organs of the Company are staffed by the same firm. International Trust Company is the sole member, holding the shares on the trusts of the Redhouse Trust, and the board consists of two of its trust officers. The formal division of power survives intact, but the independence it presupposes has to be manufactured rather than assumed.

The starting point is that there is no such thing in law as a nominee director. A director appointed by a service provider owes the company the same fiduciary duty of good faith and the same duty of care and skill as any other director, restated for BVI companies in ss.120 and 122 BCA. 'I was only doing what the trustee told me' is not a defence. The director's duty runs to the Company, not to the trustee that appointed him, and not to the beneficiaries of the Redhouse Trust. That is precisely where the conflict bites. A trust officer sitting on this board wears two hats: as officer of the trustee he is bound to act in the beneficiaries' interests, and as director he must act in what he honestly believes to be the interests of the Company. The duty of loyalty forbids a director to allow duties owed to others to conflict with the duty owed to the company. The structure builds that conflict in.

In practice the tension surfaces when the trustee wants something done. On these articles the trustee cannot direct the board, and a director who simply executes what is put in front of him because the shareholder wants it is exposed. *Bishopsgate Investment Management (In Liquidation) v Maxwell* [1994] 1 All ER 261 is the warning: the director there habitually signed documents produced by his father, who was not a director but was the prime mover behind the company, and effectively signed the company's assets away. He was liable for failing to make any inquiry at all. The lesson transfers directly. Where the trustee proposes a transaction, the board must obtain the underlying information, form its own view, and record that it did so. Section 123 BCA permits reliance on information and advice from employees and professional advisers, but only where the director acts in good faith, makes proper inquiry where the circumstances call for it, and has no reason to think the reliance unwarranted. Reliance is not abdication.

There is a further reason for the board to make its own decisions in the Islands. A company is generally taxed where its management and control are exercised, and the whole point of appointing offshore directors is to keep management and control offshore. If the board is a rubber stamp, the company risks being treated as a mere façade or challenged as a sham in the jurisdiction where the real decisions are made. That risk is at its highest where the person doing the directing is the onshore beneficial owner. It is lower here, because the trustee is itself in the Islands, but it does not disappear: pressure from a settlor or beneficiary tends to arrive through the trustee, and a board that passes it straight through to the Company's assets has abdicated in fact even if the geography looks tidy.

So what should a director do when instructed? Treat the instruction as a recommendation, which the trustee is entitled to make. Ask for the information needed to form a view. Convene the board (or pass a written resolution under art.20.15), decide, and minute the reasons under art.20.14. If the proposal is not in the Company's interests, decline it, and say so in writing. If the trustee will not accept that, the answer is resignation under art.17.1(d), not compliance. There is a legitimate safety valve, in that a director may obtain a release by making full disclosure and obtaining the sole member's prior approval or subsequent ratification, which the trustee can give by written resolution under art.12.11. It is imperfect protection. It cannot cover a dishonest or fraudulent breach, which is non-ratifiable, and it will look hollow if the trustee that approves the act is the same firm that employs the director. The exoneration and indemnity in arts 29.1 and 29.2 are similarly limited, since both stop at bad faith, fraud and wilful default.

The uncomfortable conclusion is that the division of power holds in law and bends in practice. The board is legally independent of the trustee and legally answerable to the Company. The trustee holds every share and can remove the board at will. A director who forgets the first proposition is in breach of duty; a director who forgets the second is naive.

**3.4**

Removal. The simplest and most likely route is art.15.3: the Company may by ordinary resolution remove a director before the expiry of his period of office, notwithstanding anything in any agreement between the Company and that director. No cause is required and no reasons need be given, and any protection the director thought he had in his service agreement does not preserve the office. Because International Trust Company holds all the shares, this is trivially easy. A sole shareholder is a quorum under art.12.2 and may act by written resolution under art.12.11, so removal requires a signature and nothing more. No meeting, no notice, no debate. Section 113(6) BCA reflects the position in statutory terms: a director holds office until his successor takes office or until his earlier death, resignation or removal, and s.114 BCA provides for removal.

Office may also be vacated automatically under art.17.1, without any resolution at all, if the director becomes bankrupt, is found to be or becomes of unsound mind, resigns by notice in writing to the Company, holds another office of profit under the Company without the consent of the members in general meeting, or is directly or indirectly interested in any contract with the Company or participates in the profits of any contract with the Company. That last limb deserves attention in a managed-company structure. If the Company contracts with International Trust Company (for administration or directorship fees, say), a director who is interested in that contract risks vacating office. Article 17.2 saves the director who is a *member* of the contracting corporation, provided he declared his interest at the first board meeting after becoming interested and does not vote on it, and art.18.2 permits a director to contract with the Company on disclosure and non-participation in the vote. An employee of the trustee is not obviously a member of it, so the saving in art.17.2 may not reach him. Disclosure at the earliest board meeting, followed by abstention, is the prudent course either way.

Rotation is a third route. Article 16.1 requires the whole board to retire at the first annual general meeting, and thereafter the number nearest one third to retire annually, longest-serving first (art.16.2), with lots drawn between directors appointed on the same day. With a board of two, one director retires each year. A retiring director is eligible for re-election under art.16.3, but the member is not obliged to re-elect him. Finally, a court may disqualify a rogue director, and in the Islands the regulator can achieve the same result by refusing or revoking the company management licence under which the directorship is provided.

Appointment of a replacement. Where the vacancy arises from removal, art.15.4 is directly in point: the Company may by ordinary resolution appoint another person in the place of the director removed, and the appointee inherits the removed director's position in the rotation cycle, retiring when the removed director would have retired. Again the trustee can do this by written resolution under art.12.11, and the same resolution can remove and replace in one document.

Can the board fill the vacancy itself? These articles contain no casual-vacancy power of the kind found in more modern offshore articles, where the directors may appoint a new director by board resolution. The appointment power in art.15.4 is given to the members. Article 20.8 is the only real qualification. It allows the continuing directors to act notwithstanding a vacancy, but where their number falls below the quorum, which is two under art.20.4 unless the number of directors has been fixed at one, they may act only for the purpose of increasing the number of directors up to the quorum or of summoning a general meeting, and for no other purpose. On the better reading of those words, Ms Nath, left alone, could appoint a second director to restore the quorum. She could do nothing else: she could not operate the Midcity Bank account, deal with the assets, or transact any other business until the board is reconstituted. The safer course, and the one a service provider would in fact take, is for the trustee to sign a single written resolution under arts 15.3 and 15.4 removing one director and appointing his replacement, so that the board is never inquorate. A workable alternative is for the trustee to fix the number of directors at one by ordinary resolution under art.15.2, whereupon Ms Nath becomes a quorum of one under art.20.4 and may exercise all the powers of the board by written resolution under art.20.5.

Whichever route is used, the register of directors must be updated and the change notified to the registrar.

In short: removal is at the pleasure of the trustee, exercisable in writing and without cause; vacation of office may happen automatically on the events in art.17.1; and the replacement is appointed by the trustee as sole member, the board having no power to do so beyond the narrow rescue provision in art.20.8.
