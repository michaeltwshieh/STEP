## Module 11: The Provision of Company Management Services and Beneficial Owner Control

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- describe the range of company services that are provided by corporate service providers from offshore centres
- review potential problems when the beneficial owner of a company wishes to exercise too much control.

## Introduction

In this module, after an introduction of service providers (in section 1), we review a range of services provided by corporate service providers from offshore centres (section 2). We then analyse some of the problems faced by service providers when a client wishes to be involved in the management or day-to-day activities of the managed company (section 3). The regulation of company management business is now dealt with in the STEP Advanced Certificate in Principles of International Taxation, AML and Compliance.

## 1. The nature of service providers

Company management business is performed by a variety of service providers. At one end of the scale is the independent sole proprietor who offers corporate services from a small, local office. At the other end are the large international banks and trust companies that have offices in a number of jurisdictions, employing hundreds of people to service the requirements of their high-net-worth clients. In the middle are the law firms and accountancy firms that offer company management services as an adjunct to their legal and accountancy practices.

Usually, trust companies offer company management services alongside trust services. This is certainly the case for international trust companies, whose core business has traditionally been the administration of trusts. As the sophistication of trust structures has increased and the potential benefits of using companies in financial planning schemes have become apparent, trust company personnel have had little option but to develop new skills in company administration, to keep up with client demand.

Some service providers in offshore jurisdictions provide only company management services. Trust companies, however, will not only hold a licence to carry out trust services, but also a company management licence authorising them to carry out corporate services for their high-net-worth family clients.

## 2. Range of services provided

A wide range of company services are provided from offshore centres. We shall review those that are the most common.

## 2.1 Incorporation

Service providers are often able to incorporate a company in any offshore centre. If they do not have an office or representation in the centre where incorporation is required, such service providers will generally appoint an agent to act on their behalf. In these circumstances, the fee charged to the client includes not only the fees for the main service provider but also those of the agent who is instructed to assist.

Incorporation services usually include: the preparation of the memorandum and articles of association; the provision of the subscribers; certificates of compliance; and filing the required paperwork with the registrar of companies in the jurisdiction concerned.

Precedent incorporation documents are usually held by the service provider on its computer system. This enables the paperwork to be produced quickly and efficiently. Alternatively, a service provider may hold a handful of ready-made 'shelf' companies and offer them for sale to clients as a package, without having to incorporate from scratch.

## 2.2 Registration/continuance

In addition to incorporation, a service provider often offers registration and continuation services.

Registration of a foreign company is the process whereby a company incorporated in one jurisdiction may register in another. Registration is required whenever a company wishes to establish a place of business in a new centre. Usually, the service provider must submit copies of the company's memorandum, articles and other paperwork to the registrar of companies, together with a request for registration. Once registered, the company may be deemed, for tax purposes, to be resident in the centre. The registration process is dealt with in Module 4 section 1.

Continuance, on the other hand, involves the migration of the domicile of the company from one jurisdiction to another. This process is dealt with in Module 4 section 2.

## 2.3 Provision of directors

It is a general rule of international conflict of revenue laws principles, that a company becomes liable to pay corporation taxes upon its worldwide profits to the revenue authority of the jurisdiction where it is deemed to reside for tax purposes. The residence of a company for tax purposes is determined by:

- a. the place of its incorporation; 199 or
- b. the place where its management and control are exercised. 200

The place of incorporation test is easy to apply; a company incorporated in State A is liable to pay corporation taxes on profits generated from across the globe to the revenue authority of State A. The management and control test is more difficult to apply in practice. As management and control are usually exercised by the board of directors, a company's place of residence for tax purposes is, superficially at least, the place where the directors reside and meet to transact high-level business.

Accordingly, in order to maintain the favourable tax status of a company and enhance the confidentiality of the arrangement, many service providers provide directors for managed companies incorporated on behalf of their clients. If corporate directors are permitted by the companies legislation of the jurisdiction of incorporation, the service provider will usually incorporate a subsidiary company for this purpose; otherwise, employees of the provider are appointed.

Not all service providers are prepared to offer this facility. Some do not provide directors for companies that are involved in trading activities or for which they do not maintain records and accounts. This is because the duties and responsibilities of directors carry potential liability that is borne by the service providers offering this facility.

Some clients/beneficial owners believe that where directors are provided by corporate agents, these directors act as the client's agents or nominees. There have also been occasions when service providers have described this as a 'nominee director' service. Make no mistake, there is no such thing in law as a 'nominee director' . Those who are appointed to the board of managed companies owe the same fiduciary and other duties of care and skill as their onshore counterparts. Therefore, offshore directors may be held liable for their acts and omissions as well as, in certain circumstances, the acts of the company and their client (see Module 8).

The following is a summary of some common practical issues relating to the provision of directors for companies under management.

## 2.3.1  Corporate directors

If corporate directors are permitted by the companies legislation of the jurisdiction of incorporation, it is usual for service providers to follow this route rather than to appoint their own employees as directors. This is because of the continuity that a corporate director can provide. A change to the personnel or authorised signatories of the corporate director does not affect the company on whose behalf it acts.

Deciding whom to appoint to the board of a corporate director is an important issue for the service provider, as the structure of the board may affect the status or residence for tax purpose of the underlying company. Similarly, only senior and experienced officers of the service provider should be appointed to the board because of the onerous nature of the duties that are owed by directors.

Those who are appointed as directors of a corporate director may be held liable for the actions of companies for which the corporate director acts. The directors of the corporate director owe a duty of care and act in a fiduciary capacity in relation to the corporate director itself and, in turn, owe similar duties and responsibilities to the companies for which the corporate director acts. This chain of responsibility directly links those who are appointed to the board of a corporate director to the affairs of the companies under management.

## 2.3.2  Employees

If corporate directors are not permitted (see Module 7 section 3.1), or if the service provider does not wish to use a corporate director, senior employees of the service provider are usually appointed to the boards of managed companies. These employees should be made aware of the duties and responsibilities they owe to each of the companies in which they hold office.

Service providers agree to indemnify employees who act as directors for managed companies in their personal capacity, although such an indemnity does not cover the employees against liability incurred that arises from their own deliberate default, dishonesty, fraud or bad faith. A directors' service agreement between the company and its director will also contain indemnities. In addition, with the rolling programme of global regulation, directors' and officers' insurance is not only available but, in most jurisdictions, is mandatory.

## 2.4 Provision of company secretary and other officers

If, under applicable companies legislation, a company secretary or other officer, such as a registered agent, is required, the service provider that incorporated the company typically offers to fulfil this role, either through a corporate entity or through its employees.

## 2.5 Provision of shareholders

The shares issued by a free-standing, asset-holding company (i.e., one that does not underlie a trust) may be held in the name of the client. In these circumstances, the client is known as the sole, or controlling, shareholder. More often than not, the shares of such a company are issued to the service provider, or a subsidiary. Here, the registered shareholder is known as a nominee and the firm's client is the beneficial owner of the shares.

For some companies incorporated in certain centres, the register of members is open to inspection by the public and shareholder details must also be provided to the registrar of companies. 201  The beneficial owner may, therefore, prefer to maintain an element of confidentiality and instruct the corporate agent to provide registered shareholders to act as nominee shareholders.

The relationship between the registered nominee shareholder and the beneficial owner is contractual in nature and the terms and conditions are set out in a nominee shareholder agreement, an example of which is illustrated in Appendix 27A of the online Study Resources. Alternatively, the document may be a one-party declaration, illustrated in Appendix 27B of the online Study Resources, although the critical terms and conditions remain the same. Clause (1) confirms that the service provider holds the shares registered in their/its name as a nominee or agent. Subsequent clauses confirm that the nominee will not dispose of the shares otherwise than in accordance with instructions, assigns all distributions to the beneficial owner and will vote the shares as instructed. A nominee shareholder agreement is signed by the beneficial owner or, when drafted as a declaration, is sent to the beneficial owner for information purposes.

A practice has developed for some service providers to include a provision in their nominee declaration that purports to transfer the ownership of the shares in the company on the death of the beneficial owner. It is important to note that such a provision is tantamount to a testamentary gift and must therefore comply with the statutory requirements of a valid Will. If the service provider carries out the terms of such a provision without first obtaining a grant of probate issued by a court of competent jurisdiction, there is a real risk that the service provider may be held liable for:

- intermeddling in the estate of the deceased, on the basis that the estate should be administered only by the court-appointed personal representative (executor) of the deceased, following the issue (or re-sealing) of a valid grant of probate
- committing a fraud on the lawful heirs to the estate of the deceased, on the basis that the property of the deceased should pass in accordance with the terms of the deceased's Will or according to the rules of succession in relation to personal moveable property in force in the country of the deceased's domicile or citizenship at the time of death.

To prevent these problems arising, the common practice of creating a simple estate planning trust structure has evolved whereby the shares of the company under management are owned by trustees in accordance with the terms of a standard trust instrument. In this manner, the trustee continues to hold the legal title to the company's shares (and hence the underlying assets) notwithstanding the beneficial owner's death. There is a continuity of ownership of the shares of the underlying company by the trustee. In these circumstances, it is not necessary to transfer the shares on the beneficial owner's death in accordance with the Will of the deceased. Nor is it necessary to include the value of the shares in the application for probate a procedure that is required under typical onshore regulation for the purposes of any onshore estate tax calculation.

Finally, in instances where the service provider holds shares on behalf of two or more persons jointly, it is essential for the nominee declaration to state expressly whether they are so held for the beneficial owners:

- as joint tenants (in which case the right of survivorship applies, whereby, upon the death of one co-owner, that person's beneficial ownership in the share(s) automatically vests in the surviving co-owner)
- as tenants in common (in which case, upon the death of one co-owner, that person's beneficial interest in the share(s) passes to the heirs of their estate according to their Will or the laws of succession of personal moveable property in force in the country of the deceased's domicile or citizenship at the time of death).

## 2.6 Provision of a registered office

Every company is required to have a registered office located in the jurisdiction of incorporation. The service provider usually provides this facility for managed companies.

Some clients may wish to have mail relating to the company's affairs sent to the registered office. If mail is received it is usually opened by the service provider and the appropriate action taken. Care should be exercised if the client instructs you to leave unopened mail received at the registered office, as the registered office address may, in these circumstances, be misused for fraudulent purposes.

Some clients may also require a 'hold mail' service, under which mail received for the company, or any mail addressed to the client personally, is to be held at the registered office pending collection at a later date. Although such a service is common, care should still be exercised by the service provider as this facility can also be open to misuse.

## 2.7 Provision of a registered agent

In centres that have enacted IBC legislation, such as the Bahamas, and for BVI BCs and the New Manx Vehicle, the company must have a local registered agent. The registered agent must be located in the jurisdiction of incorporation and is another service that aservice provider typically offers in such centres.

## 2.8 Maintenance of corporate records

The company legislation in most offshore centres makes the office of company secretary optional, rather than mandatory. But the usual responsibilities of a secretary still have to be fulfilled and the statutory duties imposed upon companies complied with, such as the maintenance of:

- the register of members
- the register of directors
- the register of company secretary and other officers
- the register of charges over the assets of the company
- the register of beneficial ownership, or equivalent
- the minutes of general meetings of the shareholders of the company
- the minutes of the meetings of the board of directors
- the accounting records of the company and preparation of financial statements
- the common seal (if required).

## 2.9 Corporate filing to the registrar of companies

Part of the general role of the service provider is to ensure that the company under management complies with local filing requirements and remains in good standing.

Examples of situations that may require notification to the registrar include:

- changes to the memorandum of association
- changes to the articles of association
- special resolutions passed by the members (certified copies of such resolutions must be filed)
- changes to the personal details of members of the board of directors, such as a new address, where applicable
- changes to the officers of the company, such as secretary or registered agent, and changes to the personal details of the officers, once more where applicable
- a new issue or allotment of shares
- a change of the company's registered office

In addition, an annual return will have to be filed with the registrar. 202 If a return is not submitted the company may be struck from the register of companies. A full annual return usually contains the following details (though a truncated version is permitted for exempt companies - this is essentially a declaration that the company transacts business wholly outside the jurisdiction):

- the share capital of the company
- the directors and officers
- the members

- in the case of the first return, any changes that have been made since incorporation
- for subsequent returns, any changes that have been made since the previous return.

## 2.10  Accounting services

Companies are required to maintain accurate financial records. For example, s.99 of the BVI BCA 2004 simply states:

'A [BVI BC] shall keep records that

- (a) are sufficient to show and explain the company's transactions, and
- (b) will, at any time, enable the financial position of the company to be determined with reasonable accuracy.'

Section 59 of the Companies Law 2020 Revision of the Cayman Islands has a similar provision, based upon s.147 of the 1948 Act, stating that:

'Every company shall cause to be kept proper books of account with respect to (a) all sums of money received and expended by the company... (b) all sales and purchases of goods by the company, and (c) the assets and liabilities of the company and that they must 'give a true and a fair view of the state of the companies affairs and explain its transactions' .'

Companies legislation in other jurisdictions may contain more onerous accounting requirements. 203  A number of jurisdictions not only impose a duty to maintain adequate accounting records, but also oblige the directors to prepare prescribed annual financial statements that comply with generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP). There are no accounting standards applicable to most offshore jurisdictions, and accordingly the financial statements can be prepared under the standards of choice of the service provider or the client, usually following GAAP of the US, Canada or the UK. Some offshore jurisdictions also have a default audit requirement for a company's financial statements to be audited by an independent firm of auditors to confirm their accuracy, but this can usually be waived by ordinary resolution of the shareholders in general meeting.

The maintenance of accounting records is a service that many service providers offer, together with the preparation of annual financial statements, if required. If an audit has to be performed, however, this must be undertaken by an independent firm qualified to act in such matters.

i. companies incorporated in jurisdictions where there is no statutory requirement to prepare annual financial statements (therefore no exemption and no waiver required): Bahamian and other IBCs; BVI BCs; Cayman Islands exempt companies; and the New Manx Vehicle in the Isle of Man;

ii. companies incorporated in jurisdictions where certain companies are exempt from the formal onerous requirements: Gibraltar - where accounts and audit are required by Pt VII, Chs 1 and 2, ss.237-275 of the Companies Act 2014, but certain small companies are exempt under ss.11 and 13 of the Companies (Accounts) Act 1999; and

iii. companies incorporated in jurisdictions where companies can waive some or all of the requirement (to prepare annual financial statements and/or have them audited) by members' resolution: Bermuda - ss.84-87 of the Companies Act 1981 require the preparation of financial statements but s.88 allows the company to waive the same, and s.90 deals with audit; Guernsey - s.243 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008 requires financial statements to be prepared and presented to members, s.255 requires the accounts to be audited but s.256 allows the company to waive the audit by 'waiver resolution'; Jersey - art.104 of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991 as amended requires a company to prepare financial statements and s.109 requires an audit, but such requirements can be waived by members under arts 87(4), 104(5) and 109(1); Hong Kong - financial statements are required by Pt 9, Divisions 1-4 of the Companies Ord., Cap 622 and an audit by Division 5, but the strict compliance with the requirements can be waived by members under certain prescribed circumstances; Singapore - financial statements are required by s.199 of the Companies Act Cap 50, together with audit, but the strict requirements can be waived by ss.175A and 201C and for exempt private companies.

## 2.11  General corporate secretarial services

The corporate service provider usually handles any other matters that may require attention or action on behalf of companies under its management.

This includes, although it is not restricted to, the issuing of notices of meetings, preparing minutes of meetings, executing documentation as appropriate, dealing with correspondence, managing the assets of the company, and appointing and dealing with other agents, if required, such as attorneys, bankers, brokers, custodians, investment advisers, real estate agents, tax advisers, etc.

## 3. Involvement of the beneficial owner

In this section we describe some of the ways in which the beneficial owner (the client) may wish to be involved in the management of the company. We then consider the problems that this can create and offer possible solutions.

## 3.1 Appointment as a director

The client may wish to be appointed to the board of directors to obtain direct control over the affairs of the company, its assets and its business. Such a request may, in theory at least, be granted (subject to any statutory restrictions, such as those that exist in Bermuda, where it is a requirement that directors reside in the jurisdiction of incorporation).

The main issue to be considered is the effect that the appointment may have on the tax status of the company. This is because a company is typically required to pay corporation tax in the jurisdiction where it is tax resident according to the so-called management and control test. If the client effectively manages and controls the company from an onshore jurisdiction it is possible that the company could become liable to pay corporation tax to the revenue authorities of that onshore jurisdiction. Alternatively, the company may be challenged as a 'sham' in the country where the client resides for tax purposes. Clients should therefore be urged to seek advice from tax attorneys in their home jurisdiction before being appointed to the board of an offshore company.

A possible solution is to have a split board, whereby the client and at least two other directors (preferably resident in the offshore centre where the company is incorporated) are appointed. The client would therefore have all the powers of a director but the majority of the board, and therefore management and control, would be maintained offshore.

Furthermore, apart from tax issues, the appointment of the beneficial owner as a director inevitably undermines the level of confidentiality enjoyed by the company. This is because the identity of the directors may have to be provided to the registrar of companies and therefore becomes a matter of public record. A client whose priority is to maintain confidentiality ought not to be appointed to the board. Having said that, the recent introduction of beneficial owner registers, and the reporting requirements of the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) and the Common Reporting Standard (CRS), make the concept of confidentiality somewhat illusory.

## 3.2 Appointment as an agent, such as investment adviser

Instead of being appointed as a director, the client may wish to be appointed as an agent of the company, such as its investment manager or adviser. The precise powers conferred upon the client in these circumstances, by way of delegation of the powers of the directors, must be carefully considered. It is imperative to ensure that the position is not seen to carry with it overall management and control for tax purposes. In these circumstances the board would be well advised to appoint the client/beneficial owner as the company's investment adviser, rather than its investment manager.

Furthermore, the client ought to be appointed as investment adviser or manager only in circumstances where the service provider is sure that the client is an expert in the relevant field. Otherwise, the service provider, as director of the company, could be held in negligent breach of the duty of care to the company, and liable for loss caused to the company arising from the appointment of an agent who was not reasonably qualified. 204

If the beneficial owner is appointed as investment or other adviser or agent of the company, the appointment should be properly documented by the directors. Firstly, a shareholders' resolution should be passed to approve the appointment of the beneficial owner as, say, the company's investments manager (thereby releasing the board from liability for breach of their duty of care if, it turns out, the beneficial owner is not sufficiently qualified). Then, contemporaneously, a board resolution should be passed to make the appointment and to enter into a written investment management agreement between the company and the beneficial owner, confirming the nature of the beneficial owner's role and the terms of their appointment. An extract of a specimen investment management agreement is illustrated in Appendix 28 of the online Study Resources. Finally, the agreement should be executed and filed.

## 3.3 Power of attorney

There are two main types of power of attorney.

- i. A special power of attorney gives the donee of the power certain identified rights to represent the company in relation to a specific activity or matter; a specimen is illustrated in Appendix 29A of the online Study Resources.
- ii. A general power of attorney confers upon the donee of the power the right to represent the company in matters generally and, in particular, to bind the company to contracts and to enter into deals or transactions on behalf of the company; a specimen is illustrated in Appendix 29B of the online Study Resources.

Some service providers have been known to incorporate and provide directors for companies and then grant a general power of attorney over the affairs of the company to its beneficial owner. This gives the beneficial owner virtually unrestricted control over the company and its assets but, consequently, transfers management and control to the client and therefore has adverse tax implications for the company. In addition, the directors arguably fail to comply with their fiduciary and other duties by allowing a third party to exercise control over the affairs of the company. The directors may therefore be held liable for any losses suffered by the company as a consequence of their failure to safeguard corporate assets. An exoneration clause in the company's articles or DSA may provide protection in certain jurisdictions, but even then, wilful default is excluded.

Accordingly, it is not advisable for a director to grant the beneficial owner a general power of attorney. Special powers of attorney are preferable, and the extent of the power conferred upon the beneficial owner should be as narrow as possible and carefully defined in the instrument. The specimen power of attorney in Appendix 29A restricts the powers conferred upon the attorney-in-fact in two respects. Firstly, it limits the beneficial owner's powers to enter into a defined transaction and, secondly, a time limit is imposed so that, on its expiry, the power lapses.

## 3.4 Control of the company's bank account

The person who controls the finances effectively controls the affairs of the company. As a result, it should be the directors who are sole, or joint, signatories of the company's bank account.

If the beneficial owner is authorised to control the company's bank account, it may create a number of problems. The tax position of the company may be adversely affected as it arguably offends the management and control test for tax residency. Furthermore, it is possible that transactions involving corporate funds may be entered into without the knowledge or approval of the directors. This is of great concern to the service providers, who may become criminally liable under AML and CFT legislation.

An alternative, acceptable, arrangement is for the client to be given restricted signing powers over corporate accounts. For example, the client may be given power to co-sign alongside, or consent to payments that are initially suggested by, the directors. Delays in administration may arise, however. It is also possible that this may be viewed as conferring an element of control upon the beneficial owner, with the attendant adverse tax consequences.

## 3.5 Use of a company credit card

Enabling the client to have unrestricted use of a company credit card is similar in nature to the grant of a power to sign on the company's bank account. The same risks arise.

An alternative is to issue the client with a credit card in their personal name and arrange for the company to pay debts that arise from the proper use of the card. Any sums applied in this manner should be treated as a repayment of the shareholder's loan.

## 3.6 Directing the directors

Some beneficial owners may wish to exert authority over the board of their managed companies and be able to instruct the directors to act in accordance with their wishes. There is an argument in support of this approach. After all, the beneficial owner has instructed the corporate agent to incorporate the company and provide services, and is paying the fees for this, and usually the company has been funded from the client's own resources. Regardless of any pressure exerted by the client, however, service providers must not carry out any actions that they believe are not in the best interests of the company.

The extent to which the beneficial owner may lawfully direct the directors to enter into a particular deal or transaction is a matter that should be dealt with in the articles. If the articles of the managed company is based upon the 1948 Table A, 205  then the directors must manage the affairs of the company independently, without control or direct influence of the shareholders. Alternatively, if the article is based upon the 1985 Table A or the 2008 model articles published in England, 206  then the directors must comply with any directions given to them by special resolution of the shareholders.

Notwithstanding the provisions of the articles, it is important to remember that there is no such thing in law as a nominee director. Service providers should not be treated as nominees, i.e. as the client's puppets. The keys to the provision of directors for a managed company are (a) for the service providers to ensure that they are comfortable with the bona fides and integrity of the client and the client's affairs; and (b) for the client to trust the reasonableness and integrity of the directors appointed to manage the company. The beneficial owner may, of course, make recommendations to the board of their company, but should not be seen to give binding instructions.

- be familiar with the range of company services that are provided from offshore centres, including

The statutory requirements for companies to prepare annual financial statements, have them audited and presented to members at the AGM can be categorised into three types. i. companies incorporated in jurisdictions where there is no statutory requirement to prepare annual financial statements (therefore no exemption and no waiver required): Bahamian and other IBCs; and iii. companies incorporated in jurisdictions where companies can waive some or all of the requirement (to prepare annual financial statements and/or have them audited) by members’ resolution: Bermuda – ss.84–87 of the Companies Act 1981 require the preparation of financial statements but s.88 allows the company to waive the same, and s.90 deals with audit;
