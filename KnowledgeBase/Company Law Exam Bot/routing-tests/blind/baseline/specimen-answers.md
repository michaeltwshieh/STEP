1.2

Notice should be served on C, D and E as the three registered members. It should also be served on A and B because Article 27.5(c) gives every director who is not also a shareholder a right to receive notice. If anyone had become entitled to a share following a member's death or bankruptcy, Article 27.5(b) would cover that person, but no such facts are given.

Article 11.1 requires at least 14 clear days' written notice. Neither the day of service nor 28 August is counted. Notice must therefore be served, or deemed served, no later than 13 August. The safest course is personal delivery on or before 13 August, with a signed acknowledgment. Email is also permitted by Article 27.1 where the recipient has supplied a registered email address, and Article 27.2 treats service as taking place when the email is sent. Both methods could be used on 13 August to leave a record.

If post is used, the letter must be properly addressed, prepaid and sent to the member's registered address. Article 27.2 deems a meeting notice served on the fourth day after posting, so it should be posted no later than 9 August. All members could instead consent to shorter notice under Article 11.2, but without unanimous consent the full period must be observed.

1.3

A failure to give proper notice to someone entitled to it ordinarily invalidates the meeting and its proceedings. Article 11.3 creates a narrower exception: an accidental omission to give notice to a member, or a member's accidental non-receipt, does not invalidate the proceedings. That exception should not be treated as covering a deliberate omission or a defective notice period.

A member who attends in person or by proxy will normally be taken to have received notice. Even so, proper records of service should be retained rather than relying upon attendance.

1.4

C cannot pass the resolution at the meeting as originally called on 28 August. Article 12.2 requires two shareholders personally present as the quorum because Company has three shareholders. C's 70 per cent holding does not turn one person into two people. No business can be transacted without the required number of persons.

If a quorum is still absent half an hour after the appointed time, Article 12.3 applies. Assuming the directors convened the meeting, it stands adjourned to the same day in the following week at the same time and place. If no quorum is present within half an hour at that adjourned meeting, the member present becomes the quorum. C could then pass the special resolution because C holds 70 per cent, which exceeds the two-thirds threshold in the definition of "special resolution". C could not use Article 12.11 immediately instead, because a written resolution under that article requires the signatures of all shareholders entitled to notice and to vote.

1.5

On a show of hands Article 13.1 gives each member one vote, regardless of the number of shares held. C therefore casts one vote in favour, while D and E cast two votes against. The chairman must declare the proposed special resolution lost on that show of hands.

C should demand a poll before, or when, the result is declared. Article 12.6 permits one member holding at least 15 per cent of the paid-up capital to demand a poll, and C holds 70 per cent. On the poll Article 13.1 gives one vote for each share. C's 70 per cent will then be measured against D and E's combined 30 per cent. The resolution will pass because 70 per cent exceeds the two-thirds majority prescribed for a special resolution by the articles.

# Question 3

## SUBMIT THIS

The chosen jurisdiction is the British Virgin Islands. Company Ltd is treated as a BVI company governed by the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 and by the articles attached to the paper.

3.1

The usual division puts ownership in the members and management in the board. International Trust Company owns Company's shares in its capacity as trustee of the Redhouse Trust, while Ms Avril Nath and the trust officer form the board which manages Company's worldwide assets and its Midcity Bank account. Section 109 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 reflects that arrangement by placing the business and affairs of a BVI company under the management, direction or supervision of its directors.

The members retain powers of supervision and certain reserved decisions. They appoint and remove directors, alter the constitution and decide matters such as a voluntary winding-up. Depending upon the articles and legislation, they may also approve accounts, appoint auditors or declare a final dividend. The board takes the continuing commercial and administrative decisions and binds Company collectively.

That split grew out of the nineteenth-century corporate model. Entrepreneurs needed capital, while investors wanted to supply it without managing the undertaking from day to day or accepting open-ended liability for its debts. A numerous and changing body of shareholders lacked the board's specialist knowledge. Transferable shares also allowed an investor to leave without interrupting the management of the business. Management was therefore delegated to a smaller continuing board, while voting rights and the power to change the board protected the investors. The model fits a widely held trading company more naturally than this single-shareholder trust structure, where ownership and practical control sit within International Trust Company.

3.2

Article 19.1 gives the board the residual power to manage Company and to exercise every corporate power which the legislation or articles do not reserve to the members. It follows the older Article 80 form of Table A under the Companies Act 1948. The attached article contains no wording allowing the members to give the directors binding directions by special resolution.

The result is that International Trust Company cannot displace a decision made by the board merely by passing an ad hoc members' resolution. Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon [1909] confirms that where management power is conferred on directors in this form, the members cannot usurp it by instructing the board. The member can exercise a power reserved to it, amend the articles by the required procedure or remove and replace directors. Until then, the trust officer and Ms Nath must decide for Company.

Article 19.1 therefore reinforces the usual division rather than changing it. The provision is subject to the express exceptions elsewhere in the articles. For example, Article 19.3 requires member sanction before borrowing exceeds $1 million, and voluntary winding-up is a members' decision.

3.3

The legal division remains in place even though Company is an underlying asset-holding vehicle. International Trust Company holds Company's shares under the Redhouse Trust, but Company's worldwide assets and Midcity Bank account belong to Company. The trustee exercises the rights attached to the shares; it does not own those corporate assets directly.

The practical boundary is less tidy. The trustee employs the two directors, holds all the shares and administers the structure. It can appoint or remove the board and determine matters reserved to the member. It may make recommendations about the assets held for the Redhouse Trust. Still, each director owes duties to Company. Section 120 requires an honest, good-faith belief that a decision is in Company's best interests, while section 122 requires the care, diligence and skill of a reasonable director in the circumstances. A director cannot defend a loss by saying that the trustee gave instructions.

Article 19.1 makes that independence especially plain because it has the older Table A wording. International Trust Company cannot issue binding case-by-case directions to operate the Midcity Bank account or deal with a particular asset. The board must obtain the information, consider the transaction and pass its own resolution. The trustee may use its shareholder powers if it disagrees. Separate trustee and board records should be kept so that a trust decision is not mistaken for Company's decision.

The offshore structure concentrates influence without merging the two legal capacities. The better view is that International Trust Company controls the composition and constitution of Company, while the board controls Company's business until a reserved member power is properly exercised.

3.4

International Trust Company may remove the trust officer by ordinary resolution under Article 15.3 before the end of the period of office. It need not prove misconduct or other cause. Section 114 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 also recognises removal of directors in the BVI. Since International Trust Company is the sole registered member, it can pass the resolution alone, including by a written resolution under Article 12.11. Removal from office does not by itself extinguish any separate contractual claim under a service agreement.

Article 15.4 permits the member, by ordinary resolution, to appoint another person in place of the removed director. The replacement is subject to retirement at the same time as if appointed when the former director was last elected. Removal and replacement may sensibly be dealt with in the same written resolution, after the proposed director has consented to act and eligibility has been checked. Ms Nath remains a director, so Company would not be left without a board in the interval.

A director may separately vacate office under Article 17.1 by resignation, bankruptcy, unsoundness of mind, an unapproved office of profit or a disqualifying interest in a Company contract. Those events are different from removal under Article 15.3. After any change, Company's register of directors must be updated and the required notice sent to the BVI Registrar.

# Question 4

## SUBMIT THIS

The risk is considered by reference to a British Virgin Islands company because the content of a director's duties and the available protections vary between jurisdictions.

A director receives broad powers over property and money which belong to the company. The articles may permit the board to issue shares, borrow, mortgage assets, operate accounts, appoint agents and enter into contracts. Those powers create exposure at two levels. A transaction may bind the company in favour of an outsider, while the director remains personally answerable to the company for the way the power was used.

Acting outside authority is the first problem. A board must act collectively at a properly convened and quorate meeting, or use a valid written resolution. Where the articles impose a condition, such as Article 19.3's requirement for member sanction before borrowing above $1 million, the condition should be satisfied before completion. Royal British Bank v Turquand [1856] protects a good-faith outsider from undisclosed defects in Company's internal procedure. Section 31 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 gives comparable statutory protection where a person deals with someone shown in the register, or held out, as a director or authorised agent. Company may therefore remain bound even though its director exceeded internal authority. The director may then have to compensate Company for the resulting loss.

The widest personal exposure arises from the fiduciary duty. Section 120 requires a BVI director to act honestly, in good faith and in what the director believes to be Company's best interests. Powers must be used for their proper purpose. In Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver [1942], directors diverted to themselves an opportunity which should have benefited their company and had to account for their gain. A director must therefore resist using corporate property for personal or connected-party purposes.

Loyalty brings three recurring risks. A director must avoid self-dealing and conflicts, must not retain an unauthorised profit from office and must not accept a third-party benefit because of the directorship. A loan to the director, Company security for a director's debt, a contract with a business owned by the director and diversion of a corporate opportunity all call for disclosure and approval. Section 124 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 addresses disclosure of interests. Articles 17.2 and 18.2 go further for this Company: the interested director must declare the nature of the interest and may not vote on the contract or a connected matter. The vote is not counted. Where a disinterested quorum cannot be formed, informed member approval is the proper route.

Care and skill create a different exposure. Section 122 measures conduct against the care, diligence and skill a reasonable director would exercise in the same circumstances, taking account of the nature of Company, the decision and the responsibilities undertaken. Signing a document without reading it, leaving blank signed cheques with a colleague or ignoring suspect accounts can amount to negligence. Dorchester Finance Co Ltd v Stebbing [1989] held directors liable after blank cheques enabled a co-director to misuse corporate funds. Re Barings plc (No. 5) [2000] shows that delegation does not remove the need for a workable system of supervision.

A director may rely on others, but only within limits. Under section 123, reliance on a competent employee, professional adviser, another director or an authorised committee is available where the director acts in good faith, makes proper inquiry when warning signs appear and has no knowledge that reliance is misplaced. The contrast between Lexi Holdings plc v Luqman [2009] and Madoff Securities International Ltd v Raven [2013] turns on those warning signs. Reliance was unsafe where the accounts and relationships called for investigation, but was permitted for directors outside the inner circle who had reasonable grounds to accept expert representations. Written advice, board papers, valuation material and a record of the questions asked reduce this risk.

An offshore director faces added pressure from the beneficial owner. The expression "nominee director" gives a false impression. A director supplied by a corporate services business owes the same duties as any other director and cannot act as the client's puppet. A general power of attorney, an unrestricted bank mandate or blind compliance with payment instructions can transfer practical control and expose Company to loss, tax consequences and possible AML or CFT liability. Any delegation should be narrow, documented, time-limited and monitored. Company's directors should retain control of its bank account, subject to carefully defined co-signing arrangements if the facts justify them.

Insolvency changes the risk sharply. A liquidator will review the board's conduct, payments to connected creditors and the point at which the directors knew Company could not pay its debts. Section 211 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 applies the Insolvency Act 2003. Section 55 of that Act addresses fraudulent trading, while section 256 addresses wrongful trading. Fraudulent trading requires knowing participation in business carried on with intent to defraud creditors. Wrongful trading can apply where a director knew or ought to have known there was no prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation. Continuing to incur liabilities at that stage may produce a personal contribution order. Articles, indemnities and insurance do not excuse this conduct.

The remedies show the financial stakes. Company may set aside a conflicted transaction unless a good-faith third party is protected, recover an undisclosed profit or require the director to indemnify it for loss. Negligence can lead to compensatory damages. Foss v Harbottle (1843) ordinarily makes Company the proper claimant, though a minority member may bring a derivative claim where those in control committed fraud against Company. Removal from office, disqualification and regulatory action may follow apart from damages.

Risk reduction begins before the decision. A director should confirm the source and scope of the power, obtain the papers, identify conflicts, ask questions and record why the transaction benefits Company. Proper board procedure matters. So do accurate minutes, separation of Company funds from client funds and prompt action when solvency becomes doubtful.

Informed prior approval or later ratification by the members may release a director from liability to Company after full disclosure. It can cover a disclosed connected transaction, an authorised benefit or negligent management. It cannot cleanse a dishonest appropriation of Company's property or prejudice creditors. Cook v Deeks [1916] illustrates the limit: controllers could not use their voting power to ratify the diversion of corporate contracts to themselves.

The articles and a director's service agreement may also allocate risk. Section 132 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 permits properly drawn exoneration and indemnity provisions. Article 29.1 excuses an error, oversight or mistake only in the absence of bad faith, fraud or dishonesty. Article 29.2 provides an indemnity from Company within lawful bounds but excludes wilful default, bad faith and fraud. Article 29.3 does not protect a director from the director's own wilful default. The wording matters, as an over-wide clause may be struck down.

A company management agreement may give the corporate services provider an indemnity from the client. It may fail to cover the individual director unless the director is a party, and it leaves the director exposed if the client cannot pay. It also cannot stop a third party bringing a claim. The clause should be drawn to the client's attention and confined to liability which may lawfully be covered.

Directors' and officers' insurance is the last financial layer. Section 133 permits it in the BVI, while Article 19.4 authorises the board to purchase it. A normal policy covers negligent mistakes and third-party claims arising from good-faith conduct. Deliberate default, fraud and dishonesty fall outside cover. Policy limits, exclusions and notification conditions must be checked before trouble arises.

The reward of office therefore comes with control over another person's assets and with the duties attached to that control. A careful director reduces exposure through informed and independent decisions, proper approval, disclosure, records and early action on insolvency. Contractual protection and insurance can absorb part of an honest mistake, but neither will rescue disloyal or dishonest conduct.

5.1

A members' voluntary winding-up is appropriate where Company can pay all its debts in full. The directors first make a full inquiry and sign a declaration that Company can discharge its debts within the permitted period, supported by a statement of assets and liabilities. The members then resolve to wind up Company and appoint a liquidator. The liquidator collects and realises the assets, pays every creditor, reports to the members and distributes any surplus to them before the final filing and dissolution.

A creditors' voluntary winding-up begins without a declaration of solvency. The directors convene a members' meeting to pass the winding-up resolution, then convene a meeting of creditors and present Company's financial position. The creditors have the greater voice because their money is at risk. They choose the liquidator by majority vote. The liquidator realises the assets and pays claims in the statutory order, followed by final meetings of both members and creditors.

Company must use the creditors' process. Its assets total $2,605,000, comprising Blackacre at $1,700,000, artwork at $550,000, cash of $40,000, securities of $310,000 and the $5,000 receivable. Its stated liabilities total $3,375,000 before the liquidator's $40,000 costs. The $2,400,000 mortgage is under-secured by $700,000.
