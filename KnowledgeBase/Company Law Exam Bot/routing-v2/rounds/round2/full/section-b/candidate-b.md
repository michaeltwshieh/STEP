1.2

The notice must be served on C, D and E as the registered members. It must also be served on A and B if, as the facts suggest, they are directors who are not shareholders. Article 27.5 requires notice to every member who has supplied an address or registered facsimile number, to a person entitled through the death or bankruptcy of a member, and to every director who is not also a shareholder. No person entitled through death or bankruptcy is identified. Article 27.5 also says that no other person is entitled to notice, so an auditor should be included only if the applicable Companies Legislation gives that auditor a right to notice.

Article 27.1 allows personal service, post to a member's registered address, or email to the registered email address supplied to the Company. Article 27.2 deems a notice sent by post served on the fourth day after posting, while an email is served when it is sent. Email gives the clearest evidence of timing, but I would send the notice by email and by recorded or special-delivery post and retain the transmission and delivery records.

Article 11.1 requires at least 14 clear days in writing, excluding both the day on which notice is served or deemed served and the meeting day. For a meeting on 28 August, personal service or email service should be completed by 13 August [year]. A notice posted on 9 August [year] is deemed served on 13 August under Article 27.2, so 9 August is the latest posting date on that assumption. An earlier posting date is prudent where delivery records may be disputed.

Article 11.2 permits a shorter notice period only with the consent of all members entitled to receive notice. That consent should be retained with the Company's minutes.

1.3

The ordinary position is that a meeting which has not been properly notified to a person entitled to notice has not been properly convened, so its proceedings may be invalid. Article 11.3 qualifies that position: an accidental omission to give notice to, or accidental non-receipt of notice by, a member does not invalidate the proceedings. The qualification does not cover a deliberate omission or a known failure to serve a person. A member who attends in person or by proxy will ordinarily be treated as having received notice. The Company should still serve every person identified by Article 27.5 and retain proof, since relying on an accidental-omission provision after a deliberate omission would be unsafe.

1.4

C alone cannot transact business at the meeting on 28 August. The Company has three shareholders, so Article 12.2 requires two shareholders personally present before business can begin. This is not a meeting of a sole-shareholder company.

The meeting was convened by the directors, rather than on a members' requisition. Article 12.3 therefore requires it to stand adjourned if a quorum is absent after half an hour, to the same day in the following week at the same time and place. If C is still the only member present at that adjourned meeting and half an hour passes without another shareholder, the members present then constitute the quorum. C can proceed with the notified business.

C holds 70 of the 100 issued shares. On a poll that is 70 per cent of the votes, which exceeds the two-thirds threshold for a special resolution in Article 1. If no poll is demanded, C is the only member present at the adjourned meeting and can vote on the show of hands. C cannot use a written resolution signed only by C, because Article 12.11 requires the signatures of all shareholders entitled to receive notice and vote, including D and E.

1.5

On a show of hands Article 13.1 gives each member present in person one vote. C therefore has one vote, D has one and E has one. With C voting for the motion and D and E voting against it, the motion is lost on the show of hands.

C should demand a poll before, or at the latest on, the chairman's declaration of that result. Article 12.6 permits a poll to be demanded by one member holding at least 15 per cent of the paid-up capital, and C holds 70 per cent. On the poll C has 70 votes and D and E together have 30. The resolution therefore carries by 70 per cent, which passes the two-thirds special-resolution threshold in Article 1. Under Article 12.8 the result of the poll is the resolution of the meeting. If C does not demand the poll in time, the chairman's declaration on the show of hands is conclusive under Article 12.7.

Question 3

The Bahamas is the chosen jurisdiction for this answer. Company Ltd is treated as a Bahamian company governed internally by the Articles attached to the examination question. International Trust Company is the registered shareholder under the Redhouse Trust, and the analysis keeps that trustee capacity separate from Company's legal personality.

3.1

The usual division gives the board the day-to-day management of the Company's business and gives the members ownership and the powers reserved to them by legislation and the Articles. The members provide the equity, vote in general meeting, appoint or remove directors and decide constitutional matters such as changes to the Articles, capital alterations and a voluntary winding-up. The directors act collectively as the Company's management organ, enter contracts, operate accounts and deal with assets. Their power is coupled with a fiduciary duty to act in good faith for the Company and a duty to exercise reasonable care and skill.

That arrangement answered defects in earlier business forms. A sole trader could make every decision but carried unlimited liability and could raise capital only up to personal means. A partnership spread capital and risk, but a partner's interest was hard to transfer and partnership property had to be held through the partners. The Joint Stock Companies Act 1844 allowed a company to be formed by registration, to own property as a separate person, to raise money from the public and to allow shares to be transferred.

The Limited Liability Act 1855 then limited the investor's exposure to corporate debts, subject to safeguards such as capital maintenance. Risk moved towards creditors, while shareholders could invest without managing a railway, factory or shipping business themselves. Directors were therefore appointed as the shareholders' business managers, with duties and the possibility of removal as checks on their use of corporate power.

The Companies Act 1907 recognised the smaller private company. A small undertaking did not need public fundraising, a large board or the full compliance machinery designed for a widely held company. It could combine the advantages of incorporation with simpler procedures and, in practice, closer overlap between ownership and management. The modern offshore asset-holding company uses that history in a concentrated form: the shareholder holds the investment and the board manages the Company's property for the shareholder body.

3.2

Article 19.1 adopts the old Article 80 approach from the Companies Act 1948 model. It says that the business of the Company is managed by the directors, who may exercise all the Company's powers except powers which the Companies Legislation or the Articles require the Company in general meeting to exercise. The article therefore gives the board the residual management power, including dealings with Company's assets and Midcity Bank account, unless a specific reservation applies.

The effect is different from the later Article 70 approach in the Companies Act 1985 model and the corresponding 2008 model articles, which permit directions given by special resolution. Article 19.1 contains no such direction limb. International Trust Company may use its member powers to alter the Articles, remove or appoint directors and decide matters expressly reserved to members, but it cannot simply pass a resolution directing the board to carry out a particular management transaction while Article 19.1 remains unchanged. Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon illustrates that shareholders cannot usurp a power expressly vested in the board; changing the Articles is the proper route.

3.3

Company is a separate legal person. The shares are held by International Trust Company in its trustee capacity, but Company's assets and its Midcity Bank account belong to Company. They do not belong personally to the trustee, the trust officer or the beneficiaries merely because the assets are held in an underlying structure. Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd confirms the separate personality of a properly incorporated company, including the validity of a one-person structure.

The trustee has practical influence because it is the registered member. It can exercise the member's voting rights, appoint or remove directors under Articles 15.3 and 15.4, amend the constitutional arrangements where the required resolution is passed and receive any member return permitted by law and the Articles. Those powers supervise the board's composition and the constitution. Article 19.1 keeps ordinary management with the directors. It follows that an instruction from International Trust Company to transfer or dispose of Company's assets is a recommendation or a member action only if it falls within a power reserved to the member; it is not a substitute for the board's own decision on a management matter.

The trust officer who has accepted a directorship owes the fiduciary and care duties of a director to Company. The fact that the same person is employed by International Trust Company creates a practical risk of divided loyalties, but it does not create a separate category of nominee director. The director must consider Company's interests, ask for the papers and foreseeable consequences, disclose any personal or institutional conflict and refuse an instruction that would cause a breach. The director can act on reliable professional advice after proper inquiry, but cannot defend a breach by saying that the trustee gave the instruction.

There may also be a practical tax and regulatory effect if the trustee or its officers exercise management and control from another state. Module 11 identifies risks to the Company's tax residence, service-provider liability and compliance position when a client or beneficial owner exercises unrestricted control. A narrowly documented delegation or special power of attorney can assist with a defined transaction, but a general power of attorney or blind directions would erode the independent board structure. The legal division in Article 19.1 therefore remains, although the trustee's control over membership can make the distinction difficult to maintain in practice.

3.4

Under Article 15.3, Company may remove a director before the end of the director's period of office by ordinary resolution, notwithstanding anything in a service agreement. The Article does not require the members to show cause. In this Company, International Trust Company is the sole registered member, so it can pass the removal decision by a written resolution under Article 12.11, provided the resolution is signed by its authorised representative. A general meeting is also possible, with the notice, quorum and voting requirements in Articles 10 to 13.

The office can also be vacated under Article 17.1. The listed events include bankruptcy, unsound mind, resignation by written notice and a direct or indirect interest in, or participation in the profits of, a contract with Company. Article 17.2 protects a director who is interested through membership of a corporation contracting with Company if the interest is declared at the first subsequent directors' meeting and the director does not vote on the contract or related matter. These events are vacation provisions rather than a discretionary member removal.

Article 15.4 permits Company, by ordinary resolution, to appoint another person in place of a director removed under Article 15.3. The appointment may be made in the same written resolution as the removal. The new director is subject to retirement at the time the removed director would have retired. The incoming director's written consent should be obtained, the minutes or written resolution retained, the register of directors updated and the change notified to the Registrar where required. The supplied Articles do not give the remaining board a general power to appoint a replacement. Article 20.8 permits continuing directors below quorum to act for the purpose of increasing the number of directors or summoning a general meeting, so the member's appointment route should be used.

Question 4

A directorship gives its holder access to the organ with power to bind the company, so the reward of fees or influence comes with personal exposure. Article 19.1 in the traditional articles is a useful measure of the breadth of that office: the directors manage the business and may exercise the Company's powers unless legislation or the Articles reserve the matter to the members. The risks arise because that power is exercised for the Company, through a board decision, and against a standard that does not fall away because the company is privately held or the director is supplied by a service provider.

The first risk is misuse of power. A director must act honestly and in good faith for the Company's interests, and must use a power for its proper purpose. Hutton v West Cork Railway Co. links the use of corporate property to the Company's interests. In Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver, directors who diverted an opportunity and profit to themselves had to account to the company. Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi shows the same problem where a director tried to divert a company client to himself. A director should therefore record the commercial reason for a decision, keep the Company's opportunity and property separate from personal interests and disclose any proposed benefit before acting.

Conflicts and self-dealing are a practical version of that risk. A director may have an interest in a supplier, purchaser, lender or related company, or may receive a secret profit or benefit from a third party. Article 18.2 in the traditional articles permits an interested director to contract with the Company only after disclosing the nature of the interest at the board meeting, and the director must not vote on the contract or connected matter. The director should make the disclosure in writing, leave the decision to disinterested directors where the Articles permit and obtain informed member approval where the Articles or the nature of the transaction calls for it. An approval or later ratification may release liability for a disclosed conflict or negligent act, but it cannot cleanse a fraudulent or dishonest expropriation that prejudices the Company, creditors or minority members. Aberdeen Railway v Blaikie Bros is an early warning against an interested director participating in a company contract.

A second broad exposure is care, skill and supervision. The modern standard in the course materials is objective. Dorchester Finance Co. Ltd v Stebbing treated directors who signed blank cheques and failed to inquire into payments as liable, while Norman v Theodore Goddard placed the standard against the person performing a director's functions. A director should read the facility, accounts, insurance proposal, valuation or transfer papers before signing, ask questions where the circumstances call for them and retain the advice and reasons supporting the decision. An honest business decision that was carefully made is not negligence merely because it produced a loss, but an uninformed decision is a different matter.

Delegation does not remove the duty of oversight. Re Barings plc (No.5) held that directors could delegate functions but remained responsible for supervision; a system that gave a trader uncontrolled scope to conceal losses did not meet the standard. Lexi Holdings plc (In Administration) v Luqman treated the state of the accounts and the family history as warnings that made reliance unsafe. Madoff Securities International Ltd (In Liquidation) v Raven shows the other side: reliance on expertise may be reasonable for directors with no grounds for suspicion, while an inner group with knowledge of the warning signs cannot rely on the same excuse. The protection is a real inquiry, competent advice and continuing supervision, not a signature placed on whatever a manager or client puts in front of the board. Bishopsgate Investment Management Ltd v Maxwell (No.2) is the sharper example of the danger of signing away company assets on an unconnected person's say-so.

There is also authority and procedure risk. The board must check the memorandum and Articles, identify any member approval or quorum requirement and act collectively under the applicable decision procedure. If the board enters a contract outside its internal authority, Royal British Bank v Turquand protects a good-faith outsider who had no notice of the irregularity, but that rule does not protect the director from an internal claim by the Company. The director reduces this exposure by obtaining the right resolution before signing, keeping a clear minute and limiting any power of attorney to the transaction and period actually approved.

Insolvency creates a more direct personal risk. A director who continues the business while knowing there is no prospect of paying creditors may face fraudulent-trading liability under section 332 of the Companies Act 1948, with the approach illustrated by Re William C Leitch Ltd. Wrongful trading under section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 uses the easier objective question of what the director knew or ought to have known, although the course materials record that offshore jurisdictions differ. Directors should monitor accounts and cash flow, stop incurring debts when payment has no realistic prospect, obtain insolvency advice and consider an available rescue or winding-up process. No indemnity should be treated as a licence to trade on dishonestly or recklessly.

Service-provider and trustee arrangements add a control risk. Module 8 rejects the idea that a paid "nominee director" is a puppet, and a client's instruction is not a defence to a breach of duty. Module 11 records the tax, loss and AML/CFT risks when a beneficial owner controls the bank account, grants itself a general power of attorney or directs every board act. A director should insist that the board retains real decision-making, use a narrow special power of attorney for a defined matter, check the client's bona fides and refuse an unlawful or improper instruction. This also protects the service provider's records and the Company's assets.

The available protections shift financial exposure without erasing the office. A traditional Article 29.1 may exonerate a director for an error, oversight or mistake in the absence of bad faith, fraud or dishonesty, to the extent allowed by the jurisdiction. Article 29.2 may indemnify lawful liabilities and reasonable costs, subject to the exclusions in the Articles and legislation; Article 29.3 does not make a director liable for another officer's default unless the director's own wilful default caused the loss. A directors' service agreement can repeat a carefully limited exoneration and indemnity. A company management agreement can give the service provider a client indemnity, but Rae v Meek, West v Lazard Brothers and Roywest Trust Corp (Bahamas) Ltd v Savannah NV show why a clause must be drawn to the client's attention and confined to negligence rather than fraud or a blanket exclusion. Directors' and officers' insurance is a further layer for good-faith negligence and inadvertent third-party liability, while deliberate default, fraud and dishonesty remain outside its proper cover.

Taken together, the exposure is manageable only when the director treats the appointment as an office requiring independent judgment. Articles, approvals, a carefully drawn service agreement, insurance and a client indemnity can meet the financial consequences of an honest mistake, but they do not excuse fraud, dishonesty, wilful default or continued trading without a reasonable prospect of paying creditors. A director who reads the papers, records the reasons, challenges instructions and reacts to insolvency has the best protection available under the course materials.

Question 5

The Bahamas is the chosen jurisdiction for this answer. Company Ltd is treated as a Bahamian offshore company for the purpose of applying the Bahamas references in the course materials, while the general winding-up and priority model in those materials supplies any detail not reproduced for the chosen entity. I do not invent a Bahamas form, deadline or priority rule where the materials leave it open.

5.1

A members' voluntary winding-up is the solvent route. The directors first make a full inquiry into the Company's affairs and sign a declaration of solvency stating that the debts can be paid in full, usually within 12 months from commencement, with a statement of assets and liabilities attached. The members then pass the required resolution to wind up the Company and appoint a liquidator. The liquidator takes control, realises the assets, pays the debts, presents a final account, holds or dispenses with the final members' meeting and files the account and dissolution request. A false or negligent declaration exposes the directors to the sanctions described in the course materials.

A creditors' voluntary winding-up is the appropriate route for Company because the facts state that it is insolvent. The directors cannot honestly sign a declaration that all debts will be paid in full. They should convene the members to pass the winding-up resolution, file or publish it as the applicable regime requires, and convene the creditors. The directors present the Company's assets, liabilities and creditor list at the creditors' meeting. The creditors, rather than the members, choose the liquidator by the applicable voting procedure. The liquidator then takes control, collects and sells the assets, admits proofs of debt, pays the costs and creditors in the statutory order and prepares the final account for the final meetings and filings.

The difference is therefore both financial and procedural. In a members' winding-up the directors' solvency declaration permits the members to control the appointment and the process; in a creditors' winding-up the absence of solvency shifts the effective choice of liquidator and the conduct of the process towards the creditors. Company cannot use the members' route merely because its shareholder prefers it. A fixed-charge creditor may enforce its security separately, but the unsecured shortfall and the other debts remain in the creditors' liquidation.

The course notes that Bahamas IBC legislation may permit a member or directors to pass a winding-up resolution where the memorandum or Articles authorise that route. That does not turn an insolvent Company into a members' voluntary winding-up or remove the creditors' role. The exact Bahamas provisions governing the resolution, creditors' meeting and filings are not reproduced in the permitted materials, so the general creditors' voluntary model is the safe procedural description.
