## 4. Modification of shareholder rights

If, at any time, the share capital is divided into different classes of shares, the rights attached to any class (unless otherwise provided by the terms of issue of the shares of that class) may be varied with the consent in writing of the holders of 75% of the issued shares of that class [or with the sanction of a [special] resolution passed at a separate general meeting of the holders of the shares of the class]. To every such separate general meeting the provisions of these regulations relating to general meetings shall, mutatits mutandis , apply, but so that the necessary quorum shall be two persons at least holding or representing by proxy one-third of the issued shares of the class and that any holder of shares of the class present in person or by proxy may demand a poll.

## 5. Lien

- 5.1 The Company shall have a lien on every share (not being a fully paid share) for all moneys (whether presently payable or not) called or payable at a fixed time in respect of that share, and the Company shall also have a lien on all shares (other than fully paid shares) standing registered in the name of a single person for all moneys presently payable by him or his estate to the Company. The Company's lien, if any, on a share shall extend to all dividends payable thereon.
- 5.2 The Company may sell, in such manner as the directors think fit, any shares in which the Company has a lien, but no sale shall be made unless some amount in respect of which the lien exists is presently payable nor until the expiration of 14 days after a notice in writing, demanding payment of such part of the amount in respect of which the lien exists as is presently payable, has been given to the registered holder for the time being of the share, or the persons entitled thereto by reason of his death or bankruptcy.
- 5.3 For giving effect to any such sale, the directors may authorise some person to transfer the shares sold to the purchaser thereof. The purchaser shall be registered as the holder of the shares comprised in any such transfer and he shall not be bound to see to the application of the purchase money, nor shall his title to the shares be affected by any irregularity or invalidity in the proceedings in reference to the sale.
- 5.4 The proceeds of the sale shall be received by the company and applied in payment of such part of the amount in respect of which the lien exists as is presently payable, and the residue shall (subject to a like lien for sums not presently payable as existed upon the shares prior to the sale) be paid to the person entitled to the shares at the date of the sale.

## 6. Calls on shares

- 6.1 The directors may, from time to time, make calls upon the shareholders in respect of any moneys unpaid on the shares; and each shareholder shall (subject to receiving at least 14 days' notice specifying the time or times of payment) pay to the Company at the time or times so specified the amount called on his shares.
- 6.2 The joint holders of a share shall be jointly and severally liable to pay calls in respect thereof.

- 6.3 If a sum called in respect of a share is not paid before or on the day appointed for payment thereof, the person from whom the sum is due shall pay interest upon the sum at the rate of 6% per annum from the day appointed for the payment thereof to the time of the actual payment, but the directors shall be at liberty to waive payment of that interest wholly or in part.
- 6.4 The provisions of these articles as to the liability of joint holders and as to payment of interest shall apply in the case of non-payment of any sum which, by the terms of issue of a share, becomes payable at a fixed time, whether on account of the amount of the share, or by way of premium, as if the same had become payable by virtue of a call duly made and notified.
- 6.5 The directors may make arrangements on the issue of shares for a difference between the holders in the amount of calls to be paid and in the times of payment.
- 6.6 The directors may, if they think fit, receive from any member willing to advance the same all or any part of the moneys uncalled and unpaid upon any shares held by him; and upon all or any of the moneys so advanced may (until the same would, but for such advance, become presently payable) pay interest at such rate (not exceeding, without the sanction of the Company in general meeting, 6%) as may be agreed upon between the member paying the sum in advance and the directors.

## 7. Forfeiture of shares (for unpaid calls)

- 7.1 If a member fails to pay any call or instalment of a call on the day appointed for payment thereof, the directors may, at any time thereafter during such time as any part of such call or instalment remains unpaid, serve a notice on him requiring payment of so much of the call or instalment as is unpaid, together with any interest that may have accrued.
- 7.2 The notice shall name a further day (not earlier than the expiration of fourteen days from the date of the notice) on or before which the payment required by the notice is to be made, and shall state that, in the event of non-payment at or before the time appointed, the shares in respect of which the call was made will be liable to be forfeited.
- 7.3 If the requirements of such notice are not complied with, any share in respect of which the notice has been given may, at any time thereafter, before the payment required by the notice has been made, be forfeited by a resolution of the directors to that effect.
- 7.4 A forfeited share may be sold or otherwise disposed of on such terms and in such manner as the directors think fit, and at any time before a sale or disposition the forfeiture may be cancelled on such terms as the directors think fit.
- 7.5 A person whose shares have been forfeited shall cease to be a member in respect of the forfeited shares, but shall, notwithstanding, remain liable to pay to the company all moneys that at the date of forfeiture were payable by him to the Company in respect of the shares, but his liability shall cease if and when the Company receives payment in full of the nominal amount of the shares.

- 7.6 A statutory declaration in writing by a director of the Company, declaring that a share in the Company has been duly forfeited on a date stated in the declaration, shall be conclusive evidence of the facts therein stated as against all persons claiming to be entitled to the share. The Company may receive the consideration, if any, given for the share on any sale or disposition thereof and may execute a transfer of the share in favour of the person to whom the share is sold or disposed of and he shall thereupon be registered as the holder of the share, and shall not be bound to see to the application of the purchase money, if any, nor shall his title to the share be affected by any irregularity or invalidity in the proceedings in reference to the forfeiture, sale or disposal of the share.
- 7.7 The provisions of these articles as to forfeiture shall apply in the case of non-payment of any sum which by the terms of issue of a share becomes payable at a fixed time, whether on account of the amount of the share, or by way of premium, as if the same had been payable by virtue of a call duly made and notified.

## 8. Transfer and transmission of shares

- 8.1 The instrument of transfer of any share shall be executed by or on behalf of the transferor and transferee, and the transferor shall be deemed to remain a holder of the share until the name of the transferee is entered in the register of members in respect thereof.
- 8.2 Shares shall be transferred in the following form, or in any usual or common form approved by the directors:

'I, [name of transferor], of [address] in consideration of the sum of $[price] paid to me by [name of transferee], of [address] (hereinafter called 'the said transferee') do hereby transfer to the said transferee the share (or shares) numbered in the undertaking called [name of the Company], to hold unto the said transferee, subject to the several conditions on which I hold the same: and I, the said transferee, do hereby agree to take the said share (or shares) subject to the conditions aforesaid.'

- 8.3 The directors may decline to register any transfer of shares to a person of whom they do not approve, and may also decline to register any transfer of shares on which the Company has a lien.

## 9. Alteration of capital

- 9.1 The company may, from time to time by [special] resolution, increase the share capital by such sum, to be divided into shares of such amount, as the resolution shall prescribe.
- 9.2 Subject to any direction to the contrary that may be given by the company in general meeting, all new shares shall, before issue, be offered to such persons as at the date of the offer are entitled to receive notices from the company of general meetings in proportion, as nearly as the circumstances admit, to the amount of the existing shares to which they are entitled. The offer shall be made by notice specifying the number of shares offered, and limiting a time within which the offer, if not accepted, will be deemed to be declined, and after the expiration of that time, or on receipt of an intimation from the person to whom the offer is made that he declines to accept the shares offered, the directors may dispose of those shares in such manner as they think most beneficial to the company. The directors may likewise so dispose of any new shares which (by reason of the ratio which the new shares bear to shares held by persons entitled to an offer of new shares) cannot, in the opinion of the directors, be conveniently offered under this article.

- 9.3 The new shares shall be subject to the same provisions with reference to the payment of calls, lien, transfer, transmission, forfeiture and otherwise as the shares in the original share capital.
- 9.4 The Company may by [ordinary] resolution:
- a. consolidate and divide all or any of its share capital into shares of larger amount than its existing shares;
- b. sub-divide its existing shares, or any of them, into shares of smaller amounts than is fixed by the memorandum of association, subject to the provisions of Companies Legislation; and
- c. cancel any shares which, at the date of the passing of the resolution, have not been taken or agreed to be taken by any person.
- 9.5 The Company may, by [special] resolution, reduce its share capital and any capital redemption reserve fund in any manner and with, and subject to, any incident authorised and consent required by the Companies Legislation.

## 10. General meetings

- 10.1 The first general meeting of the Company shall be held within a period of not more than 18 months from the day that the Company has the right to commence business.
- 10.2 A general meeting ('the annual general meeting') shall be held once in every calendar year at such time (not being more than 15 months after the holding of the last preceding general meeting) and place as may be resolved by the company in general meeting. If the directors fail to convene an annual general meeting, it may be convened by any two members in the same manner as nearly as possible as that in which meetings are to be convened by the directors.
- 10.3 All general meetings other than annual general meetings shall be called extraordinary [special] general meetings.
- 10.4 The directors may, whenever they think fit, convene an extraordinary [special] general meeting. Alternatively any member(s) holding at least 10% of the issued share capital of the Company may convene an extraordinary [special] general meeting in the same manner as nearly as possible as that in which meetings may be convened by the directors.

## 11. Notice of general meetings

- 11.1 Subject to the requirements of the Companies Legislation relating to [special] resolutions, at least 14 clear days' notice in writing (exclusive of the day on which the notice is served or deemed to be served, and of the day for which notice is given) specifying the place, the day and hour of meeting and, in case of special business, the general nature of that business shall be given in the manner provided by para.27 of these articles, to such persons as are entitled to receive such notices from the company.
- 11.2 A general meeting may be convened by such shorter notice with the consent of all the members entitled to receive notice of the meeting, and in such manner as those members may think fit.

- 11.3 The accidental omission to give notice of a meeting to, or the non-receipt of a notice of a meeting by, any member shall not invalidate the proceedings at any meeting.

## 12. Proceedings at general meetings

- 12.1 All business shall be deemed special that is transacted at any extraordinary meeting, and also all that is transacted at an annual general meeting, with the exception of sanctioning a dividend, consideration of the accounts, balance sheets, the report of the directors (and auditors, if any) and the election of directors and other officers in the place of those retiring by rotation.
- 12.2 No business shall be transacted at any general meeting unless a quorum of members is present at the time when the meeting proceeds to business; save as herein otherwise provided, two shareholders personally present shall be a quorum.
- 12.3 If, within half an hour from the time appointed for the meeting, a quorum is not present, the meeting, if convened upon the requisition of members, shall be dissolved; in any other case, it shall stand adjourned to the same day in the next week, at the time and place, and if at the adjourned meeting a quorum is not present within half an hour from the time appointed for the meeting, the members present shall be a quorum.
- 12.4 The shareholders present in person or by proxy and entitled to vote shall elect by majority vote one of their number to act as chairman.

## Directors' Powers and Responsibilities

## 3. Directors' general authority

Subject to the articles, the directors are responsible for the management of the company's business, for which purpose they may exercise all the powers of the company.

## 4. Shareholders' reserve power

- (1) The shareholders may, by special resolution, direct the directors to take, or refrain from taking, specified action.
- (2) No such special resolution invalidates anything which the directors have done before the passing of the resolution.

## 5. Directors may delegate

- (1) Subject to the articles, the directors may delegate any of the powers which are conferred on them under the articles-
2. (a) to such person or committee;
3. (b) by such means (including by power of attorney);
4. (c) to such an extent;

6. (e) on such terms and conditions;

- (3) The directors may revoke any delegation in whole or part, or alter its terms and conditions.

## 6. Committees

- (1) Committees to which the directors delegate any of their powers must follow procedures which are based as far as they are applicable on those provisions of the articles which govern the taking of decisions by directors.
- (2) The directors may make rules of procedure for all or any committees, which prevail over rules derived from the articles if they are not consistent with them.

## Decision-Making by Directors

## 7. Directors to take decisions collectively

- (1) The general rule about decision-making by directors is that any decision of the directors must be either a majority decision at a meeting or a decision taken in accordance with article 8.
- (2) If:
3. (a) the company only has one director, and
4. (b) no provision of the articles requires it to have more than one director,

the general rule does not apply, and the director may take decisions without regard to any of the provisions of the articles relating to directors' decision-making.

## 8. Unanimous decisions

- (1) A decision of the directors is taken in accordance with this article when all eligible directors indicate to each other by any means that they share a common view on a matter.
- (2) Such a decision may take the form of a resolution in writing, copies of which have been signed by each eligible director or to which each eligible director has indicated agreement in writing.
- (3) References in this article to eligible directors are to directors who would have been entitled to vote on the matter had it been proposed as a resolution at a directors' meeting.
- (4) A decision may not be taken in accordance with this article if the eligible directors would not have formed a quorum at such a meeting.

## 9. Calling a directors' meeting

- (1) Any director may call a directors' meeting by giving notice of the meeting to the directors or by authorising the company secretary (if any) to give such notice.

- (2) Notice of any directors' meeting must indicate-
2. (a) its proposed date and time;
3. (b) where it is to take place; and
4. (c) if it is anticipated that directors participating in the meeting will not be in the same place, how it is proposed that they should communicate with each other during the meeting.
- (3) Notice of a directors' meeting must be given to each director, but need not be in writing.
- (4) Notice of a directors' meeting need not be given to directors who waive their entitlement to notice of that meeting, by giving notice to that effect to the company not more than 7 days after the date on which the meeting is held. Where such notice is given after the meeting has been held, that does not affect the validity of the meeting, or of any business conducted at it.

## 10. Participation in directors' meetings

- (1) Subject to the articles, directors participate in a directors' meeting, or part of a directors' meeting, when:
2. (a) the meeting has been called and takes place in accordance with the articles, and
3. (b) they can each communicate to the others any information or opinions they have on any particular item of the business of the meeting.
- (2) In determining whether directors are participating in a directors' meeting, it is irrelevant where any director is or how they communicate with each other.
- (3) If all the directors participating in a meeting are not in the same place, they may decide that the meeting is to be treated as taking place wherever any of them is.

## 11. Quorum for directors' meetings

- (1) At a directors' meeting, unless a quorum is participating, no proposal is to be voted on, except a proposal to call another meeting.
- (2) The quorum for directors' meetings may be fixed from time to time by a decision of the directors, but it must never be less than two, and unless otherwise fixed it is two.
- (3) If the total number of directors for the time being is less than the quorum required, the directors must not take any decision other than a decision:
4. (a) to appoint further directors, or
5. (b) to call a general meeting so as to enable the shareholders to appoint further directors.

## 12. Chairing of directors' meetings

- (1) The directors may appoint a director to chair their meetings.
- (2) The person so appointed for the time being is known as the chairman.
- (3) The directors may terminate the chairman's appointment at any time.
- (4) If the chairman is not participating in a directors' meeting within ten minutes of the time at which it was to start, the participating directors must appoint one of themselves to chair it.

## 13. Casting vote

- (1) If the numbers of votes for and against a proposal are equal, the chairman or other director chairing the meeting has a casting vote.
- (2) But this does not apply if, in accordance with the articles, the chairman or other director is not to be counted as participating in the decision-making process for quorum or voting purposes.

## 14. Conflicts of interest

- (1) If a proposed decision of the directors is concerned with an actual or proposed transaction or arrangement with the company in which a director is interested, that director is not to be counted as participating in the decision-making process for quorum or voting purposes.
- (2) But if paragraph (3) applies, a director who is interested in an actual or proposed transaction or arrangement with the company is to be counted as participating in the decision-making process for quorum and voting purposes.
- (3) This paragraph applies when:
4. (a) the company by ordinary resolution disapplies the provision of the articles which would otherwise prevent a director from being counted as participating in the decision-making process;
5. (b) the director's interest cannot reasonably be regarded as likely to give rise to a conflict of interest; or
6. (c) the director's conflict of interest arises from a permitted cause.
- (4) For the purposes of this article, the following are permitted causes:
8. (a) a guarantee given, or to be given, by or to a director in respect of an obligation incurred by or on behalf of the company or any of its subsidiaries;
9. (b) subscription, or an agreement to subscribe, for shares or other securities of the company or any of its subsidiaries, or to underwrite, subunderwrite, or guarantee subscription for any such shares or securities; and
10. (c) arrangements pursuant to which benefits are made available to employees and directors or former employees and directors of the company or any of its subsidiaries which do not provide special benefits for directors or former directors.

- (5) For the purposes of this article, references to proposed decisions and decision-making processes include any directors' meeting or part of a directors' meeting.
- (6) Subject to paragraph (7), if a question arises at a meeting of directors or of a committee of directors as to the right of a director to participate in the meeting (or part of the meeting) for voting or quorum purposes, the question may, before the conclusion of the meeting, be referred to the chairman whose ruling in relation to any director other than the chairman is to be final and conclusive.
- (7) If any question as to the right to participate in the meeting (or part of the meeting) should arise in respect of the chairman, the question is to be decided by a decision of the directors at that meeting, for which purpose the chairman is not to be counted as participating in the meeting (or that part of the meeting) for voting or quorum purposes.

## 15. Records of decisions to be kept

The directors must ensure that the company keeps a record, in writing, for at least 10 years from the date of the decision recorded, of every unanimous or majority decision taken by the directors.

## 16. Alternate directors and discretion to make further rules

- (1) A director may appoint any person as his alternate by depositing a notice of appointment, signed by the director, with the secretary at the registered office. Any alternate director may be removed by the company or by the board or by the director appointing him, but otherwise the office of alternative director shall continue until the next election of directors. The alternate director shall be entitled to receive notices of all meetings of directors, to attend and be counted in the quorum and vote at such meetings at which the director to whom he is the alternate is not personally present. Every person acting as an alternate director shall be subject in all respects to the provisions of these articles and shall be responsible to the company for his own acts and defaults and not deemed to be the agent of the director for whom he is the alternate.
- (2) Subject to the articles, the directors may make any rule which they think fit about how they take decisions, and about how such rules are to be recorded or communicated to directors.

## Appointment of Directors

## 17. Methods of appointing directors

- (1) Any person who is willing to act as a director, and is permitted by law to do so, may be appointed to be a director:
2. (a) by ordinary resolution, or
3. (b) by a decision of the directors.

- (2) In any case where, as a result of death, the company has no shareholders and no directors, the personal representatives of the last shareholder to have died have the right, by notice in writing, to appoint a person to be a director.
- (3) For the purposes of paragraph (2), where 2 or more shareholders die in circumstances rendering it uncertain who was the last to die, a younger shareholder is deemed to have survived an older shareholder.

## 18. Termination of director's appointment

A person ceases to be a director as soon as-

- (a) that person ceases to be a director by virtue of any provision of the companies legislation or is prohibited from being a director by law;
- (b) a bankruptcy order is made against that person;
- (c) a composition is made with that person's creditors generally in satisfaction of that person's debts;
- (d) a registered medical practitioner who is treating that person gives a written opinion to the company stating that that person has become physically or mentally incapable of acting as a director and may remain so for more than three months;
- (e) notification is received by the company from the director that the director is resigning from office, and such resignation has taken effect in accordance with its terms.

## 19. Directors' remuneration

- (1) Directors may undertake any services for the company that the directors decide.
- (2) Directors are entitled to such remuneration as the directors determine-
3. (a) for their services to the company as directors, and
4. (b) for any other service which they undertake for the company.
- (3) Subject to the articles, a director's remuneration may-
6. (a) take any form, and
7. (b) include any arrangements in connection with the payment of a pension, allowance or gratuity, or any death, sickness or disability benefits, to or in respect of that director.
- (4) Unless the directors decide otherwise, directors' remuneration accrues from day to day.
- (5) Unless the directors decide otherwise, directors are not accountable to the company for any remuneration which they receive as directors or other officers or employees of the company's subsidiaries or of any other body corporate in which the company is interested.

## 20. Directors' expenses

The company may pay any reasonable expenses which the directors properly incur in connection with their attendance at:

- (a) meetings of directors or committees of directors,
- (b) general meetings, or
- (c) separate meetings of the holders of any class of shares or of debentures of the company,

or otherwise in connection with the exercise of their powers and the discharge of their responsibilities in relation to the company.

## SHARES AND DEBENTURES Shares

21. All shares to be fully paid up
- (1) No share is to be issued for less than the aggregate of its nominal value and any premium to be paid to the company in consideration for its issue.
- (2) This does not apply to shares taken on the formation of the company by the subscribers to the company's memorandum.

## 22. Powers to issue different classes of share

- (1) Subject to the articles, but without prejudice to the rights attached to any existing share, the company may issue shares with such rights or restrictions as may be determined by ordinary resolution.
- (2) The company may issue shares which are to be redeemed, or are liable to be redeemed at the option of the company or the holder, and the directors may determine the terms, conditions and manner of redemption of any such shares.

## 23. Company not bound by less than absolute interests

Except as required by law, no person is to be recognised by the company as holding any share upon any trust, and except as otherwise required by law or the articles, the company is not in any way to be bound by or recognise any interest in a share other than the holder's absolute ownership of it and all the rights attaching to it.

## 24. Share certificates

- (1) The company must issue each shareholder, free of charge, with one or more certificates in respect of the shares which that shareholder holds.
- (2) Every certificate must specify:
3. (a) in respect of how many shares, of what class, it is issued;
4. (b) the nominal value of those shares;
5. (c) that the shares are fully paid; and
6. (d) any distinguishing numbers assigned to them.

- (3) No certificate may be issued in respect of shares of more than one class.
- (4) If more than one person holds a share, only one certificate may be issued in respect of it.
- (5) Certificates must have affixed to them the company's common seal, or be otherwise executed in accordance with the companies legislation.

## 25. Replacement share certificates

- (1) If a certificate issued in respect of a shareholder's shares is:
2. (a) damaged or defaced, or
3. (b) said to be lost, stolen or destroyed,

that shareholder is entitled to be issued with a replacement certificate in respect of the same shares.

- (2) A shareholder exercising the right to be issued with such a replacement certificate-
2. (a) may at the same time exercise the right to be issued with a single certificate or separate certificates;
3. (b) must return the certificate which is to be replaced to the company if it is damaged or defaced; and
4. (c) must comply with such conditions as to evidence, indemnity and the payment of a reasonable fee as the directors decide.

## 26. Share transfers

- (1) Shares may be transferred by means of an instrument of transfer in any usual form or any other form approved by the directors, which is executed by or on behalf of the transferor.
- (2) No fee may be charged for registering any instrument of transfer or other document relating to or affecting the title to any share.
- (3) The company may retain any instrument of transfer which is registered.
- (4) The transferor remains the holder of a share until the transferee's name is entered in the register of members as holder of it.
- (5) The directors may refuse to register the transfer of a share, and if they do so, the instrument of transfer must be returned to the transferee with the notice of refusal unless they suspect that the proposed transfer may be fraudulent.

## 27. Transmission of shares

- (1) If title to a share passes to a transmittee, the company may only recognise the transmittee as having any title to that share.

- (2) A transmittee who produces such evidence of entitlement to shares as the directors may properly require-
2. (a) may, subject to the articles, choose either to become the holder of those shares or to have them transferred to another person, and
3. (b) subject to the articles, and pending any transfer of the shares to another person, has the same rights as the holder had.
- (3) But transmittees do not have the right to attend or vote at a general meeting, or agree to a proposed written resolution, in respect of shares to which they are entitled, by reason of the holder's death or bankruptcy or otherwise, unless they become the holders of those shares.

## 28. Exercise of transmittees' rights

- (1) Transmittees who wish to become the holders of shares to which they have become entitled must notify the company in writing of that wish.
- (2) If the transmittee wishes to have a share transferred to another person, the transmittee must execute an instrument of transfer in respect of it.
- (3) Any transfer made or executed under this article is to be treated as if it were made or executed by the person from whom the transmittee has derived rights in respect of the share, and as if the event which gave rise to the transmission had not occurred.

## 29. Transmittees bound by prior notices

If a notice is given to a shareholder in respect of shares and a transmittee is entitled to those shares, the transmittee is bound by the notice if it was given to the shareholder before the transmittee's name has been entered in the register of members.

## Dividends and Other Distributions

## 30. Procedure for declaring dividends

- (1) The company may by ordinary resolution declare dividends, and the directors may decide to pay interim dividends.
- (2) A dividend must not be declared unless the directors have made a recommendation as to its amount. Such a dividend must not exceed the amount recommended by the directors.
- (3) No dividend may be declared or paid unless it is in accordance with shareholders' respective rights.
- (4) Unless the shareholders' resolution to declare or directors' decision to pay a dividend, or the terms on which shares are issued, specify otherwise, it must be paid by reference to each shareholder's holding of shares on the date of the resolution or decision to declare or pay it.
- (5) If the company's share capital is divided into different classes, no interim dividend may be paid on shares carrying deferred or non-preferred rights if, at the time of payment, any preferential dividend is in arrear.

- (6) The directors may pay at intervals any dividend payable at a fixed rate if it appears to them that the profits available for distribution justify the payment.
- (7) If the directors act in good faith, they do not incur any liability to the holders of shares conferring preferred rights for any loss they may suffer by the lawful payment of an interim dividend on shares with deferred or non-preferred rights.

## 31. Payment of dividends and other distributions

- (1) Where a dividend or other sum which is a distribution is payable in respect of a share, it must be paid by one or more of the following means:
2. (a) transfer to a bank or building society account specified by the distribution recipient either in writing or as the directors may otherwise decide;
3. (b) sending a cheque made payable to the distribution recipient by post to the distribution recipient at the distribution recipient's registered address (if the distribution recipient is a holder of the share), or (in any other case) to an address specified by the distribution recipient either in writing or as the directors may otherwise decide;
4. (c) sending a cheque made payable to such person by post to such person at such address as the distribution recipient has specified either in writing or as the directors may otherwise decide; or
5. (d) any other means of payment as the directors agree with the distribution recipient either in writing or by such other means as the directors decide.
- (2) In the articles, 'the distribution recipient' means, in respect of a share in respect of which a dividend or other sum is payable-
7. (a) the holder of the share; or
8. (b) if the share has two or more joint holders, whichever of them is named first in the register of members; or
9. (c) if the holder is no longer entitled to the share by reason of death or bankruptcy, or otherwise by operation of law, the transmittee.

## 32. No interest on distributions

The company may not pay interest on any dividend or other sum payable in respect of a share unless otherwise provided by-

- (a) the terms on which the share was issued, or
- (b) the provisions of another agreement between the holder of that share and the company.

## 33. Unclaimed distributions

- (1) All dividends or other sums which are:
2. (a) payable in respect of shares, and
3. (b) unclaimed after having been declared or become payable,

may be invested or otherwise made use of by the directors for the benefit of the company until claimed.

- (2) The payment of any such dividend or other sum into a separate account does not make the company a trustee in respect of it.
- (3) If:
3. (a) twelve years have passed from the date on which a dividend or other sum became due for payment, and
4. (b) the distribution recipient has not claimed it,

the distribution recipient is no longer entitled to that dividend or other sum and it ceases to remain owing by the company.

## 34. Non-cash distributions

- (1) Subject to the terms of issue of the share in question, the company may, by ordinary resolution on the recommendation of the directors, decide to pay all or part of a dividend or other distribution payable in respect of a share by transferring non-cash assets of equivalent value (including, without limitation, shares or other securities in any company).
- (2) For the purposes of paying a non-cash distribution, the directors may make whatever arrangements they think fit, including, where any difficulty arises regarding the distribution-
3. (a) fixing the value of any assets;
4. (b) paying cash to any distribution recipient on the basis of that value in order to adjust the rights of recipients; and
5. (c) vesting any assets in trustees.

## 35. Waiver of distributions

Distribution recipients may waive their entitlement to a dividend or other distribution payable in respect of a share by giving the company notice in writing to that effect, but if:

- (a) the share has more than one holder, or
- (b) more than one person is entitled to the share, whether by reason of the death or bankruptcy of one or more joint holders, or otherwise,

the notice is not effective unless it is expressed to be given, and signed, by all the holders or persons otherwise entitled to the share.

## Capitalisation of Profits

## 36. Authority to capitalise and appropriation of capitalised sums

- (1) Subject to the articles, the directors may, if they are so authorised by an ordinary resolution-
2. (a) decide to capitalise any profits of the company (whether or not they are available for distribution) which are not required for paying a preferential

dividend, or any sum standing to the credit of the company's share premium account or capital redemption reserve; and

- (b) appropriate any sum which they so decide to capitalise (a 'capitalised sum') to the persons who would have been entitled to it if it were distributed by way of dividend (the 'persons entitled') and in the same proportions.
- (2) Capitalised sums must be applied-
- (a) on behalf of the persons entitled, and
- (b) in the same proportions as a dividend would have been distributed to them.
- (3) Any capitalised sum may be applied in paying up new shares of a nominal amount equal to the capitalised sum which are then allotted credited as fully paid to the persons entitled or as they may direct.
- (4) A capitalised sum which was appropriated from profits available for distribution may be applied in paying up new debentures of the company which are then allotted credited as fully paid to the persons entitled or as they may direct.
- (5) Subject to the articles the directors may:
- (a) apply capitalised sums in accordance with paragraphs (3) and (4) partly in one way and partly in another;
- (b) make such arrangements as they think fit to deal with shares or debentures becoming distributable in fractions under this article (including the issuing of fractional certificates or the making of cash payments); and
- (c) authorise any person to enter into an agreement with the company on behalf of all the persons entitled which is binding on them in respect of the allotment of shares and debentures to them under this article.

## DECISION MAKING BY SHAREHOLDERS Organisation of General Meetings

## 37. Convening, attending and speaking at general meetings

- (1A) The directors may, whenever they think fit, convene an extraordinary [special] general meeting. Alternatively any member(s) holding at least 10% of the issued share capital of the Company may convene an extraordinary [special] general meeting in the same manner as nearly as possible as that in which meetings may be convened by the directors.
- (1) A person is able to exercise the right to speak at a general meeting when that person is in a position to communicate to all those attending the meeting, during the meeting, any information or opinions which that person has on the business of the meeting.

- (2) A person is able to exercise the right to vote at a general meeting when:
2. (a) that person is able to vote, during the meeting, on resolutions put to the vote at the meeting, and
3. (b) that person's vote can be taken into account in determining whether or not such resolutions are passed at the same time as the votes of all the other persons attending the meeting.
- (3) The directors may make whatever arrangements they consider appropriate to enable those attending a general meeting to exercise their rights to speak or vote at it.
- (4) In determining attendance at a general meeting, it is immaterial whether any two or more members attending it are in the same place as each other.
- (5) Two or more persons who are not in the same place as each other attend a general meeting if their circumstances are such that if they have (or were to have) rights to speak and vote at that meeting, they are (or would be) able to exercise them.

## 38. Quorum for general meetings

No business other than the appointment of the chairman of the meeting is to be transacted at a general meeting if the persons attending it do not constitute a quorum.

## 39. Chairing general meetings

- (1) If the directors have appointed a chairman, the chairman shall chair general meetings if present and willing to do so.
- (2) If the directors have not appointed a chairman, or if the chairman is unwilling to chair the meeting or is not present within ten minutes of the time at which a meeting was due to start-
3. (a) the directors present, or
4. (b) (if no directors are present), the meeting,

must appoint a director or shareholder to chair the meeting, and the appointment of the chairman of the meeting must be the first business of the meeting.

- (3) The person chairing a meeting in accordance with this article is referred to as 'the chairman of the meeting' .

## 40. Attendance and speaking by directors and non-shareholders

- (1) Directors may attend and speak at general meetings, whether or not they are shareholders.

- (2) The chairman of the meeting may permit other persons who are not:
2. (a) shareholders of the company, or
3. (b) otherwise entitled to exercise the rights of shareholders in relation to general meetings,

to attend and speak at a general meeting.

## 41. Adjournment

- (1) If the persons attending a general meeting within half an hour of the time at which the meeting was due to start do not constitute a quorum, or if during a meeting a quorum ceases to be present, the chairman of the meeting must adjourn it.
- (2) The chairman of the meeting may adjourn a general meeting at which a quorum is present if:
3. (a) the meeting consents to an adjournment, or
4. (b) it appears to the chairman of the meeting that an adjournment is necessary to protect the safety of any person attending the meeting or ensure that the business of the meeting is conducted in an orderly manner.
- (3) The chairman of the meeting must adjourn a general meeting if directed to do so by the meeting.
- (4) When adjourning a general meeting, the chairman of the meeting must-
7. (a) either specify the time and place to which it is adjourned or state that it is to continue at a time and place to be fixed by the directors, and
8. (b) have regard to any directions as to the time and place of any adjournment which have been given by the meeting.
- (5) If the continuation of an adjourned meeting is to take place more than 14 days after it was adjourned, the company must give at least 7 clear days' notice of it (that is, excluding the day of the adjourned meeting and the day on which the notice is given):
10. (a) to the same persons to whom notice of the company's general meetings is required to be given, and
11. (b) containing the same information which such notice is required to contain.
- (6) No business may be transacted at an adjourned general meeting which could not properly have been transacted at the meeting if the adjournment had not taken place.

## 42. Voting: general

- (1) A resolution put to the vote of a general meeting must be decided on a show of hands unless a poll is duly demanded in accordance with the articles.
- (2) Except in the case of removal of auditors, anything that may be done by resolution of the company in general meeting may, without a meeting and without prior notice being required, be done by resolution in writing signed by all the shareholders of the company who are entitled to attend a meeting and vote on the resolution. A resolution in writing made in accordance with this article is valid as if it had been passed by the company in general meeting and the date thereof is the date when the resolution is signed by the last shareholder.

## 43. Errors and disputes

- (1) No objection may be raised to the qualification of any person voting at a general meeting except at the meeting or adjourned meeting at which the vote objected to is tendered, and every vote not disallowed at the meeting is valid.
- (2) Any such objection must be referred to the chairman of the meeting, whose decision is final.

## 44. Poll votes

- (1) A poll on a resolution may be demanded:
2. (a) in advance of the general meeting where it is to be put to the vote, or
3. (b) at a general meeting, either before a show of hands on that resolution or immediately after the result of a show of hands on that resolution is declared.
- (2) A poll may be demanded by:
5. (a) the chairman of the meeting;
6. (b) the directors;
7. (c) two or more persons having the right to vote on the resolution; or
8. (d) a person or persons representing not less than one tenth of the total voting rights of all the shareholders having the right to vote on the resolution.
- (3) A demand for a poll may be withdrawn if:
10. (a) the poll has not yet been taken, and
11. (b) the chairman of the meeting consents to the withdrawal.
- (4) Polls must be taken immediately and in such manner as the chairman of the meeting directs.

## Voting at General Meetings

## 45. Content of proxy notices

- (1) Proxies may only validly be appointed by a notice in writing (a 'proxy notice') which:
2. (a) states the name and address of the shareholder appointing the proxy;
3. (b) identifies the person appointed to be that shareholder's proxy and the general meeting in relation to which that person is appointed;
4. (c) is signed by or on behalf of the shareholder appointing the proxy, or is authenticated in such manner as the directors may determine; and
5. (d) is delivered to the company in accordance with the articles and any instructions contained in the notice of the general meeting to which they relate.
- (2) The company may require proxy notices to be delivered in a particular form, and may specify different forms for different purposes.
- (3) Proxy notices may specify how the proxy appointed under them is to vote (or that the proxy is to abstain from voting) on one or more resolutions.
- (4) Unless a proxy notice indicates otherwise, it must be treated as:
9. (a) allowing the person appointed under it as a proxy discretion as to how to vote  on any ancillary or procedural resolutions put to the meeting, and
10. (b) appointing that person as a proxy in relation to any adjournment of the general meeting to which it relates as well as the meeting itself.

## 46. Delivery of proxy notices

- (1) A person who is entitled to attend, speak or vote (either on a show of hands or on a poll) at a general meeting remains so entitled in respect of that meeting or any adjournment of it, even though a valid proxy notice has been delivered to the company by or on behalf of that person.
- (2) An appointment under a proxy notice may be revoked by delivering to the company a notice in writing given by or on behalf of the person by whom or on whose behalf the proxy notice was given.
- (3) A notice revoking a proxy appointment only takes effect if it is delivered before the start of the meeting or adjourned meeting to which it relates.
- (4) If a proxy notice is not executed by the person appointing the proxy, it must be accompanied by written evidence of the authority of the person who executed it to execute it on the appointor's behalf.

## 47. Amendments to resolutions

- (1) An ordinary resolution to be proposed at a general meeting may be amended by ordinary resolution if:
2. (a) notice of the proposed amendment is given to the company in writing by a person entitled to vote at the general meeting at which it is to be proposed not less than 48 hours before the meeting is to take place (or such later time as the chairman of the meeting may determine), and

- (b) the proposed amendment does not, in the reasonable opinion of the chairman of the meeting, materially alter the scope of the resolution.
- (2) A special resolution to be proposed at a general meeting may be amended by ordinary resolution, if:
- (a) the chairman of the meeting proposes the amendment at the general meeting at which the resolution is to be proposed, and
- (b) the amendment does not go beyond what is necessary to correct a grammatical or other non-substantive error in the resolution.
- (3) If the chairman of the meeting, acting in good faith, wrongly decides that an amendment to a resolution is out of order, the chairman's error does not invalidate the vote on that resolution.

- (1) Subject to the articles, anything sent or supplied by or to the company under the articles may be sent or supplied in the following manner:

(a) Subject to the requirements of the companies legislation relating to [special] resolutions, a general meeting of the company may be convened by giving at least 14 clear days' notice in writing (exclusive of the day on which the notice is served or deemed to be served, and of the day for which notice is given) specifying the place, the day and hour of meeting and, in case of special business, the general nature of that business shall be given in the manner provided sub-clause (2) below, to such persons as are entitled to receive such notices from the company. - (1) Any common seal may only be used by the authority of the directors.

## Appendix 2:

## Distinguishing features of a company and partnership, public and private companies

## 1. Company compared to a partnership

The essential differences between a company and a partnership are as follows.

- Unlike a company, a partnership is not a separate legal entity.
- A partnership is usually created by a private contract whereas a company has to be created by legal process (called incorporation) prescribed by statute.
- Partners share the profits generated by the partnership equally (unless the partnership agreement provides otherwise) and are jointly and severally liable for the partnership's debts. In a company the profits of the company may be retained and ploughed back into the business or distributed. If distributed the distributable profits are shared between the members according to the number and type of shares owned. The liability of shareholders for the debts of a company is limited; it only extends to the amount (if any) that they agree to pay for the shares they have received when issued. The company is liable in full for its own debts.
- A partnership is automatically dissolved following the death or retirement of one of the partners whereas a company has perpetual succession and its continuation is not affected by the death or retirement of a member or by the transfer of member's shares.
- Partnership property is vested in the names of the partners. In a company the property is vested in the name of the company (not in the name of the members or officers).
- The partners usually manage the business of the partnership as agents and generally have equal rights of management. In a company the management is exercised by the board of directors.
- A partnership often has limited borrowing powers whereas a company has unlimited powers to raise capital from the public to finance its business. A knock-on effect of this is that there are usually no restrictions on the withdrawal of capital from a partnership but in a company there are specific restrictions and requirements relating to the distribution of capital (the so-called capital maintenance doctrine, reviewed in detail in Module 6).
- Income tax is often imposed on the profits of the partners of a partnership and not on the partnership itself. Corporation tax on the profits of a company is usually imposed upon the entity itself and not on the members or officers of the company.

In this preliminary analysis we have distinguished companies and partnerships generally.

Before we leave the subject of partnerships, a brief word should be included about limited partnerships. In recent years a new form of partnership has developed, mainly in offshore centres, which provides an element of limited liability. Limited partnerships are unincorporated partnerships that generally comprise two types of partners.

- General partners: the general partners manage the partnership business and their liability for the debts of the partnership is unlimited (in the usual way).
- Limited partners: the limited partners take a passive role in the management of the partnership. Their liability is limited to the amount set out in the partnership agreement.

A limited partnership is usually treated as a separate entity in the centre where it is created, although it retains an element of tax transparency that can create possible financial planning benefits.

Limited partnerships are used as collective investment vehicles and as a quasi trust, where the limited partners assume a role similar to that of beneficiaries and the general partners have powers and duties similar to those of a trustee.

A limited liability partnership ('LLP') is a further variation on the theme of a general partnership. The liability of the partners is limited to the assets of the partnership, which means that the individual partners are not liable for the debts of the LLP .

## 2. Public compared to a private company

The essential differences between a public company and a private company relate to ownership and management.

## a. Ownership

In general terms a public company is one whose shares are offered for sale to the general public. There is no restriction on ownership. Public companies are often listed on a recognised stock exchange and their shares are freely traded. In view of this, certain regulatory and accounting requirements have to be fulfilled by public companies in order to provide an element of investor and shareholder protection.

Private companies, on the other hand, are companies that impose a restriction on share ownership and, in general terms, ownership is not made available to the general public. There is often a restriction upon a shareholder's freedom to transfer his shares. There is therefore no ready market for the shares of a private company.

As a result the regulations and requirements imposed on private companies are not as onerous as those imposed on public companies.

## b. Management and control

A public company is often very large. It has a significant number of shareholders, but they do not run the business. This is undertaken by a full time board of executive directors and sometimes also part time non-executive directors).

In contrast, a private company is much smaller and the directors and shareholders are often the same persons.

The vast majority of companies incorporated in offshore financial centres are private companies. These provide considerable financial planning opportunities for international clients. Offshore public companies do exist but they are not widely used. Corporate issues relating to public companies are probably best handled by attorneys.

## Appendix 3:

## Lifting the veil of incorporation

## The veil of incorporation

1. There are two well-established principles of company law enshrined in Salomon v A Salomon and Co. Ltd . 20 Firstly, a company is a completely separate entity from its shareholders who are said to exist on the other side of the veil of incorporation. Secondly, as a consequence, the liability of shareholders for the company's debts is limited to the amount they have paid, or agreed to pay, for their shares when they were issued. Accordingly, if shareholders have paid the full issue price when shares were acquired from the company, they cannot be held liable to pay any debts incurred by the company. The company is liable for its own debts; not the shareholders. The shareholders can lose their investment, but no more, in the event that the company becomes insolvent and unable to pay its own creditors. From a creditor's perspective, a creditor of a company can only enforce payment of the debts against the company and cannot go behind the corporate veil to enforce payment against its shareholders.

## Lift the veil of incorporation

2. When and in what circumstances does the common law permit a creditor of a company to lift the veil of incorporation? In other words, are there circumstances where the courts are prepared to ignore the separate legal personality of a company? If there are such circumstances, it will have two consequences. Firstly, it will permit thereby a creditor to impose personal liability upon the company's shareholders. Secondly, it will permit the creditors of a shareholder to enforce payment against the assets of the company.
3. The leading case of Adams v Cape Industries and the more modern case of VTB Capital plc v Nutritek International Corp analysed the very limited circumstances when a court would be prepared to lift the veil of incorporation, distinguishing circumstances that involve fraud and circumstances where a parent can be held liable for the debts of a subsidiary. 21

## Fraud

4. A court may lift the corporate veil and disregard a company's separate personality when the company:
2. (a) is clearly a 'sham' or 'mere façade' and
3. (b) has been used fraudulently in order to avoid an existing legal obligation.

- 4.1 Example 1: In Jones v Lipman a court ordered A to transfer certain property to B. Whereupon, in order to avoid such legal obligation, A transferred the property to a company of which A was sole shareholder. The court was prepared to lift the veil of incorporation and permit B to enforce the original obligation against the property of the company. 22
- 4.2 Example 2: In Gencor ACP Ltd v Dalby a director of a public company (Company A) dishonestly stole money from the company and transferred it to Company B, a private company owned and controlled by him. Company A obtained judgment against the director, but the court permitted it to enforce the judgment against the assets of Company B. It was held that Company B was a mere façade and had been formed by the director in order to avoid fraudulently his existing liability to Company A. 23
- 4.3 Example 3: In Wallersteiner v Moir a complex corporate structure was established by a single person in order to hold money that he had fraudulently obtained by deception. The court allowed the corporate veil to be lifted on the basis that the corporate entities were mere 'fronts' or a façade, and were designed deliberately to avoid to that person's legal obligations. The network of companies were dismantled so that the corporate assets were deemed to belong to the dishonest shareholder; the creditors of the shareholder could then attach the assets of the corporate entities. 24

## When a parent can be held liable for the debts of a subsidiary

5. The starting point in the analysis of whether the corporate veil of a subsidiary can be lifted in order to render its parent liable for the debts of its subsidiary is this: each member within a group of companies is a separate legal entity in its own right, with completely separate rights and liabilities from other entities or members of the group.

Nevertheless, it was held in Cape that where the group is operated as a 'single economic unit' , a court can ignore the separate legal entities and treat them as one. But, in reality, it is extremely difficult (some may say almost impossible) to prove that each member of the group of companies is a single economic unit when they have, for example: a different board, their own bank accounts, their own property and contractual rights, have their own accounting records and prepare their own financials, etc. For example, it was stated in Atlas Maritime Co. SA. v Avalon Maritime Ltd, the Coral Rose that:

'The creation or purchase of a subsidiary company with minimal liability, which will operate with the parent's funds and on the parent's directions but not expose the parent to liability may not seem to some the most honest way of trading. But it is extremely common in the international shipping industry and elsewhere [namely in the world of commerce at large]. To hold that it creates as agency relationship between subsidiary and parent [or forms a single economic unit] would be a revolutionary doctrine.' 25

## United States' law

6. In these circumstances it is pretty exceptional indeed for an English court to be prepared to lift the veil of incorporation. This is in contrast to US law which has departed from English common law principles, particularly when a private company has been established and carries on business, incurring thereby debts and becoming insolvent principally because it has always been hopelessly under-capitalised by its owner. For example, the owner has financed the business by very little of his own money, but an enormous amount of debt. In these circumstances a court in the US would much more readily be prepared to lift the veil to permit creditors of the company to enforce payments of the debt from the company's shareholder personally.

## Appendix 6A:

## Types of company around the offshore world, generally

## 1. Resident companies

Resident companies (sometimes referred to as local or domestic ordinary companies, depending upon the jurisdiction and context in which the phrase is used) are usually the only type of corporate entity that is permitted to conduct business locally, within the local economy. They are required to meet local filing and regulatory requirements that are usually more onerous than those imposed on other types of company which conduct business overseas rather than locally.

## 2. Tax-exempt companies

Companies incorporated in certain low-tax Crown Dependencies were able to relinquish their local resident status and instead apply to become exempt from tax in the local centre. When a company receives its new tax status, it is generally referred to as a tax-exempt company. These tax-exempt companies were abolished when low-tax jurisdictions introduced a zero rate of corporate tax - see Appendix 4A in respect of Jersey, Guernsey, Isle of Man and Gibraltar.

## 3. International companies

These should not be confused with IBCs, despite the use of the name 'International' in the title. International companies were an extension of the principle of the tax-exempt company and were available in Alderney, Gibraltar, Guernsey, Jersey and the Isle of Man. They were similar in nature to tax-exempt companies in that a locally incorporated company was able to relinquish its resident status in favour of an alternative tax treatment. The requirements to be met in order for a company to obtain international status were similar to those that apply to exempt companies.

The essential difference between tax-exempt and international companies was in relation to the amount of tax that an international company could elect to pay. Instead of paying a nominal amount of duty, the extent of which usually equates to the amount of the exempt company tax, an international company may instead elect to pay tax locally at a fixed rate, usually somewhere between 0.5% and 35%.

Why would an offshore company voluntarily elect to pay tax? And, if it could choose the rate of tax, what rate should it choose?

The answer to these questions are found in the complex world of double tax treaties. Dividends paid by international companies are generally deemed to have been made after the deduction of the standard rate of corporate tax that applies in the centre concerned. In addition, international companies are not required to withhold tax from distributions made to non-residents. An onshore taxpayer can, in these circumstances, take advantage of the tax credit under model double-taxation treaties.

These companies have been abolished following OECD and EU pressure - see Appendix 4A.

## 4. Non-resident companies

In recent years a few offshore centres have offered what have been termed non resident companies . These were companies incorporated locally but that, rather than take the residence of their place of incorporation, applied to be treated as non resident in the centre concerned.

In the offshore financial centres where this type of company was initially offered (the Isle of Man and Ireland are the most commonly quoted examples), non-resident companies were not assessed to tax on their income and profits, unless received from local sources (bank interest excepted). In return for this favourable treatment, a nonresident company was required to pay a nominal annual duty to the local revenue authorities, although the duty is usually at a rate higher than the duty payable by tax-exempt companies in the same centre. The key feature of a non-resident company was that management and control had to take place outside the offshore centre. This usually meant that local directors were not appointed and, instead, the client, or nominees of the client, would manage the company. The knock-on effect of this was that often the affairs of a non-resident company would not be known to the service provider and there would be no one locally who could be held accountable for the actions of the company.

This, of course, developed into a regulator's nightmare as, perhaps not unexpectedly, incidents began to materialise where a non-resident company was involved in fraudulent or criminal use. In the Isle of Man and Ireland non-resident companies threatened the reputation of those centres and steps were taken to reduce, and eventually outlaw, the use of this type of vehicle.

The Manx and Irish non-resident company should not be confused with the Caymanian variety. The Cayman ordinary non-resident company is a private limited company but one that cannot carry on business within the Cayman Islands and is therefore not subject to local shareholding requirements. It is nevertheless managed and controlled within the Cayman Islands.

## 5. International Business Companies ('IBCs')

The IBC is very common in offshore financial centres located in the Caribbean and other 'nil-tax' centres. Examples of the centres that offer IBCs include Antigua, Anguilla, the Bahamas, Barbados, Belize, British Virgin Islands (now called BVI BCs), Niue, St. Vincent and the Seychelles.

In some centres, such as the Cook Islands, Mauritius and Samoa, corporate vehicles that have characteristics similar to those of the IBCs are known as International Companies. In Bermuda, the Cayman Islands and Turks and Caicos Islands there is a similar vehicle known as an exempted company. In this context 'exempted' refers to the fact that they are exempt from the requirement to be majority owned by Caymanian citizens.

The following are features of these vehicles.

- Single and corporate directors are allowed.
- Single members are allowed, subscribers do not have to take up any shares, and shareholder resolutions are passed by majority vote.
- A company secretary is not required (but if one is voluntarily appointed it may be a corporate secretary).
- Directors need not be resident in the offshore centre of incorporation and there are no rules regarding frequency and location of director's meetings.
- Annual general meetings of shareholders are not required, nor are accounts required to be laid before the members at an AGM.
- An annual return is not required, but an annual duty is payable to the Registrar of Companies.
- A registered agent is required, that is, an agent who must reside in the jurisdiction of incorporation and who is responsible for ensuring that the local company law requirements are adhered to.
- Limited information is required by the Registrar of Companies (memorandum and articles upon incorporation together with the registered office and record of shareholding). Even less information is public.
- They are not permitted to trade in the offshore centre, although this prohibition is being relaxed under OECD pressure - see Module 3, section 3.2.4.
- IBCs enjoy modern flexible capital maintenance provisions that enable the company to distribute capital surplus (what the British call share premiums).

## 6. Limited Duration Companies ('LDCs')

This type of company had its origins in the company laws of the US. The Internal Revenue Service has issued favourable tax rulings stating that an LDC should be treated as a partnership for tax purposes. This provides tax planning opportunities for US citizens and corporations.

An LDC enjoys the usual characteristics of a registered company but has a limited duration, generally 30 years or until the completion of a specified task or activity set out in the company's articles of association.

It is usual for the members of an LDC to manage the affairs of the company, and any income or realised gains are assessed on the members, rather than on the company itself. This transparency for tax purposes can create a number of possible financial planning opportunities as, unlike a partnership, an LDC is a separate legal entity with limited liability for its members. However, as the gains and income are assessed on the individual members, their personal reliefs and allowances, which might otherwise have gone unused, are available to set off against the taxes payable.

## 7. Companies limited by guarantee

A guarantee company is one that does not have share capital. Instead, persons are elected to membership and are asked to contribute to the assets of the company in the event that the company is wound up. The members pay an entry subscription that comprises the initial capital of the company and may be asked to pay further subscriptions if required in the future. As indicated in Module 3, this type of company is often used for undertakings that do not expect to distribute any profits. They are available in most offshore jurisdictions.

## 8. Hybrid companies

A hybrid company is an extension of the guarantee company, as it is one that is limited by both shares and guarantee.

The hybrid has a board of directors and members. However, the members are required to contribute to the capital of the company and, in return, they acquire rights that are pro rata to the contributions they have made. They are issued with shares but are required to contribute further funds should the company go into liquidation as a result of insolvency.

In recent years, hybrid companies have increased in popularity particularly as a form of a 'corporate trust' or quasi foundation. This particular use of a hybrid company appeals to clients from civil law countries, where the trust concept is not widely accepted but the use of companies is well known and understood.

The following is an outline of how a hybrid company may be structured so as to take on some of the features of a trust.

- First, the articles of association are drafted in such a way that the management and control of the company rests with the directors as well as those members who have voting shares. Neither the directors nor the shareholders have the right to receive distributions from the company, although the shareholders have wide voting powers.
- Secondly, the rights of the guarantee members are such that, under the articles, they are entitled to receive dividends and loan payments from the company (provided, of course, that dividends are declared or loans agreed). The guarantee members, however, have limited voting rights, if any at all.

By structuring the company in this manner, the directors and voting shareholders have roles that are similar in nature to those of trustees of a trust. Similarly, the guarantee members occupy a position similar to that of the beneficiaries of a discretionary trust, as they receive benefits whenever the directors exercise their dispositive powers in their favour. The similarity to a trust can be extended further if the articles provide for the appointment of a person whose role is to oversee the actions of the directors. This person acts in a similar way to a protector and the articles should specify whether he owes fiduciary duties or not.

Although this type of arrangement assumes many of the key features of a trust, it should be remembered that the hybrid is nevertheless a company and therefore enjoys the special characteristics of a company, such as perpetual succession, separate personality and, of course, limited liability.

## 9. Protected cell companies ('PCCs')

This type of company is a relatively new concept, having developed in Guernsey under the provisions of the Protected Cell Companies Ordinance 1997 in response to the requirements of the local mutual fund industry and certain tax planning opportunities. They are now also available in other jurisdictions. 26

PCCs have many of the usual features and characteristics of a limited company but the main difference is that their capital is divided up into separate parts, or cells, which are designed to segregate and protect the assets of the company from attachment by creditors and other claimants.

The theory is that, if the assets held in one cell are attached by creditors of the company, the assets in the other cells are protected because of the segregation of the capital. This can create benefits for those who create offshore mutual funds or other types of collective investment schemes, as the capital may be divided into separate cells. Each cell can then be operated as a separate sub-fund within the collective investment fund, thus enabling parts of the fund to invest in high-risk assets while preserving the protection of the other parts of the fund where increased risk is not acceptable.

These companies can also be used to avoid onshore controlled foreign company anti tax avoidance legislation (but it is impossible, in practice, to obtain a definitive opinion upon their effectiveness).

## 10. Companies in high-tax centres

Some companies incorporated in certain high-tax centres may also provide financial planning opportunities for non-residents. The following is a summary of the features of certain types of corporate entity that may be incorporated in Luxembourg, Switzerland and Delaware.

## 10.1 Luxembourg 1929 Holding Companies

The two most common types of corporate entity in Luxembourg are the Société Anonyme ('SA') and the Société à Responsabilité Limitée ('SARL'). However, the most common type of company for non-residents is the 1929 Holding Company, which can either be an SA or a SARL (although most are SAs).

A 1929 Holding Company must fulfil certain requirements, including a minimum paid-up capital of FLUX 1,000,000 (or the Euro equivalent). In addition, the sole object of the company must be to hold participating interests in other undertakings. Provided these requirements are met, the company is not liable to taxes in Luxembourg, nor are distributions subject to withholding taxes.

This type of company can be used for a variety of purposes, including holding investment portfolios, patents and trademarks, gold and precious metals, foreign currencies or interests in companies that own real estate.

Holding companies can also be used to act as the central holding company in a financial planning structure and a finance company for a group of companies.

## 10.2 Swiss companies

There are three main types of Swiss company that may be formed for use as a taxefficient asset-holding vehicle.

- A company that has a registered office in Switzerland but that does not conduct any commercial activities, nor have any permanent office staff in Switzerland, is classified as a domiciliary company . As such it is only liable to pay tax at the Federal level (and not at the Cantonal and Communal levels that apply to most other types of Swiss company).
- Service companies (companies with an administrative office in Switzerland that may provide a service to other companies within its group) are granted taxation benefits in so far as they are usually allocated a concessionary rate of tax payable upon 'service income' .
- Auxilliary companies are companies that have a presence in Switzerland but do not engage in commercial activities within the geographical jurisdiction of Switzerland. This type of company is also granted favourable tax benefits.

## 10.3 Delaware Limited Liability Company ('LLC')

In summary, a company is a separate legal entity, owned by its shareholders, managed by its directors, and which is liable to pay corporation tax in its state of incorporation. A US LLC is a statutory variation, first enacted in Wyoming in 1977 based upon the German GmbH. LLCs are available in a number of states in the US, but most are incorporated in the state of Delaware. An LLC is a separate legal entity offering its shareholders limited liability, but it is managed and taxed in a completely different way. There is no equivalent corporate structure in the UK or elsewhere in the offshore world. The closest comparable entity is the limited partnership.

## Management

An LLC does not have a board of directors. The company is managed by its shareholders, called members , of which there are two types: the ordinary members and the managing member. The managing member is appointed by the members and is given all powers of management of the business of the company on a day-to-day basis. The LLC has Articles of Organisation but, in addition, it is typical for the members and managing member to enter into an Operating Agreement - this is an agreement that sets out internal rules governing such matters as membership rights, management powers and duties, operating procedures, matters relating to distribution of profits, etc.

## Taxation

When formed the LLC can elect to be taxed for US tax purposes as a C Corporation (and become liable to US corporation tax upon its worldwide profits from every source) or an S Corporation (and be treated for US tax purposes as a partnership). Most holding structures elect to be taxed as an S Corporation. In these circumstances the company files a Tax Return that reports profits, but is treated as pass-through , i.e. the company is not subject to corporation tax in the US and all annual profits are attributed to the members.

## Appendix 6B: Types of company available in selected jurisdictions

## 1. The Bahamas

The Bahamas are a nil-tax jurisdiction insofar as they do not impose corporation or withholding taxes upon companies, nor income taxes etc. upon individuals.

The main corporate entity incorporated in the Bahamas and used for offshore asset holding purposes is the IBC, incorporated under the International Business Companies Act 2000 as last amended in 2014 ('IBCA'). This legislation is copied from the old BVI legislation of the same name, the IBCA 1984, itself sharing some of the features of the English model as well as US company legislation. The IBCA did not supersede the existing companies law when first introduced in 1989, but complemented it insofar as it exists alongside the traditional companies legislation, most recently restated in the Companies Act 1992. The Companies Act 1992 is based upon the English 1948 Act and permits the incorporation of companies to carry on business in the Bahamas - they can be public or private, unlimited, limited by shares or guarantee. The Bahamas have also passed additional companies legislation for the provision of special purpose corporate vehicles.

## 1.1 Domestic limited company

A domestic resident company limited by shares can be incorporated under the Companies Act 1992. This type of company is used for the purpose of carrying on business in the Bahamas. It must have at least two directors, and the company's annual return must include a list of shareholders which is kept on the public register. Financial statements do not have to be audited and are not filed.

## 1.2 Company limited by guarantee

For a general description of this type of company you are referred to sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4. The memorandum of a company limited by guarantee must state the amount of the members' guarantee. The articles may state that members have differing 'shares' of assets and liabilities and, as such, can be structured as a 'hybrid' . Particulars of members are not filed.

## 1.3 Public company

Formed under the Companies Act 1992, it must be intended for the shares of a public company to be distributed to the public. A public company in the Bahamas must have at least three directors, it must produce annual accounts and have them audited. A list of all directors and officers must be filed with the company's annual return.

## 1.4 International Business Company

The IBC incorporated under the IBCA 1989, now 2000 as amended, is used for offshore asset holding purposes. The company only has IBC status on condition that it does not carry on business, nor hold real estate, in the Bahamas (although it may hold shares in other Bahamian companies). It may have one director and one shareholder (who may or may not be a resident in the Bahamas), and must also have a registered agent (who must reside in the Bahamas). There is no minimum share capital, shares must be registered, not bearer, denominated in any currency. Meetings may be held in the Bahamas, and it can be managed and operated from the Bahamas or elsewhere. The Bahamian government guarantees that an IBC will not attract taxes in the Bahamas for 20 years. The memorandum and articles are the only documents held on the public record. When the IBCA 1989 was passed there was no requirement to file particulars of directors, officers or shareholders. However, under the 2001 and 2004 amendments, IBCs are now required to file the names and addresses of directors, officers and 'beneficial owners' .

## 1.5 Limited duration companies

The limited duration company ('LDC') was introduced by the International Business Companies (Amendment) Act 1994, and the provisions are now found in Pt 10 of the IBCA 2000, as amended. This type of company is essentially an IBC, but (guess what?) it has a limited duration of up to 30 years. The LDC is a structure designed for US citizens as, under US tax legislation, it is treated as a US LLC so does not become liable to US corporation tax at the corporate level. Instead its annual profits flow through to the shareholders.

## 1.6 Bahamas foreign company

A foreign, non-Bahamian company can become registered under the Companies Act 1992 as a 'foreign' company. When granted a Certificate of Registration, the foreign company can operate a 'branch' or undertake business within the Bahamas, but must also obtain a business licence from the Investment Authority.

## 1.7 Segregated account company

Incorporated under the Segregated Account Companies Act 2004, this type of company is structured as a separate legal entity, but one that can segregate assets and liabilities of different accounts (or shares) from assets and liabilities of other accounts (or classes of shares). Each segregated account (or class) is insulated from the others. Assets of one account (or class) are not available to meet liabilities of another account (or class). In other words, creditors of portfolio no.1 cannot attach assets of the company that are held in, or constitute, portfolio no.2. The segregated account company is used by the investment funds industry when an asset management group structures its family of funds under a single 'umbrella' .

## 2. Bermuda

Bermuda is a nil-tax jurisdiction insofar as it does not impose direct income taxes, estate, wealth or capital taxes of any sort upon individuals (however, there is 'payroll' tax, payable by resident employees, which is a type of mandatory pension and social security provision). Nor does it impose corporation or withholding taxes upon companies.

The principal companies legislation in Bermuda is the Companies Act 1981. It underwent significant amendments in 2006 and more minor changes in 2009. The Bermuda Monetary Authority is responsible for corporate registrations.

## 2.1 Local company limited by shares

A local company is a company incorporated under Part 9 of the Companies Act 1981 that must have at least 60% of its shares owned by Bermudian citizens. In these circumstances a local company is allowed to carry on business within Bermuda. It must have at least two directors, who may be corporate and need not be Bermudian. A sole shareholder is permitted. The company must keep accounts at its registered office but they do not have to be filed and they are not, therefore, open to inspection. The register of directors, register of shareholders, memorandum and articles are all held in the public domain and are available for inspection.

## 2.2 Mutual company

A mutual company incorporated under Pt 12 of the Companies Act 1981 is essentially a guarantee company, in respect of which you are referred to sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4.

## 2.3 Bermuda 'permit' company

A foreign, non-Bermudian company can apply to the Ministry of Finance under the Companies Act 1981 to become registered as a 'permit' company. When granted an appropriate 'permit' under Pt 11 of the Act, the company can operate a 'branch' or undertake business within Bermuda.

## 2.4 Exempt company

The name 'exempt' under Pt 10 of the 1981 Act does not derive from the exemption from local corporation tax, as it did in the Channel Islands (because, as a nil-tax haven, Bermuda does not impose corporation taxes), but it derives from the fact that the company is exempt from the 60% local ownership requirement that applies to a company that wishes to carry on business from within Bermuda. Exempt companies must have at least two officers, who cannot be corporate, who reside in Bermuda either two directors, or one director and a secretary or 'permanent representative' . The annual fee for a Bermudian exempt company is expensive!

## 2.5 Mutual fund company

A 'mutual fund company' (as opposed to a 'mutual company') incorporated under Part 12A of the Companies Act 1981 as amended is clearly designed for the Bermudian mutual funds industry. The mutual fund company has the essential characteristics of a segregated account company referred to in Appendix 4, Section 9 and 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund.

## 3. British Virgin Islands (BVI)

The BVI are a nil-tax jurisdiction insofar as they do not impose direct income taxes, estate, wealth or capital taxes of any sort upon individuals. Nor do they impose corporation or withholding taxes upon companies.

Historically, the main corporate entity incorporated in the BVI and used for offshore asset holding purposes was the hugely successful IBC, incorporated under the International Business Companies Act 1984. This legislation shares some of the features of the UK model as well as US companies legislation. The IBCA did not supersede the existing companies law in 1984, but complemented it insofar as it existed alongside the traditional companies legislation, namely the Companies Act 1963 (Cap 285). The Companies Act 1963 was based upon the English 1948 Act and allowed for the incorporation of companies to carry on business in the BVI - they could be public or private, unlimited, limited by shares or guarantee, and became known colloquially as Cap 285 companies.

However, this regime was changed from 2006 by the enactment of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 ('BVI BCA'). The BVI BCA became the only piece of legislation applicable to companies in the BVI, whether public or private, carrying on business in the BVI or elsewhere, for the purposes of commercial trading or asset-holding. Just one type of company is available, namely the BVI Business Company (BVI BC), but this can be:

- limited by shares - the most popular form of BVI BC
- limited by guarantee, whether prohibited from issuing shares (i.e. a standard type of guarantee company) or permitted to issue shares (i.e. a 'hybrid' guarantee company, reviewed in section 8 of Appendix 4)
- with unlimited liability
- with segregated portfolios or cells (pursuant to Pt 7 BVI BCA, an entity similar to the type overviewed in section 1(7) above).

The new BVI BC of whatever type or colour is not subject to local corporation tax, as there is none. It therefore complies with OECD notions of 'fair' taxation principles, and also satisfies the EU Savings Tax Directive and Code of Conduct on Business Taxation (as required by the UK for all its Overseas Territories) insofar as there is no discriminatory treatment between resident and truly 'offshore' companies.

The following types of company formerly available in the BVI have been superseded: the IBC (transitional provisions stated that all existing IBC's convert automatically to BVI BCs by 2008), the public company, the ordinary resident company, the ordinary non-resident company, the company limited by guarantee (both resident and nonresident), and finally the Cap 285 company.

## 4. The Cayman Islands

The Cayman Islands are a nil-tax jurisdiction insofar as they do not impose direct income taxes, estate, wealth or capital taxes of any sort upon individuals. Nor do they impose corporation or withholding taxes upon companies.

The principal companies legislation is the Companies Law 1961, which has been amended many times and was most recently revised in 2020. The Companies Law is largely based upon the English 1948 Act and provides for the incorporation of several types of company.

## 4.1 The ordinary resident company

The ordinary resident company is typically incorporated in order to carry on business in the local economy within the geographic territory of the Cayman Islands. It is also governed by the Local Companies (Control) Law 1995, which states that it must be at least 60% owned by Caymanian citizens or obtain a local license authorising it to trade notwithstanding that it does not have majority Caymanian ownership. Hence the colloquial name given to this type of company is a 'local company' . A list of shareholders must be filed with the Registrar of Companies with the annual return, which is available for inspection. Bearer shares are not permitted. An AGM must be held each year. Accounts must be maintained, although formal financial statements are not mandatory and do not have to be filed, nor is an audit necessary.

## 4.2 The ordinary non-resident company

The ordinary non-resident company is subject to much the same statutory requirements under the Companies Law 2020 Revision as the resident company, except that pursuant to the Local Companies (Control) Law it cannot carry on any business within the jurisdiction of the Cayman Islands. Meetings of directors and of shareholders can take place in the Cayman Islands or abroad, although the annual return contains an undertaking that local business is not being transacted. Registers of directors, officers and shareholders must be kept, but are not filed and they are not in the public domain. The company must keep accounts, but there is no audit or filing requirement. This type of company is used for foreign asset holding purposes as an alternative to the exempt company.

## 4.3 Exempt company

The name 'exempt' does not derive from the exemption from local corporation tax, as it did in the Channel Islands (as the Cayman Islands are a nil-tax haven, and do not impose corporation taxes), but it derives from the fact that the company is exempt from the 60% local ownership requirement that applies to a company that wishes to carry on business from within the Cayman Islands. The exempt company is basically the same as the non-resident company except that, under Pt 7 of the Companies Law, it:

- may issue bearer shares, subject to immobilisation provisions
- does not have to use the word 'Limited' in its name
- has a government guarantee that it will not be subject to corporation taxes for 20 years.

## 4.4 Exempted limited duration company ('LDC')

Under Pt 8 of the Companies Law, the LDC shares some of the characteristics of the Delaware LLC reviewed in Appendix 4, section 10.3 and in 1(5) above and, of course, has a limited duration.

## 4.5 Segregated portfolio company

Under Pt 14 of the Companies Law 2020 Revision, the segregated portfolio company is similar to the segregated account company reviewed in section 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund.

## 4.6 Foreign company

A foreign, non-Caymanian company can apply to the Registrar under Pt 9 of the Companies Law 2020 to become registered as a 'foreign' company. When granted an appropriate certificate of registration under Pt 9 of the Law, the company can operate a 'branch' or undertake business within the Cayman Islands.

## 4.7 Guarantee company

For a description of guarantee companies generally you are referred to sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4, and s.9 of the Companies Law, 2020 Revision.

## 4.8 Limited Liability Company (LLC)

The Cayman Islands LLC is based upon the Delaware template and governed by the Limited Liability Companies Law 2016. As in Delaware, a Caymanian LLC does not have a board of directors, but is managed by a managing member elected by the shareholders. The managing member is typically given all powers of management of the business of the company on a day-to-day basis. It has Articles but, in addition, it is typical for the members and managing member to enter into an LLC Agreement to set out the internal capital structure and rules governing management and administration. Because it enjoys greater flexibility than an ordinary company in terms of its management, organisation and allocation of profits and losses (in a manner similar to a partnership) it is typically used as a vehicle for investment funds, joint venture companies, private equity transactions, securitisations and other international financing structures.

## 5. Gibraltar

Gibraltar was classified by OECD as a 'tax haven' . It would be better described as a not so-low-tax haven. Ordinary companies are subject to Gibraltar corporation tax at a standard rate, which has been, until recently, 35% (with a small company rate of 20% where profits are less than £35,000 p.a.). What makes it a tax haven in the eyes of OECD is that the 'exempt' company is not subject to taxes in Gibraltar. The exempt company is essentially one that does not carry on business in the local economy and is not owned, beneficially or otherwise, by Gibraltarian citizens or residents. This has lead to an interesting legal battle with Europe.

Having regard to OECD and EU criticism of the duplicity of the ordinary private company regime for 'locals' being subject to corporation tax of 35% and the exempt and qualifying company regime for 'foreigners' being essentially exempt from tax, the Gibraltar government proposed to abolish the exempt and qualifying company in 2003 and introduce a new form of corporation tax payable by all Gibraltarian companies. The new corporation tax would not be assessed upon the company's profits in the normal

(EU-recognised) way, but upon the size of its payroll and occupation of business premises.

It was observed that the typical Gibraltarian asset holding company (with no more than a P.O. box in Gibraltar) would not become liable to pay any such tax. This was exactly why it was designed in this way, namely to protect Gibraltar's financial services industry. Accordingly the European Commission brought a lawsuit alleging, amongst other things, that the proposed tax reforms were unfair under 'regional selectivity' because they were advantageous to Gibraltar companies when compared to those incorporated elsewhere in Europe, and in the UK in particular.

Anticipating defeat, and after years of litigation, Gibraltar phased out the exempt and qualifying company by 2010.

In order to preserve Gibraltar's financial service industry some out-of-the-box thinking was required. It could become a nil-tax jurisdiction like, for instance, the Cayman Islands, or become a quasi-nil-corporate-tax jurisdiction and introduce a Zero/10 tax regime like the Channel Islands and the Isle of Man. But, for fiscal reasons specifically applicable to Gibraltar, its expected tax revenues would be insufficient to maintain its public expenditures. Alternatively, Gibraltar could follow the Cyprus model and move to a low rate of corporation tax of 10% that would apply to all Gibraltarian companies across the board.

Negotiations with the EU continued alongside the lawsuits. Announcements of a 10% rate of corporation tax were made then renounced. Then in 2008 the European Court ruled in favour of Gibraltar stating that it could indeed introduce its own tax system, and did not have to adopt the UK model. Accordingly, since 2011 when the Income Tax Act 2010 came into force, a Gibraltarian Ordinary Resident Company pays corporate income tax at the current rate of 10% per annum upon global earnings; whereas a Non-Resident Company (defined as a company incorporated in Gibraltar that does not trade or derive any earnings in or from Gibraltar) pays tax at a nil rate.

Gibraltar now offers the following types of corporate entity.

## 5.1 Private company limited by shares

Companies incorporated in Gibraltar were governed by the Companies Ordinance 1930 as amended, which was based upon the English Companies Acts of 1929 and 1948. The Companies Ordinance was modernised and amended by the introduction of the Companies Act 2014. A private company is one with a maximum of 50 shareholders, particulars of which are filed with the annual return and available for public inspection. A sole director is permitted who may be corporate.  As indicated above, a private company incorporated in Gibraltar may be an Ordinary Resident company or Non-Resident.

- The Ordinary Resident Company is one that carries on business in Gibraltar or whose management and control is exercised in Gibraltar. In these circumstances it pays corporate income tax at the current rate of 10% per annum upon global earnings.
- The Non-Resident Company is a company incorporated in Gibraltar that does not trade or derive any earnings in or from Gibraltar and pays corporate income tax at a nil rate.

## 5.2 Public company limited by shares

A public company is one that is not a private company and must have 'plc' in its name.

## 5.3 Company limited by guarantee

The Gibraltar Companies Ordinance permits companies to be limited by guarantee in the normal way, and to be limited by guarantee but still issue shares as a 'hybrid'. The hybrids can be used as a holding vehicle instead of the discretionary trust see section 8 of Appendix 4.

## 5.4 Exempt company (abolished)

An exempt company used to be a private company limited by shares that applied for and obtained tax-exempt status. In these circumstances it was exempt from Gibraltar corporation tax and dividends were not subject to withholding taxes. It is for these reasons that the exempt company had to be abolished.

## 5.5 Qualifying company (abolished)

This used to be a company incorporated in Gibraltar limited by shares that applied for and obtained 'Qualifying company' status. To qualify the company must not have conducted business in Gibraltar, and satisfied the same conditions that applied to the exempt company. In fact the qualifying company was merely an extension of the exempt company, because if it qualified as such, instead of paying nil tax, it could agree the rate of tax it paid with the Financial and Development Secretary. This was usually between 1% and 35% in order to ensure that the company paid just enough tax to avoid any tax-avoidance rules that apply in the state in which the company carries on its main business. The qualifying company has now been withdrawn under OECD and EU pressure.

## 5.6 Overseas company

A foreign company can obtain permission to carry on its business in or from Gibraltar by registering as an 'overseas' company.

## 6. Guernsey

Guernsey was classified by OECD as a 'tax haven' , but ought to be described as a nil-corporate-tax haven. There is no corporation tax as such. Until 1 January 2008 both individuals and Guernsey companies paid income tax at the rate of 20%. However, Guernsey companies could apply for and obtain tax-exempt status it which case they paid no corporate income tax at all. Tax exemption was essentially conferred when the company did not carry on business in the local economy and was beneficially owned by foreigners, i.e. non-Guernsey citizens or residents.

The dichotomy of one tax regime for 'local, resident companies' and another for 'exempt' companies lead to much criticism by OECD. Clearly OECD wanted Guernsey to impose the same level of corporate income taxes upon both resident and otherwise exempt companies. Seeing the writing on the wall, Guernsey decided to abolish the concept of the exempt company (as well as the international company). The government then surprised OECD by introducing a new 'Zero/10' tax regime from 2008 which applies to companies across the board, irrespective of whether they carry on business in Guernsey or elsewhere. 'Zero/10' refers to the fact that ordinary companies pay corporate income tax at a nil rate, whereas banks and financial service providers regulated by the Guernsey FSC pay tax at 10%.

To coincide with the new tax system, Guernsey enacted new companies legislation in the form of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008, which streamlined incorporation procedures and introduced a modern companies registry. So, for instance, it is now possible to incorporate a Guernsey company by application to the registrar, rather than involving the court.

Under the new Guernsey regime, the exempt company and the international company have been abolished. This leaves the following types of company still available.

## 6.1 Company limited by shares

In respect of Guernsey companies currently available, see Part 1, sections 1 through 9 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008. There is no distinction between private and public companies. A company limited by shares is the bog-standard. A sole director is permitted and there must be a secretary. Corporate procedures are simplified and there are no requirements for the holding of meetings.

## 6.2 Company limited by guarantee

A company limited by guarantee in accordance with s.7 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008 is no different to other guarantee companies, in respect of which see sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4.

## 6.3 Unlimited liability company

Permitted under s.8 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008. There is also a mixed liability 'hybrid' available under s.9.

## 6.4 Cell companies

Originally known as 'protected cell companies' under the Protected Cell Companies Ordinance 1997, these are now governed by Pt 27 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008. A cell company has the essential features that are identical to the segregated portfolio company, in respect of which you are referred to section 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund. There is also a variation called the Incorporated Cell Company under s.4.

## 6.5 Exempt company (abolished 2008)

Prior to 2008, a private company could apply for and obtain tax exempt status under the Income Tax (Exempt Bodies) (Guernsey) Ordinance on condition it did not trade within the local economy. The annual fee was £600. The exempt company has been phased out and any exempt companies became resident for tax purposes on 1 January 2008, so that their profits are liable to tax at the rate of nil% (or 10% if a bank, etc.).

## 6.6 International company (abolished 2008)

Prior to 2008, a private company could apply for and obtain 'international company' status under the Income Tax (International Bodies)(Guernsey) Law 1993. In order to qualify the company had to be resident for tax purposes in Guernsey but must not conduct business with Guernsey citizens or residents. Upon acquiring international company status the company could, instead of paying nil tax as an exempt company, agree the rate of tax it pays with the Guernsey tax authorities. This was usually between 1% and 30% in order to ensure that the company paid just enough tax to avoid any tax-avoidance rules that apply in the state in which it carries on its main business. The international company was abolished as of 1 January 2008.

## 7. Hong Kong

Hong Kong is a low-tax centre. There is no corporation tax as such. Instead, income tax is imposed upon profits made by 'persons' who carry on a trade or profession in or from Hong Kong. 'Persons' in this context includes individuals, and also companies that are incorporated or registered in Hong Kong. Accordingly, both Hong Kong-resident individuals and Hong Kong-resident companies pay income tax (historically, companies at the rate of 16% and individuals at 15%).

The type of corporate entity available in Hong Kong is fairly basic: public, private and registered branch of an overseas company. However, it is the tax treatment of these 'persons' that create tax planning opportunities. Tax is charged on a territorial basis. In other words, corporate income tax is only imposed upon profits made by a Hong Kong company that are actually earned in or derive from Hong Kong. All foreign earned profits, or earnings that derive from outside of Hong Kong, are free from corporate income tax. In these circumstances, a Hong Kong private company that holds and manages income producing assets outside Hong Kong can earn profits tax-free, without incurring any liability in Hong Kong to pay corporate income tax.

The old companies legislation in Hong Kong, namely the Companies Ordinance 1984, consolidated into a 1997 version was based upon the English Acts of 1948 and 1985. However, this was modernised and significantly changed by the introduction of amendments to the Companies Ordinance, Cap 622 in 2014. Hong Kong has never catered for the exempt company nor the BVI style IBC.

## 7.1 Private company limited by shares

A private company incorporated under the Hong Kong Companies Ordinance looks, feels and smells much like an English company incorporated under the 1948 Act. Having said that, the 2014 legislation is a modern equivalent and introduces one or two interesting concepts as well as a considerable amount of detail and additional complexity. The private company limited by shares, in accordance with section 8 Companies Ordinance, Cap 622, 2014, is the type of vehicle used by businessmen for trading purposes within Hong Kong and also by international investors, family offices and fiduciaries for asset holding purposes. There is also available a private company limited by guarantee in accordance with s.9 of the Companies Ordinance, Cap 622, 2014, and an unlimited company in accordance with s.10.

## 7.2 Public company limited by shares

A public company is any company other than a private company as defined in s.12 of the Companies Ordinance, Cap 622, 2014.

## 7.3 Branch of an overseas company

See Pt 16, Divisions 1 and 2, ss.774-795 of the Companies Ordinance, Cap 622, 2014.

## 8. The Isle of Man

Like the Channel Islands, the Isle of Man was classified by OECD as a 'tax haven' . But it ought to be described as a nil-corporate tax haven. There is no corporation tax as such. Until 1 January 2006 both individuals and Manx companies paid income tax (historically companies paid corporate income tax at the rate of 20% but, from 2003, the first £500,000 was taxed at 10% and, in the last year before the introduction of 'Zero/10' in 2006, the rate was 15% for trading companies and 18% for non-trading companies). However, prior to 2006, Manx companies could apply for and obtain tax-exempt status, in which case they paid no corporate income tax at all. Tax exemption was essentially conferred when the company did not carry on business in the local economy and was beneficially owned by foreigners, i.e. non-Manx citizens or residents.

The dichotomy of one tax regime for 'local, resident companies' and another for 'exempt' companies lead to much criticism by OECD. Clearly OECD wanted the Isle of Man to impose the same level of taxes upon both resident and otherwise exempt companies. The Isle of Man therefore decided to abolish the concept of the exempt company (and the non-resident and international company). The Manx government then surprised OECD by introducing a new 'Zero/10' tax regime from 2006 which applies to companies across the board, irrespective of whether they carry on business in the Isle of Man or elsewhere. 'Zero/10' refers to the fact that ordinary companies pay corporate income tax at a nil rate, whereas banks and financial service providers regulated by the Manx FSC pay corporate income tax of 10%.

To coincide with the new tax system, the Isle of Man enacted new companies legislation in the form of the Companies Act 2006. The 2006 Act operates alongside the old Companies Act 1931, as amended from time to time, and introduced a new company, called (rather appropriately) the 'New Manx Vehicle' .

Under the new Manx corporate and tax regime, the exempt company, non-resident company and the international company have been abolished. This leaves the following types of company still available in the Isle of Man.

## 8.1 Private company limited by shares

The Manx Companies Act 1931, as amended, distinguishes between a public and private company. A private company must state it is a private company in its memorandum; it may have a sole shareholder and particulars must be filed in the annual return and are then available for public inspection. A company limited by shares is the bog-standard company based upon the English 1948 Act.

## 8.2 Public company

A public company is one that it not a private company, as defined, and must have 'plc' in its name. It must have a minimum of two shareholders although it is anticipated that the shares will be made available to the investing public.

## 8.3 Company limited by guarantee (and 'hybrid')

A company limited by guarantee in the Isle of Man is no different to other guarantee companies and the company limited by guarantee with shares is the classic 'hybrid' company, in respect of which you are referred to sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4.

## 8.4 Unlimited liability company

Permitted under Pt 4 s.61 of the Companies Act 2006.

## 8.5 Protected cell companies

Protected cell companies' under the Manx Protected Cell Companies Act 2004 have the essential features that are similar to the segregated portfolio company, in respect of which you are referred to 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund.

## 8.6 Limited liability company ('LLC')

The LLC was introduced into Manx law by the Limited Liability Companies Act 1996. It does not have directors, but must have at least two members and a registered agent who is required to reside in the Isle of Man. The internal rules of governance of an LLC are set out in Articles of Organisation. It has a maximum duration of up to 30 years. One of the important features of an LLC is that it is not subject to corporate income tax (even at the new nil rate). Instead it is taxed as a partnership, so that corporate profits flow-through to the members and are taxed in the hands of the individual members. (For a detailed description of an LLC you are referred to Appendix 4, section 10.3).

## 8.7 New Manx Vehicle

The Companies Act 2006 created a new, stream-lined, cheap-and-cheerful, easy-tomanage company, which has become known as the 'New Manx Vehicle' . Each such company is given a company number in the normal way, but it contains the suffix 'V' to distinguish it from the ordinary company incorporated under the Companies Act 1931. The New Manx Vehicle is used for asset holding purposes and is therefore taxed at the zero rate of corporate income tax applicable in the Isle of Man. It is similar to the BVI BC.

## 8.8 Exempt company (abolished 2006)

Prior to 2006, a Manx private company could apply for and obtain tax exempt status under the Income Tax (Exempt Companies) Act 1984 on condition that it did not trade within the local economy and was not owned, beneficially or otherwise, by Manx citizens or residents and had no Manx income. The annual fee was £475. The exempt company was phased out and any existing exempt companies became resident for tax purposes on 1 January 2007, so that their profits are now liable to tax at the nil rate (or 10% if a bank, etc.).

## 8.9 International company (abolished 2006)

Prior to 2006, a Manx private company could apply for and obtain 'international company' status under the International Business Act 1994. In order to qualify the company must have been resident for tax purposes in the Isle of Man, but not conduct business with Manx citizens or residents so that its income was derived from outside the Isle of Man. Upon acquiring international company status the company could, instead of paying nil tax as an exempt company, agree the rate of tax it paid to the Manx tax authorities. This was usually between 1% and 30% in order to ensure that the company paid just enough tax to avoid any tax-avoidance rules that apply in the state in which it carries on its main business. The international company was abolished as of 1 January 2006.

## 8.10 Non-resident company (abolished 2006)

A private Manx company could apply to become non-resident by submitting a Declaration of Non-Residency, upon condition that its central management and control was exercised overseas, namely outside the geographical territory of the Isle of Man. In these circumstances it was not liable to pay Manx corporate income tax. For the reasons explained in Appendix 4, section 3, they have been phased out and ultimately abolished in 2006.

## 9. Jersey

Jersey, like Guernsey, was classified by OECD as a 'tax haven' . However, like Guernsey and the Isle of Man, it ought to be described as a nil-corporate-tax centre. There is no corporation tax as such. Until 2008 both Jersey-resident individuals and Jersey-resident companies paid income tax at the rate of 20%. However, Jersey companies could apply for and obtain tax-exempt status in which case they paid no corporate income tax at all. Tax exemption was essentially conferred when the company did not carry on business in the local economy and was beneficially owned by foreigners, i.e. non-Jersey citizens or residents.

The dichotomy of one tax regime for 'local, resident companies' and another for 'exempt' companies lead to much criticism by OECD. Clearly OECD wanted Jersey to impose the same level of corporate income taxes upon both resident and otherwise exempt companies. Accordingly, Jersey (like Guernsey and the Isle of Man, which had similar regimes) decided to abolish the concept of the exempt company (as well as the international business company). The government then introduced a new 'Zero/10' tax regime by the Income Tax (Amendment No.28)(Jersey) Law 2007 which, from 1 January 2009, applies to companies across the board, irrespective of whether they carry on business in Jersey or elsewhere. 'Zero/10' refers to the fact that ordinary companies pay corporate income tax at a nil rate, whereas banks and financial service providers regulated by the Jersey FSC pay tax at 10%. This clearly resulted in lost tax revenues so, in order to bridge the gap, Jersey introduced a 'goods and sales tax' ('GST').

Under the new Jersey corporate income tax regime, the exempt company and the international business company have been abolished. This leaves the following types of company still available.

## 9.1 Private company limited by shares

In respect of Jersey companies currently available, see Pt 2, arts 3A-3I of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended from time to time and consolidated in 2019. There remains a distinction between private companies (art.3A) and public companies (art.3B). A company limited by shares is the bog-standard and is used for asset holding purposes. A sole director is permitted and there must be a secretary. Corporate procedures are simplified and there are no requirements for the holding of meetings.

## 9.2 Public companies limited by shares

See the above and art.3B of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended and consolidated 2019.

## 9.3 Company limited by guarantee

A company limited by guarantee in Jersey under art.3G of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991 as amended, is no different to other guarantee companies, in respect of which see sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4.

## 9.4 Unlimited liability company

Permitted under art.3D of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991 as amended and revised 2019.

## 9.5 Par value and no-par value companies

See arts 3E and 3F of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended.

## 9.6 Limited Life companies

See art.3H of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended.

## 9.7 Cell companies

Sometimes known as 'protected cell companies' , these are now available under art.3I, and are governed by Pt 18D of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended. The Jersey cell company has all of the essential features that apply to the segregated portfolio company, in respect of which see section 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund.

## 9.8 Exempt company (abolished 2009)

Prior to June 2008, a Jersey private company could apply to the Comptroller of Income Tax and obtain tax exempt status under the Income Tax (Jersey) Law 1961 on condition it did not trade within the local economy. The annual fee was £600. The exempt company has been phased out and any existing exempt companies on 3 June 2009

became resident for tax purposes, so that their profits are liable to tax at the nil rate (or 10% if a bank, etc.).

## 9.9 International business company (abolished 2011)

Prior to January 2006, a Jersey private company could apply for and obtain 'international business company' status (not to be confused with the BVI company of the same name). In order to qualify the company must have been resident in Jersey for tax purposes but not conduct business with Jersey citizens or residents. Upon acquiring international business company status the company could, instead of paying nil tax as an exempt company, agree the rate of tax it pays with the Jersey tax authority. This was usually between 1% and 30% in order to ensure that the company paid just enough tax to avoid any tax-avoidance rules that apply in the state in which it carries on its main business. The Jersey version of the international business company was completely abolished in 2011.

## 10. Singapore

Singapore is a not-so-low-tax centre. Corporation tax at the rate of 17% (as of 2017) is imposed upon profits made by companies that are resident in Singapore (essentially incorporated in Singapore or that are managed and controlled from Singapore). The type of corporate entity available in Singapore is fairly basic: public, private and registered branch of an overseas company. However, it is the tax treatment of these companies that create tax planning opportunities. Corporation tax is charged on a territorial basis. In other words, corporation tax is only imposed upon earnings made by a Singaporean company that are actually earned in, or derive from, or are received in, Singapore. All foreign earned profits, or earnings that derive from sources outside Singapore, are free from corporation tax. In these circumstances, a Singaporean private company that holds and manages income producing assets outside Singapore can earn profits tax-free, without incurring any liability in Singapore to pay corporation tax. Singapore is also an attractive jurisdiction to establish a group holding company.

The companies legislation in Singapore, namely the Companies Act, Cap 50 consolidated 2017, is based upon the English 1948 and 1985 Acts, although a number of amendments were introduced in 2016 prior to the publication of a consolidated version of Cap 50 in 2017. Singapore has never catered for the exempt company nor BVI style IBC.

## 10.1 Private company limited by shares

A private company incorporated under the Singapore Companies Act Cap 50 is very similar to a private company incorporated under the English 1948 Act. This is the type of vehicle used by businessmen for trading purposes within Singapore, and also by international investors, family offices and fiduciaries for asset holding purposes. It is also used as a group holding structure.

The Exempt Private Company (EPC) must have less than 20 shareholders and enjoys certain exemptions, such as no audit requirement and accounts do not need to be filed with the Accounting and Corporate Regulatory Authority (ACRA).

## 10.2 Public company limited by shares

A public company is any company other than a private company, as defined in s.18 of the Companies Act, Cap 50.

## 10.3 Company limited by guarantee

Usually carry out not-for-profit activities; must register with ACRA, have its accounts audited and filed.

## 10.4 Branch of an overseas company

See Pt 11, Division 2, ss.365-386 of the Companies Act, Cap 50.

## Appendix 17A:

## Class rights

Although there are variations upon each theme, there are basically two classes of share: ordinary shares (called common stock in the US), and preference shares (called preferred stock in the US).

## 1. Ordinary shares

An owner of ordinary shares generally has the following rights.

## i. Voting

An owner of ordinary shares (of common stock) may attend meetings of members of the company, propose and discuss motions and vote on them. Generally, an ordinary shareholder has one vote per share (on a poll). The ordinary shareholders control the company in general meeting and influence management by appointing the directors.

## ii. Dividends

An owner of ordinary shares (of common stock) may be paid a dividend when:

- a. the company makes a profit and
- b. when declared by the company.

But ordinary shareholders only receive the surplus of the declared dividend after a dividend has been paid to the preference shareholders according to their express rights thereto, in respect of which see section 2 below.

## iii. Return of capital

Ordinary shareholders have the right to the return of capital when the company is wound up either alongside (or equal with) other shareholders or, if the preference shareholders are expressly given priority, after the return of capital to the preference shareholders.

## 2. Preference shares

'Preference share' is a generic name given to various classes of share in order to distinguish them from ordinary shares. Their distinguishing features depend upon the rights conferred upon holders of them as set out in the memorandum, in the articles or in the terms of their issue. In a nutshell, they often have priority over ordinary shares in relation to the payment of a dividend and the return of capital when the company is wound up.

An owner of preference shares will typically have the following rights.

## i. Voting

A preference shareholder generally has limited voting rights. The right of a preference shareholder to attend meetings of members and, moreover, to vote on proposed resolutions is usually limited to such matters as the winding-up of the company, restrictions to vary their class rights or to vote in connection with their dividends that are in arrears. Other than the above they have no rights to vote and thereby influence the constitution of, or the direction of the business of the company, for instance by hiring and firing directors, etc.

## ii. Dividends

A preference shareholder generally has the right to a first claim upon any dividend declared by the company. The right to a dividend is presumed to be non-participating, unless the articles or terms of issue expressly state otherwise. This means that they are not entitled to participate (share) in the surplus dividend with the ordinary shareholders.

## Example 1

A company, XYZ Ltd, has issued 1,000 ordinary shares. It declares a dividend of $5,000. Each share receives a dividend of $5.

## Example 2

XYZ Ltd issues 1,000 ordinary shares and 500 preference shares which carry the right to a $2 dividend preference. It declares a dividend of $5,000. The preference shareholders receive a dividend of $1,000 (or $2 per share), and the ordinary shareholders share a dividend of $4,000 (or $4 per share).

## Example 3

If the preference shareholders have the express right to participate in the profits, on the facts of Example 2 they would receive a first dividend of $1,000, as above, and the balance of $4,000 would be divided equally between the ordinary and the preference shareholders ($2.66 per share).

The terms of the dividend preference is usually expressed as a percentage of the par (or nominal) value of the shares.

## Example 4

On the facts of Example 2, assume that XYZ Ltd issues 50010% preference shares with a par value of $20. If the shares are non-participating, the preference shareholders receive $1,000 (500$2010%).

A preference shareholder's right to a dividend may be cumulative . This means that if the company does not declare a dividend in any one year, the right is carried forward to subsequent years and paid before ordinary shareholders as soon as a sufficient dividend is declared.

## Example 5

XYZ Ltd does not declare a dividend in year one, but in year two it declares a dividend of $5,000. If the shares are cumulative and non-participating the preference shareholders receive $2,000 ($1,000 plus $1,000 carried forward from year one), and the ordinary shareholders share $3,000 or $3 per share.

The right to a cumulative dividend is presumed unless it is specifically displaced by express words in the articles or terms of issue.

## Example 6

On the facts of Example 5, assume that the articles of XYZ Ltd state that the preference shareholders have the right to a $2 dividend preference 'payable out of the yearly profits only' . In these circumstances they would not receive a dividend in year one, and would receive only $1,000 in year two, as no carry forward is permitted.

## iii. Return of capital

When a company ceases to trade and its life ends upon the company being formally wound up, the articles should state whether preference shareholders are entitled to the return of their capital in priority to, or alongside (with equal rights to), the ordinary shareholders. As a rule, however, they do not have any right to share the surplus assets after the return of capital to the shareholders.

## Example 7

Assume that XYZ Ltd issues 1,000 ordinary shares with a par value of $10 each, and 500 preference shares also with a $10 par value. The company is wound up with net assets (after paying all debts) of $20,000. The ordinary and preference shareholders may be entitled to the return of their capital equally, pari passu . So the ordinary shareholders receive a return of their capital of $10,000, and the preference shareholders receive $5,000. The surplus of $5,000 is paid over to the ordinary shareholders.

By contrast, the articles of XYZ Ltd or the terms of issue may give preference shareholders priority over ordinary shareholders, so that the ordinary shareholders are repaid their capital contributions only after preference shareholders have received theirs.

## Example 8

Assume that the facts are as in Example 7, save that the net assets of XYZ Ltd only amount to $10,000. The articles give preference shareholders priority rights to the return of their capital. The preference shareholders here receive $5,000, the return of their capital in full. The remaining $5,000 is shared pari passu between the ordinary shareholders - each share receiving a return of capital of $5 only.
