# Appendix 3: Lifting the veil of incorporation

## Appendix 3:

## Lifting the veil of incorporation

## The veil of incorporation

1. There are two well-established principles of company law enshrined in Salomon v A Salomon and Co. Ltd . 20 Firstly, a company is a completely separate entity from its shareholders who are said to exist on the other side of the veil of incorporation. Secondly, as a consequence, the liability of shareholders for the company's debts is limited to the amount they have paid, or agreed to pay, for their shares when they were issued. Accordingly, if shareholders have paid the full issue price when shares were acquired from the company, they cannot be held liable to pay any debts incurred by the company. The company is liable for its own debts; not the shareholders. The shareholders can lose their investment, but no more, in the event that the company becomes insolvent and unable to pay its own creditors. From a creditor's perspective, a creditor of a company can only enforce payment of the debts against the company and cannot go behind the corporate veil to enforce payment against its shareholders.

## Lift the veil of incorporation

2. When and in what circumstances does the common law permit a creditor of a company to lift the veil of incorporation? In other words, are there circumstances where the courts are prepared to ignore the separate legal personality of a company? If there are such circumstances, it will have two consequences. Firstly, it will permit thereby a creditor to impose personal liability upon the company's shareholders. Secondly, it will permit the creditors of a shareholder to enforce payment against the assets of the company.
3. The leading case of Adams v Cape Industries and the more modern case of VTB Capital plc v Nutritek International Corp analysed the very limited circumstances when a court would be prepared to lift the veil of incorporation, distinguishing circumstances that involve fraud and circumstances where a parent can be held liable for the debts of a subsidiary. 21

## Fraud

4. A court may lift the corporate veil and disregard a company's separate personality when the company:
2. (a) is clearly a 'sham' or 'mere façade' and
3. (b) has been used fraudulently in order to avoid an existing legal obligation.

- 4.1 Example 1: In Jones v Lipman a court ordered A to transfer certain property to B. Whereupon, in order to avoid such legal obligation, A transferred the property to a company of which A was sole shareholder. The court was prepared to lift the veil of incorporation and permit B to enforce the original obligation against the property of the company. 22
- 4.2 Example 2: In Gencor ACP Ltd v Dalby a director of a public company (Company A) dishonestly stole money from the company and transferred it to Company B, a private company owned and controlled by him. Company A obtained judgment against the director, but the court permitted it to enforce the judgment against the assets of Company B. It was held that Company B was a mere façade and had been formed by the director in order to avoid fraudulently his existing liability to Company A. 23
- 4.3 Example 3: In Wallersteiner v Moir a complex corporate structure was established by a single person in order to hold money that he had fraudulently obtained by deception. The court allowed the corporate veil to be lifted on the basis that the corporate entities were mere 'fronts' or a façade, and were designed deliberately to avoid to that person's legal obligations. The network of companies were dismantled so that the corporate assets were deemed to belong to the dishonest shareholder; the creditors of the shareholder could then attach the assets of the corporate entities. 24

## When a parent can be held liable for the debts of a subsidiary

5. The starting point in the analysis of whether the corporate veil of a subsidiary can be lifted in order to render its parent liable for the debts of its subsidiary is this: each member within a group of companies is a separate legal entity in its own right, with completely separate rights and liabilities from other entities or members of the group.

Nevertheless, it was held in Cape that where the group is operated as a 'single economic unit' , a court can ignore the separate legal entities and treat them as one. But, in reality, it is extremely difficult (some may say almost impossible) to prove that each member of the group of companies is a single economic unit when they have, for example: a different board, their own bank accounts, their own property and contractual rights, have their own accounting records and prepare their own financials, etc. For example, it was stated in Atlas Maritime Co. SA. v Avalon Maritime Ltd, the Coral Rose that:

'The creation or purchase of a subsidiary company with minimal liability, which will operate with the parent's funds and on the parent's directions but not expose the parent to liability may not seem to some the most honest way of trading. But it is extremely common in the international shipping industry and elsewhere [namely in the world of commerce at large]. To hold that it creates as agency relationship between subsidiary and parent [or forms a single economic unit] would be a revolutionary doctrine.' 25

## United States' law

6. In these circumstances it is pretty exceptional indeed for an English court to be prepared to lift the veil of incorporation. This is in contrast to US law which has departed from English common law principles, particularly when a private company has been established and carries on business, incurring thereby debts and becoming insolvent principally because it has always been hopelessly under-capitalised by its owner. For example, the owner has financed the business by very little of his own money, but an enormous amount of debt. In these circumstances a court in the US would much more readily be prepared to lift the veil to permit creditors of the company to enforce payments of the debt from the company's shareholder personally.

England, and most of the common law financial centres have, in these circumstances, gone down a different path, permitting creditors to sue the company's board and shareholders for fraudulent or wrongful trading. These are remedies discussed further in Module 7.
