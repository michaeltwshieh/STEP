## Module 8: Directors Part II - Directors' Powers and Duties

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- review the powers usually conferred upon directors
- introduce the mechanics of board meetings and the directors' decision-making process
- analyse the legal effect of a transaction purportedly entered into on behalf of a company, but which was beyond the powers conferred upon the directors
- outline the duties owed by the directors to their company
- examine a director's fiduciary duty
- examine a director's duty to exercise his powers of management with appropriate care and skill
- consider what is expected of a director of an offshore company
- explain the circumstances in which a director may become liable to the company and to creditors of the company.

## 1. Powers of directors

## 1.1 Typical powers

The powers enjoyed by the directors are conferred upon them by the articles of a company. The directors are usually given a general power to manage the company, 62 often with additional specific powers conferred by the articles. For instance, they may be specifically empowered:

- to allot and issue shares in exchange for capital introduced to a company by investors 63
- to borrow money, to issue promissory notes or bonds, and to enter into loan agreements with lenders on behalf of the company, and to mortgage company property as security for such borrowing 64
- to make calls on unpaid or partly paid shares and procure shares to be forfeited when calls are not paid 65

<!-- image -->

- to convene general meetings of the shareholders of the company and meetings of directors 66
- to pay interim dividends, recommend the payment of a final dividend, or actually decide upon whether to make a distribution 67
- to manage the affairs of the company generally, a power that includes a multitude of collateral powers, for instance:
- -to buy and sell goods and services
- -to hire and fire staff
- -to appoint agents and officers 68
- -to open and operate bank accounts
- -to keep accounts 69
- -to bind the company by entering into deals and contracts
- -to execute deeds and documents on behalf of the company 70
- -to do what is generally necessary to run the company. 71

## 1.2 Exercise of the powers conferred upon directors

When a company's board consists of more than a sole director, the board makes business decisions collectively. In other words, the directors exercise their powers and function generally at meetings. The powers set out above for managing the business of the company are given to the board as a whole rather than to any one individual director (unless, of course, there is only a sole director). Accordingly, the directors traditionally make a decision on behalf of the company only by discussion of a motion at a properly convened board meeting, of which proper notice has been given and which is quorate, by then voting on the motion and passing an appropriate resolution.

The specific requirements for corporate decision making are determined by the articles 72  and are reviewed in Module 10. The general rule is that each director may attend meetings of the board and is given a single vote to cast on each issue. Resolutions are passed by a simple majority of the directors who attend and vote. Nowadays, however, if all the directors are in unanimous agreement on an issue, a written resolution may be passed, thereby dispensing with the need for a formal meeting. 73

## 1.3 The effect of actions beyond the powers conferred upon directors

A starting point of the following analysis is this: when the articles confer a general power to manage the business of a company on its board, and the board properly exercises such power by entering into a contract on behalf of the company with a third party, that contract must be valid and enforceable by each party according to its terms. This is clear and obvious because:

- a. the board had the requisite power to enter into the contract on behalf of the company; and
- b. the directors exercised such power properly.

A couple of linked questions arise, however, about the validity of the contract. What would be the legal effect upon the contract if:

- a. the articles did not in fact confer the power of management upon the board; or
- b. the articles did indeed confer the power of management upon the directors, but the board did not exercise such power correctly by following the required corporate procedures?

Would these matters cause the contract to be invalid and unenforceable against the company?

Prima facie , decisions made on behalf of a company can be made only by the board of directors acting in accordance with, and pursuant to, the terms of their powers as set out in the articles. If a specific power has not been expressly or impliedly conferred, a director does not have power to act. But how does this affect the legality of a transaction purportedly entered into by the directors on behalf of a company but where either:

- a. the transaction was outside or beyond the powers conferred on them; or
- b. the board's decision-making process suffered from some irregularity in the company's internal procedures?

Is this contract invalid (so that the company can wriggle out of liability) or can it still be enforced by the third party? Surely there is a need to protect third parties dealing with the company in these circumstances?

For example, assume that the directors of AB Ltd are given a general power to manage the company and purport to purchase a quantity of iron and steel on credit from CD in order to build a railway in Belgium. In fact, the lawful objects of AB Ltd are restricted to building railway carriages. 74 Logically, the contract would be void. It could not therefore be enforced by either AB Ltd (if CD failed to deliver the goods) or CD (if AB Ltd failed to pay for them). This conclusion is justified on the basis that the transaction was entered into by the exercise of powers that the directors did not have. The directors could exercise their powers only in pursuance of proper objectives. The directors therefore lacked the authority to enter into a contract for a purpose that was outside or beyond their powers.

Such an outcome is harsh for the third party. It would mean that a third party could not safely assume that the person with whom they are dealing is, in fact, a director and is acting in accordance with the powers conferred by the company's articles.

The common law and modern companies legislation have actually come to the rescue of third parties in these circumstances. The contract between the company and third party is deemed valid provided certain identified pre-conditions are satisfied. Note, however, that the rules we are about to review apply only to the external liability of a company to third parties. They do not affect the internal liability of the directors to their company - a director who purports to act on behalf of the company (by entering into a transaction with another) but who does not have the authority to do so, and who thereby causes loss to the company, may be held liable to compensate the company on the grounds of a breach of duty (see section 4 below). The company is nevertheless bound by the terms of the contract entered into on its behalf, even though the directors had acted beyond their powers and authority, i.e. in a manner that was ultra vires .

## 1.3.1  The common law position, the rule in Royal British Bank

Whenever a director purports to enter into a contract on behalf of a company (AB Ltd) with a third party (CD) but in fact has not been given the necessary power or authority to do so by the articles, the other contracting party (CD) may nevertheless enforce the contract, provided:

- a. the third party acted honestly and in good faith; and
- b. did not know, or could not reasonably have known, that the company's internal management requirements had not been met.

The facts of Royal British Bank v Turquand 75 were these: it was provided in the articles that the company could borrow money from third parties provided such borrowing was authorised by the shareholders of the company in general meeting. Notwithstanding this provision, the directors of the company borrowed significant funds from Royal British Bank, issuing the bank with a mortgage document executed by the company's common seal, but did not first obtain a resolution of the company's shareholders to authorise the borrowing. The company subsequently collapsed and went into liquidation. During the winding-up process, the liquidator of the company argued that the company was not liable to repay the bank loan, as the funds had been borrowed by the directors without the necessary authority being obtained under the terms of the articles. This argument was rejected. It was held that a third party dealing with a company through its directors is not adversely affected by any irregularities in the internal procedures of the company, provided the third party acted in good faith and did not have notice of the irregularity. The loan agreement, as evidenced by the mortgage document, was therefore binding. A third party, such as Royal British Bank, may therefore successfully bring a lawsuit against the company for breach of contract. The company cannot assert that the transaction does not bind the company because the directors had exceeded their powers or had failed to comply with the company's procedures as set out in the articles.

Some jurisdictions have not enacted provision in their companies legislation dealing with this matter. In those jurisdictions where the legislation is silent, the common law rule in Royal British Bank applies.

## 1.3.2  Modern statutory provision

Modern companies legislation enacted in a number of common law jurisdictions has codified the rule in Royal British Bank to make it abundantly clear that a third party may assume that a director with whom they are dealing is acting within his or her powers. Accordingly, whenever a director enters into a transaction on behalf of a company, the company becomes bound by the transaction, notwithstanding anything in the memorandum or articles, or in any resolution of the shareholders of the company, which purports to restrict the director's ability to enter into the transaction.

The companies legislation of the major offshore centres that codify the rule in Royal British Bank tends to do so by broadly following the English template which is now found in s.40 of the Companies Act 2006, and states:

'In favour of a person dealing with a company in good faith, the power of the company's directors to bind it, or to authorise others to do so, is deemed free from any limitation imposed under the company's constitution.'

It may be seen from the above extract that not only is a contract entered into by the directors binding upon the company, but so is a contract entered into by other persons duly authorised to act for the company, such as an executive to whom the directors have delegated all or some of their authority. Nonetheless, as Gower 76 points out, notwithstanding this type of protection afforded to third parties dealing with a company, it would be absurd for a third party to assume that the company's directors had authorised the check-out operator to sell the supermarket. In such circumstances, whenever the third party is dealing with someone other than the directors of the company, it must be reasonable for that person to be given authority to bind the company. If it is not reasonable, the third party will not be able to satisfy the good faith criterion, and the transaction will be void and unenforceable.

The BVI companies legislation follows the essential principles of the English legislation, but goes a little further and is more explicit. Section 31(1) of the BVI BCA 2004 states:

'A [BVI BC] … may not assert against a person dealing with the company, or with a person who has acquired assets, rights or interests from the company, that

- (a) … the articles of the company have not been complied with, or
- (b) a person named as a director in the company's register of directors
- (i) is not a director of the company,
- (ii) has not been duly appointed as a director of the company, or
- (iii) does not have authority to exercise a power which a director of a company … customarily has authority to exercise,

- (c) a person held out by the company as a director, employee or agent of the company
- (i) has not been duly appointed, or
- (ii) does not have authority to exercise a power which a director, employee or agent of a company … customarily has authority to exercise,
- (d) a person held out by the company as a director, employee or agent of the company with authority to exercise a power which a director, employee or agent of a company … customarily has authority to exercise, does not have authority to exercise that power,

unless the person has, or ought to have, by virtue of his relationship to the company, knowledge of the matters referred to in any of paragraphs (a) to (d).'

The statutory provisions that codify the rule in Royal British Bank are reproduced in the BVI, Guernsey, Hong Kong and the Isle of Man. 77  In these circumstances, the common law continues to apply in jurisdictions where the companies legislation is silent, such as the Bahamas, Bermuda, the Cayman Islands, Jersey and Singapore.

In summary, therefore, it is the board of directors that negotiate, and then enter into deals, contracts and other transactions, on behalf of the company. This subject matter bleeds into the law of contract and, in particular, two supplemental topics, namely the formalities required for a company to enter into contracts and transactions and their execution and authentication.

## 1.4 Entering into contracts and transactions, execution and authentication

## 1.4.1  Entering into contracts and transactions

The 1948 template deals with how corporate contracts and transactions should be entered into. Section 32 of the Companies Act 1948 states that transactions and contracts entered into by a company may be made as follows:

- a. a transaction that, if entered into between persons, would be required to be in writing and sealed (such as to buy or sell an interest in real estate or confer a power of attorney), shall be made in writing and lawfully sealed with the common seal of the company or, more modernly, by a director, or person acting under his lawful authority, executing the deed and expressing an intention that it be regarded as executed as a deed and under seal;
- b. a transaction that, if entered into between persons, would be required to be in writing and sealed (such as to buy or sell an interest in real estate or confer a power of attorney), shall be made in writing and lawfully sealed with the common seal of the company or, more modernly, by a director, or person acting under his lawful authority, executing the deed and expressing an intention that it be regarded as executed as a deed and under seal; and
- c. a contract that, if made between persons, would be valid if made verbally, may be made verbally by a director or person acting under his lawful authority.

These statutory provisions based upon the 1948 Act are reproduced across the offshore jurisdictions. 78

## 1.4.2  Execution and authentication of documentation

The 1948 template deals with how corporate contracts should be executed. Section 36 of the Companies Act 1948 states that: 'A document ... requiring authentication by a company may be signed by a director, secretary or other authorised officer of the company.'

This provision was reproduced in s.41 of the Companies Act 1985, but not the 2006 Act, which states that a document is validly executed by a company if it is signed on behalf of the company by two directors, or a director and secretary, or by a single director in the presence of a witness.

It is generally the 1948 Act template that is reproduced across the offshore jurisdictions. 79

Where there is no statutory provision, it is submitted that, as a matter of general principle, the propositions in the 1948 Act will nonetheless be followed. Accordingly, when the board of directors (or a sole director) enters into a deal, contract or transaction on behalf of the company, the directors should prima facie execute the documentation, or authorise others to do so on their behalf by, for instance, board resolution or power of attorney.

## 2. Duties of directors

The articles confer wide powers upon the directors. They may, for instance, raise capital (by share issue or by borrowing) and use that capital in an infinite number of ways to earn a profit for the company and its shareholders. The directors possess most of the power to run the company, subject only to a limited role played by the shareholders.

Are the directors free to exercise their powers in any way they wish? Are their powers unfettered and free from any sort of implied restriction or limitation?

No (to both questions). The directors are subject to two broad duties that they owe to the company:

- i. the fiduciary duty to act honestly and in good faith; and
- ii. the duty to exercise reasonable and appropriate care and skill in the exercise of their powers.

The former is an equitable duty in so far as it developed in the Court of Chancery in medieval England to ensure that persons in a position of trust, who may affect the legal position of others (e.g. trustees), behaved with impeccable honesty and integrity towards their principals (e.g. the beneficiaries of the trust).

<!-- image -->

The latter is a common law duty of care in so far as it developed to ensure that, in circumstances where it is reasonably foreseeable that the actions of one person may affect another (their neighbour), that person must act with reasonable care and skill to prevent loss or injury to the neighbour.

These implied equitable and common law duties are imposed upon the directors of a company by judge-made law that developed alongside the evolution of companies in the manner described in Module 2 sections 2.1 and 2.2. In some jurisdictions these duties have been reiterated by modern company legislation. For instance, the BVI BCA 2004 states:

## ' Section 120

1. A director of a company in exercising his powers or in performing his duties shall act honestly and in good faith and in what the director believes to be in the best interests of the company

## Section 122

... exercise the care, diligence and skill that a reasonable director would exercise in the same circumstances, taking into account the nature of the company, the nature of the decision and the position of the director and nature of the responsibilities undertaken.'

## 2.1 The fiduciary duty

Directors stand in a fiduciary position of trust. A trustee owes fiduciary duties to the beneficiaries of a trust, whereas a director owes similar fiduciary duties to the company.

The current companies legislation in England codifies the various components of the fiduciary duty in ss.170-177 of the Companies Act 2006. 81  The companies legislation of the offshore financial centres has not followed suit. Instead, the elements of the fiduciary duty have been identified piecemeal as common law and equitable principles. These following two components of the directors' duties are distilled from case law: essentially directors must always:

- exercise their powers on behalf of the company in good faith and in the best interests of the company

81.

The statutory components of a director's fiduciary duty are:

a. to act within the scope of the powers that have been conferred upon them (s.171 of the Companies Act 2006)

b. to act in good faith and promote the success of the company (s.172)

c. to exercise independent judgement (s.173)

d. to avoid conflicts of interests (s.175)

e. to not accept benefits from third parties (s.176)

f. to be transparent and declare interests in proposed transactions (s.177)

(The statutory duty of care, which we shall address in section 2.2, is codified in s.174 of the CA 2006.)

- be loyal to the company and not allow their own self-interest, or duties that they may owe to others, to conflict with their duty to the company.

The statutory codification in England is a modern interpretation of the common law and equitable principles that apply elsewhere and there is, of course, overlap between the two aspects of the directors' fiduciary duty.

## 2.1.1 Duty to act in good faith in the best interests of the company

Directors must act honestly. This is the basic, core element of the fiduciary duty insofar as it applies to every decision a director makes. Whatever action directors take, whatever power they exercise, they must honestly believe that such action benefits the company. This common law rule differs from what is now found in s.171 of the Companies Act 2006 of England, which requires directors to 'promote the success of the company' by having regard not only to the interests of shareholders, but also to the interests of employees, business relationships with suppliers, customers and others, the community and the environment and the desirability of maintaining a reputation for high standards of business.

The common law rule essentially means that when a director makes a decision, carries out an act or enters into a transaction, it must benefit the body of shareholders. It involves a balancing exercise between the short-term interests of the shareholders (to make a quick buck) and the long-term interests of the company as a whole. The common law duty is not owed to individual shareholders, nor to employees, customers, creditors or indeed regulators. It is owed to the company as a whole. Naturally, however, actions designed to attract customers, benefit the workforce and keep the regulator happy are also likely to be beneficial to the company as well as to the company's shareholders.

These duties continue even after a director has ceased to be a director. For example, former directors may not use for their personal advantage any information that came to them when and because they were directors. A summary of directors' fiduciary duties can be found in the judgment of Hunter Kane Ltd v Watkins. 82

That directors must exercise the powers conferred upon them in the best interests of the company means, in practice, that they must use their powers for proper purposes. It follows that the directors must not use their powers for an improper or collateral purpose that does not benefit the company.

A classic discussion of the rule that the powers of management of directors must be exercised for proper purposes is found in the old case of Hutton v West Cork Railway Co : 83

'[Corporate funds are] not theirs but the company's. [It is fine] if they are spending it for purposes which are reasonably incidental to the carrying on of the business of the company. Bona fides cannot be the sole test, otherwise you might have a lunatic conducting the affairs of the company, and paying away its money with both hands in a manner perfectly bona fide yet perfectly irrational … It is for the directors to judge, provided it is a matter which is reasonably incidental to the carrying on of the business of the company … The law does not say that there are to be no cakes and ale,

but there are to be no cakes and ale except such as are required for the benefit of the company.'

Examples of breach of this component of the fiduciary duty are numerous.

- Directors are in breach of fiduciary duty if they exercise their powers for their own purposes. The landmark decision in Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver 84 was based upon complex facts, but essentially Company A owned a cinema and the directors resolved for Company A to incorporate a subsidiary, Company B, and for Company B to acquire more cinemas so the group could be sold at a profit. Before the initial transaction was complete the terms were changed. Instead of the shares of Company B being issued to Company A, they were issued to the directors in their personal name. Later, all three cinemas were sold at a considerable profit. Had the original resolution been implemented the profit would have been earned by Company A; instead it was shared with Company B. Company A successfully brought a lawsuit against its directors for breach of their fiduciary duty by exercising powers for an improper purpose that benefited themselves rather than the company.
- Directors are in breach of fiduciary duty if they use corporate assets for purposes that benefit their friends, families or associates rather than the company. In Lee Panavision Ltd v Lee Lighting Ltd , 85  a director of Company A negotiated, on behalf of Company A, a management agreement with Company B, of which he was the controlling shareholder, on terms most beneficial to Company B. The court held that the director was in breach of his fiduciary duty to Company A: he had used his powers as director of Company A for a collateral purpose that did not benefit the company.
- A decision by directors to give the company's assets away is a clear example of a decision that is not in the interests of the company. This is the case even though companies frequently make political donations or give money to support various causes. The problem is that the directors may wish to promote the political party or cause, but is it in the interests of the company to do so? It has been held, in the case of Re Lee 86 , that this type of donation can only be made when there is a bona fide belief that it could actually promote the prosperity of the company in some way.
- In Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi 87 a director was held in breach for attempting to persuade a client of the company to renew a contract with himself personally, rather than the company.
- Other examples of a breach of this aspect of a director's duty have arisen in relation to the exercise of a director's powers to make calls on shares, to refuse to register a transfer of shares and to expel a member.

## 2.1.2 The duty of loyalty

The director's duty of loyalty to the company may overlap with the above duty to act in the best interests of the company. The duty of loyalty can itself be broken down into three parts:

- i. a duty to avoid self-dealing and conflicts of interests (the so-called 'no-conflicts rule')

- ii. a duty to avoid making a personal profit unless authorised (the so-called 'no-profits rule')
- iii. a duty not to accept benefits from third parties (the so-called 'no-benefits (or bribes) rule' .

## i. The 'no -conflicts rule': to avoid self-dealing and conflicts of interests

Directors must not allow their own self-interest, or the interests of others, to conflict with their duty to the company. Directors can be in breach of this duty even though the company does not suffer loss. Breach occurs by allowing themselves to be cornered in a conflict situation.

The broad 'self-dealing rule' is that if a director buys property from, or sells property to, the company, that transaction may be set aside by the company no matter how fair it is. 88

The 'no-conflicts rule' extends this principle to any transaction with the company that a director is remotely interested in, such as:

- a loan made by the company to a director, or a director's friends or associates
- a payment made by the company on behalf of a director to the director's creditors (either with or without an understanding that the director will reimburse the company)
- an arrangement whereby the company guarantees a liability of a director to a creditor, or gives security on the director's behalf
- an assumption by the company of a debt owed by a director to a third party
- the provision of goods or services by the company to its director or their associate (at a discount or otherwise).

How, if at all, may directors enter into a personal contract with their company without being in breach of duty and risking that the contract will subsequently be set aside?

In answering this question it is necessary to look at three sources of law. Each offers an answer that is a slight variation on the same theme. The broad rule is that, before entering into the transaction, a director must make full disclosure in writing and obtain the prior informed consent of the company.

At common law it has been held that a director, before entering into a transaction with the company, should: 89

- make full disclosure of the facts relating to the proposed transaction to the shareholders of the company
- obtain the prior approval or ratification of the transaction by a resolution of the company in general meeting.

## ii. The 'no-profits rule': duty to avoid personal profits unless authorised

Fiduciaries must not make a profit from their position unless specifically authorised. Accordingly, directors must not profit from the use of corporate assets for their own benefit. If they do, they must turn over any profit to the company or pay compensation.

Examples include the following situations.

- As fiduciaries, directors have no right to be paid for their services, nor may they be given a gift or bonus out of the company's assets, unless authorised by the articles or by the shareholders of the company in general meeting. It is standard practice, therefore, to include an express article in the following terms: 93

'The remuneration of the directors shall from time to time be determined by the company in general meeting...'

- In an onshore trading company, it is usual for an executive director to be employed by the company under a fixed-term contract of service, and receive a salary. By contrast, the remuneration of offshore directors is a matter between the service provider and the client and is usually prescribed in the directors' service agreement (the so-called DSA, for example cl.3 of Appendix 21C of the online Study Resources).
- A director must not usurp a corporate opportunity. This is illustrated by the facts of:
- a. Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi , 94 referred to in section 2.1.1;
- b. O'Donnell v Shanahan ; 95  and
- c. a hypothetical situation where directors of a development company find land for sale at a very low price; the directors must not buy that land for themselves in their personal capacity - to do so would usurp a corporate opportunity in breach of their duty to the company.
- Are there any circumstances when a director may use corporate assets in the manner illustrated in option (c) of the above bullet point? For instance, if a company decides not to pursue a particular investment opportunity, may the director do so personally, using personal funds? Yes, provided the director first obtains the consent of the company in general meeting after full disclosure of all the relevant facts.

- Directors must not use confidential, price-sensitive, information that they acquire as directors for their own benefit. So, for example, if a director acquires information of an imminent take-over bid or adverse trading results that are not in the public domain, and the director uses that information to buy and/or sell shares in the company, the director:
- a. becomes liable to turn over any profit to the company for breach of fiduciary duty, i.e., for misusing corporate information and making a 'secret' profit; and
- b. may become liable for the criminal offence of 'insider dealing' by knowingly using inside information to trade in shares of the company on a stock exchange. 96
- Directors must not compete against their company. A director who does so becomes liable to hand over all the profits made on a personal basis to the company. So, for example,  a director of, say, a software company who opens a software store in the same city, or is merely a minority shareholder of a competing software company, becomes liable for breach of the duty of loyalty to the company. This is not to say that  directors cannot hold other directorships or indeed own, or hold executive positions in, other companies, or be in business on their own account at the same time as holding office as a director of a company. In each case, the informed consent of the company ought to be obtained first.

## iii. The no benefits (or bribes) rule: not to accept benefits from third parties

Directors must not keep for their personal benefit or advantage any gift or benefit from a third party conferred:

- a. by reason of being a director, or
- b. in exchange for doing, or not doing, something as director.

The risk of such benefits being, or looking very much like, a bribe, is such a clear conflict of interests, which risks distorting the proper exercise of the directors' powers, that it deserves a separate statement of principle.

If a director receives a bribe to do, or refrain from doing, something such as entering into a particular transaction, it amounts to a clear breach of fiduciary duty. 97 But it applies to any situation where a third party makes a gift or confers a personal benefit upon the director, irrespective of corrupt motive, or whether the payer intended to influence the director, or whether the director was actually influenced.

As with some other types of breach of duty, the receipt of this type of benefit can be 'whitewashed' by making full disclosure and obtaining shareholder approval. This is covered further in section 5.1.

## 2.2 Duty of care and skill

A director owes a duty to use care, skill and diligence in the management of the company and its business. A director whose management ability falls below that standard becomes liable to compensate the company.

The circumstances in which this duty is often enforced are usually:

- when a public company suffers loss as a result of the acts, or omissions to act, of an errant director
- when a company becomes insolvent and liquidators are appointed to wind up the affairs of the company - the liquidators often investigate the actions of the directors with a fine-tooth comb, and, if they consider that management has lacked the appropriate care and skill, they will issue legal proceedings on behalf of the company for damages; if the lawsuit is successful, any damages payable by an errant director are used by the liquidators to pay the company's creditors.

The nature of the duty to use care, skill and diligence evolved from English common law. We shall review the development of the principle at common law and then analyse how it has been affected by modern companies legislation.

## 2.2.1  The common law principle

The traditional common law duty of care owed by directors was originally formulated in Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co. 98  In that case, an insurance company made significant losses, owing mainly to the incompetent investment strategies, and ultimately the outright fraud, of its board chairman. The company was rendered insolvent and it was ordered by the court to be wound up. By this time, the chairman had disappeared and was untraceable. The court-appointed liquidator issued legal proceedings against the chairman's co- (non-executive) directors on the grounds that they were in breach of their duty of care in failing to supervise or exercise any effective control over the chairman.

It was argued by the liquidator that the co-directors should not have blindly accepted the decisions and representations of, and the false financial statements tendered to them by, the chairman. The directors, however, argued that they were entitled to rely on the apparent competence of the chairman, and also to rely upon the auditors to discover if the chairman was up to no good.

The court held that directors must perform their duties with the care and skill that may be reasonably expected of a person with their knowledge and experience . The directors in question were honourable but inexperienced amateurs, not versed in the management of an insurance company. They therefore avoided liability!

The decision in City Equitable called for a subjective test to be used when assessing the liability of directors - the standard of care expected varied according to the knowledge and experience of the director concerned. A lesser standard was expected of the less intelligent and less experienced director, who could therefore avoid liability for incompetent mistakes. The decision was justified on the basis that, according to the traditional corporate model, the shareholders elected the directors. If they chose stupid, incompetent directors to run their company, it was their own fault. They could not then complain and, through the company, commence legal proceedings against the directors for damages, by alleging breach of duty.

The subjective test propounded in the City Equitable case was heavily criticised. It was said that the subjective test protected the bumbling amateur director against liability when this was precisely the type of incompetent director against whom the law ought to protect the company, its creditors and the public. A more onerous, more objective standard of care and skill was called for in order to tighten up corporate governance.

Accordingly, the test has been strengthened. A wholly objective test is now appropriate in the modern context. In the later case of Dorchester Finance Co. Ltd v Stebbing 99 it was held that a director must nowadays exercise such care and skill as might reasonably be expected of an ordinary person. In Norman v Theodore Goddard 100 it was held that a director must exercise such care and skill as might reasonably be expected of a person carrying out the functions of a director.

These are subtle but important distinctions. Under City Equitable the test was a subjective one - the standard of care expected varies with the competence and experience of the director. In the case of Dorchester Finance and in Norman the modern test is an objective one. In the former case it was the standard reasonably expected of an ordinarily competent person; in the latter it appears to be a higher standard: that expected of an ordinarily competent director.

## 2.2.2  Modern offshore statutory provision

The judicial debate about the appropriate standard of care expected of a director under judge-made, common law principles came to a halt in England when the appropriate test was codified by companies legislation. The modern statutory rule in England is found in s.174 of the Companies Act 2006 and is a combination of objectivity and subjectivity.

Directors, under English law, are expected to exercise the care, skill and diligence of a reasonably diligent person (an objective test) with:

- the general knowledge, skill and experience of a person (still objective) carrying out the functions of that director (subjective)
- the general knowledge, skill and experience of that particular director (again, subjective).

The statutory standard of care under modern offshore companies legislation, in jurisdictions where it is enacted, is either purely objective or a combination of objective and subjective, tending to follow the Dorchester Finance formulation set out above.

For example, in Singaporean companies legislation 101  a simple objective test is laid down insofar as it states:

'A director shall at all times ... use reasonable diligence in the discharge of the duties of his office.'

The companies legislation in the Bahamas, Bermuda and Jersey 102  states that a director must 'exercise the care, diligence and skill that a reasonably prudent person would exercise in comparable circumstances' .

These are essentially objective tests. Every director is judged against the same standard. The standard is that expected of a reasonably prudent person (i.e. an ordinarily prudent person but one without the special skills or experience of a business person). Nonetheless, the standard retains an element of subjectivity, namely the reasonable standard expected in 'comparable circumstances' . This enables the standard of care required of a director to float up or down, not according to the intelligence or experience of the director, but according to the type, structure, size of company and the act the director is accused of performing negligently (or wrongly omitting to perform at all).

The BVI companies legislation states at s.122 of the BVI BCA that:

'A director of a company, when exercising powers or performing duties as a director, shall exercise the care, skill and diligence that a reasonable director would exercise in the same circumstances taking into account:

- a. the nature of the company
- b. the nature of the decision, and
- c. the position of the director and the nature of the responsibilities undertaken by him.'

The new BVI statutory duty of care is, like its predecessor in the IBCA, part objective and part subjective. The objective test uses a notional 'reasonable director' as opposed to a reasonable person and therefore tends to follow the Theodore Goddard test. The subjective element lists specific Criteria (a)-(c), which can raise or lower the bar by influencing the level of care and skill expected in the circumstances.

Furthermore, Criterion (c) is very interesting. Could it be argued that a corporate director of a passive, money-box, asset-holding company owes a lower level of care and skill than a full-time, fully remunerated, executive director of a public or trading company?

## 2.2.3 The duty of care in practice

A review of some factual circumstances, some hypothetical, some from decided cases, may help to illustrate how the duty of care is applied in practice.

A failure to review corporate records by a director or to enquire into the dealings, or the proposed transactions, of a company may very well fall below the standard of care expected of a prudent person. A prudent person would surely not enter into a deal or transaction on their own behalf unless they were fully aware of all the circumstances and the foreseeable consequences! 103

If a director fails to complete an application for insurance form correctly before submitting it to the insurance company, giving the insurance company grounds to reject a subsequent claim (for example, when company property is damaged by fire), the director is probably negligent for failing to review or understand the form properly before completing it. The director will therefore be in breach of duty and becomes liable to compensate the company for its loss. 104

If the facts of City Equitable (see section 2.2.1) were decided today under modern objective statutory provisions, it is probable that the court would hold the non-executive directors negligent for failing to supervise their co-director and thereby falling below the standard of care expected of a reasonably prudent person. A prudent person would surely not unquestioningly follow claims made, or guidance or direction given, by third parties. A prudent person would surely consider the question of the third party's integrity and supervise a fellow director more closely.

In Dorchester Finance (see section 2.2.1), a company had three directors, A, B and C. B and C were in the habit of signing cheques in blank whenever A asked them to. As a result, A was able to defraud the company of substantial funds. B and C were held liable and obliged to compensate the company for its loss in failing to pay attention and inquire into corporate dealings, in particular for allowing A to defraud the company in these circumstances.

The signing of blank cheques by a director is clearly negligent. On the other hand, when signing a cheque already made out by the company's bookkeeper in favour of a payee, does a director have to double-check that the moneys are in fact owed by the company to the payee? Probably not, although it depends upon, for instance, the accounting practices, and the size and structure of the company concerned. If, for instance, a cheque has been made out by an otherwise reliable accounts executive, giving no cause for suspicion, a director who fails to make enquiries will arguably not be held liable. If there are grounds for suspicion, however, the director must investigate the circumstances surrounding the proposed payment before signing the cheque, or be in breach of duty.

May directors be held in breach of duty for relying on information given to them by staff, or by company agents such as attorneys or auditors? Probably not, provided the directors have no grounds to suspect that the information is incorrect.

The issue of whether a director may rely upon others has been debated in a long line of cases, but most agree that the judgment in Re City Equitable contains an accurate proposition, namely that certain duties in running a company may properly be left to some other executive or official and that 'a director is, in the absence of grounds for suspicion, justified in trusting that official to perform his duties honestly' . This recognises the fact that some companies are big organisations and the directors cannot do everything. Running a company requires a large staff and, although the directors are responsible for monitoring and oversight, as Paul Davies points out in Gower, 105  a line has to be drawn between reasonable oversight and operational activity.

An interesting application of this rule is found in Re Barings plc. 106

In the case of Lexi Holdings plc v Luqman , it was held that co-directors ought not to have relied upon information provided by the managing director due to the particular circumstances and relationships. 107  The company's board borrowed £60 million from a consortium of banks, which was then stolen by the managing director (who was therefore clearly in breach of his fiduciary duty to the company). The issue was whether his two sisters and co-directors sitting on the board were liable for breach of their duty of care in relying on the company's financial statements and accounts and failing to adequately supervise the actions of their brother, the managing director. It was found that the state of the company's financial records and accounts were such that it should have aroused a reasonable suspicion and that it was well known within the family that the managing director had previously been imprisoned for deception. Their reliance upon information provided by him and their lack of supervision and oversight in the particular circumstances amounted to a breach of duty.

Lexi Holdings plc is in contrast to Madoff Securities International Ltd v Raven , where it was held that some directors were entitled to rely upon the expertise, experience and representations of Mr Madoff, who had held that certain unusual transactions were lawful and in the best interests of the company. These directors were not, therefore, liable for breach of their duty of care in failing to exercise appropriate supervision and oversight. Nonetheless, other directors, the inner circle, were not entitled to such reliance and so were held liable. 108

The propositions of law in City Equitable (and applied in Lexi Holdings plc and Madoff Securities ) has been codified by modern companies legislation, essentially in those jurisdictions that have enacted a statutory standard of care. 109

For example s.123 of the BVI BCA 2004 states:

- '(1) A director of a company, when exercising his powers or performing his duties as a director, is entitled to rely upon ... books, records, financial statements and other information prepared or supplied, and on professional or expert advice given, by
- (a) an employee of the company whom the director believes on reasonable grounds to be reliable and competent in relation to the matters concerned;
- (b) a professional adviser or expert in relation to matters which the director believes on reasonable grounds to be within the person's professional or expert competence; and

- (c) any other director, or committee of directors upon which the director did not serve, in relation to matters within the director's or committee's designated authority.
- (2) Subsection (1) applies only if the director
- (i) acts in good faith;
- (ii) makes proper inquiry where the need for the inquiry is indicated by the circumstances; and
- (iii) has no knowledge that his reliance on the ... books, records, financial statements and other information or expert advice is not warranted.'

May a director be held in breach of duty for relying on information received from third parties? Again, it depends on the facts, but it is not usually prudent to trust blindly the representations of a third party who is unconnected with the company and who does not owe the company any duty of care (for instance, who is not an employee, attorney or auditor of the company). The case of Bishopsgate Investment Management (In Liquidation) v Maxwell 110 is relevant here. A director of a company signed, as he was in the habit of doing, certain documents presented to him by his father Mr Maxwell Sr, who was not a director or other executive of the company, but was the prime mover behind it. The documents virtually signed away most of the company's assets. The director was held to have acted in breach of duty and became liable for the loss caused to the company by failing to make any enquiry into the documents he was asked to sign and the transactions to which they related.

How far can a director be held liable for bad business decisions made on behalf of the company? At common law, an honest error of business judgement made by a director, even though the company suffers massive losses, does not incur liability for breach of duty. This is justified on the grounds that the carrying on of business involves the taking of calculated risks. Calculated (or informed) risk taking is not negligent. For example, assume a director persuades the board to diversify business from textiles to fashion clothing, but the new venture is a complete disaster and suffers massive losses. Provided the director applied their mind in an informed manner, carefully assessing the risks of the new venture, they cannot be held liable if the decision turns out to have been a bad one. 111

## 2.2.4  Summary of directors' duties

The following propositions relating to a director's duty of care can be distilled from the cases.

- a. The formulation of the common law and statutory duty of care creates an objective test insofar as, when reaching and implementing business decisions, a director must exercise the care, skill and diligence of a reasonable person ( Dorchester Finance ).
- b. The directors of a company can divide different functions between board members, in which case a breach by one does not necessarily mean a breach by all unless, on the facts, there is a lack of reasonable supervision and oversight in the circumstances ( City Equitable , Lexi Holdings plc and Madoff Securities ).

<!-- image -->

- c. The directors can also delegate their management of the business to others, such as officers, managers and employees. Accordingly, a disastrous error made by an employee does not necessarily amount to a breach of duty of care owed by the directors unless it can be established that there was a negligent lack of supervision and oversight in the circumstances ( Re Barings ).
- d. Most cases arise when directors have made a terrible business decision which contributes to the insolvency of the company. But, as operating companies are designed for risk and return, the mere fact that the directors made an error of business judgement causing financial loss does not, in itself, amount to a breach of the duty of care. The so-called business judgement rule prevents a company bringing a lawsuit against its board every time it suffers a loss in any one financial period!

## 3. The position of (nominee?!) directors

It is usual for shareholders (or the beneficial owners of the shares) of a company incorporated offshore to appoint service providers, who carry on business from the offshore jurisdiction, to provide directors for the managed company. This goes some way towards ensuring that the company is managed and controlled offshore for tax purposes.

Such directors have, in the past, been colloquially called ' nominee directors ' .

The term 'nominee directors' is, however, a misnomer. 'Nominee' suggests that the incumbent directors merely have to follow the instructions given to them by their clients/the beneficial owners of the company, in much the same way as a nominee shareholder does. It is of the utmost importance that professionals in offshore company management practice realise that there is no such thing as a nominee director in this sense. Either the individual or corporate entity is a director (in which case they owe the fiduciary and other duties of care reviewed in sections 2.1 and 2.2 above) or they are not. If an individual or corporate entity accepts an appointment as director, in exchange for a fee, they are responsible as, and owe all the duties of, a director. They may therefore be held liable for breach.

Saying 'I was simply following the client's instructions' is no defence to a claim that a director is in breach of fiduciary or other duty of care to the company (save in circumstances where the prior approval or ratification of the shareholders of the company was obtained by resolution at a general meeting of the company - a topic reviewed in section 5.1 below).

## 4. Remedies for breach of duty

The remedies available to a company against an errant director depend upon whether the director is in breach of the fiduciary duty or the duty of care and skill.

A company may commence legal proceedings against a director who in in breach of the fiduciary duty by claiming one (or more) of the following remedies. The company may:

- set aside any transaction entered into between the company and the director (or an associate of that director), but not a transaction entered into between the company and a third party acting in good faith without notice 112
- force the errant director to turn over any undisclosed profit to the company
- force the errant director to indemnify the company against any loss suffered or liability incurred by the company as a result of the breach.

A director who is found to have exercised any of his or her powers in a manner that falls below the expected level of care and skill becomes liable to compensate the company for its loss.

In each case, it is a well established common law principle that only the company itself may commence legal proceedings in a court of law against the errant director (the so-called 'proper plaintiff' rule in Foss v Harbottle ). 113 This requires a resolution of the shareholders in general meeting. If too few shareholders vote in favour of taking legal action against the director, the company may not issue legal proceedings itself or lend its name to the lawsuit. Under common law principles, individual shareholders may not commence the lawsuit. This comes as a great surprise to our American colleagues, who are used to derivative class actions in these circumstances. This does not mean, however, that individual minority shareholders may go without redress in jurisdictions that follow the English practice. There is an exception to the rule in Foss v Harbottle that permit individual shareholders to bring an action where it can be established that the alleged wrongdoers are in control of the company so that it is impossible for the company to bring an action in its own name, and a fraud has been committed against the company, or at least against the minority shareholders.

So for example, in Pavlides v Jensen , 114 the three directors of a company sold its major asset (a mine) to an associated company at an undervalue. The minority shareholder brought a lawsuit against the three directors for compensation on the grounds that the price asked for the asset was manifestly too low.. It was held that the minority shareholder had no right to commence the legal proceedings in these circumstances. The judge said:

113. Foss v Harbottle

114. Pavlides v Jensen

'On the facts ... the sale of the company's mine was not beyond the powers of the directors in so far as it was not ultra vires . There is no allegation of fraud against the directors or appropriation of assets of the company. It was open to the company, by a resolution of the majority of the shareholders, to sell the mine at a price determined by the company. And it was open to the company by a vote of the majority of shareholders to decide that, if the directors by their negligence or error of judgement had sold the mine at an undervalue, proceeding should not be taken by the company against the directors.'

An act of negligence may, however, amount to fraud when the errant directors have received a benefit as a result of their wrongdoing at the company's expense. In Anglo Eastern v Knutz , 115  the minority shareholders brought a lawsuit against Knutz, a director and holder of 51% of the shares of a company, on the grounds that Knutz had wrongfully used his position as director to divert earnings to another company wholly owned by him. It was held that the derivative action brought by the minority shareholders was lawful as an exception to the 'proper plaintiff' rule in Foss v Harbottle , as Knutz had exercised his powers as a director fraudulently (referred to as 'a fraud on a power'). Likewise, in the UK cases of Daniels v Daniels 116  and Prudential Assurance Co Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd 117  it was held that minority shareholders could commence a derivative lawsuit against errant directors who had defrauded the company of funds for their own benefit.

In summary, a minority shareholder may only bring what is referred to as a derivative action in their personal name against a director if it is alleged that the director has acted fraudulently. In the Prudential Assurance case the court stated that the shareholder/claimant must establish as a preliminary issue that he or she has proper legal standing to commence the lawsuit, referred to as the court's 'gatekeeper role' . In the UK, a modern statutory version of this exception to the proper plaintiff rule in Foss v Harbottle was introduced by the Companies Act 2006 to bring English law more in line with the US by permitting individual shareholders to sue directors on behalf of the company (derivatively).

Part 11 Companies Act 2006 introduces a new statutory procedure in the UK where individual minority shareholders are permitted to bring legal proceedings against directors subject to conditions designed to constrain frivolous or vexatious claims. Having filed a lawsuit against a director the shareholder/claimant must seek the permission of the court to continue. The court will permit the claim to continue only when it is deemed to be in the best interests of the company as whole. This type of derivative action can be complex and is essentially beyond the syllabus. In any event, no such statutory procedure exists in the offshore financial centres where the so-called proper plaintiff rule in Foss v Harbottle continues to apply, subject to the exception when fraud is alleged against the director.

This leads us on to the subject of whether any defences are available to an otherwise negligent director or one in breach of fiduciary duty, the topic reviewed next.

## 5. Provisions to protect directors from liability

Directors may be able to protect themselves against incurring liability for acts that would otherwise amount to a breach of their fiduciary duty, or duty to use reasonable care and skill by obtaining:

- approval (or ratification) of those actions that may amount (or have already amounted) to a breach
- exoneration (sometimes called exculpatory or exclusion of liability) provisions in the company's articles and/or under the terms of the directors' service agreement
- an indemnity from the beneficial owner in the company management agreement
- indemnity insurance.

## 5.1 Approval/ratification by shareholders

Directors may obtain a release of all liability that they may otherwise incur in exercising their powers provided that:

- a. they make full disclosure to the shareholders of the circumstances of the (proposed) breach; and
- b. they obtain the shareholders' prior approval, or the subsequent ratification, of their actions, by ordinary resolution of the company in general meeting (or supermajority if the articles so require).

A prior approval of a director's action is obtained by a shareholders' vote, or written resolution, authorising the director to carry out a proposed action, in advance, before the act is carried out. A specimen written resolution of the sole shareholder of a company to approve a property transaction is illustrated in Appendix 23C.

A ratification occurs after a director has carried out a wrongful act that amounts to a breach, but that the shareholders subsequently adopt.

In each case, the shareholders effectively release the director from all liability incurred to the company for breach of duty. Neither the company, nor the liquidator in the event of an insolvent winding-up of the company, may then commence legal proceedings against the director alleging a breach of duty.

A final issue is whether all breaches of duty can be whitewashed by obtaining the prior approval or ratification of the majority of shareholders. Problems may clearly occur, for instance, when a dishonest director is also a majority shareholder of the company. For example, assume that XYZ Ltd has two directors, A and B, each owning 25.5% of the voting share capital. A and B may, as directors, unlawfully transfer corporate assets to themselves, and then, as majority shareholders, ratify their actions and release themselves from liability by combining their votes and passing an ordinary resolution by a simple majority of 51%.

The so-called common law 'doctrine of non-ratifiable breaches' (which, incidentally, also applies to prior approvals) 118 states that directors who are also the majority shareholders of a company cannot ratify the expropriation to themselves of corporate property in the manner described above. 119

In summary, a director may be released from liability to the company for breach of duty by obtaining the prior approval of, or ratification by, the shareholders for the following actions:

- buying property from, or selling to, the company, in breach of the self-dealing rule
- entering into a contract with the company, in breach of the no-conflicts rule
- receiving a benefit from a third party, in breach of the no-benefits rule
- exercising the management function of the business or over corporate assets in a negligent way, in breach of the duty of care.

But a director who has expropriated assets from the company to the prejudice of its minority shareholders or creditors cannot ratify, and thereby obtain a release from liability for, a fraudulent or dishonest breach of the fiduciary duty of good faith.

## 5.2 Provisions in the articles and/or terms of the director's service agreement

Express provisions in the articles may excuse a director from what would otherwise amount to a breach of fiduciary duty.

For example, a typical article similar to art.18.2 of Appendix 1B permits directors to breach the self-dealing and the no-conflicts rule by authorising them to transact business, or enter into a contract with, the company provided that they make full disclosure of their interest to the company and obtain its informed consent in general meeting. 120 Similarly, a typical article, such as art.18.1 or 18.3 of Appendix 1B, permits directors to breach the no-profits and part of the no-benefits rule by authorising them to make and/or retain a profit made from their directorial position, again provided full disclosure is made to the company in general meeting of the circumstances and amount of the anticipated profit. 121 These types of article are lawful, according to their terms, across all jurisdictions.

Historically, in England, certain articles have tried to go further and exonerate and/or indemnify directors from liability for all breaches of duty. This was considered abusive so, in s.205 of the 1948 Companies Act, the English Parliament introduced a principle that provisions in the articles, or in a contract between the company and the directors, that purport to exonerate a director from liability for breach of duty is void and of no legal effect. The current prohibition of exoneration provisions is extended by s.232 of the Companies Act 2006 to provisions that purport to indemnify a director in respect of similar liabilities. So, have the offshore financial centres followed suit?

Company management service providers in a number of offshore jurisdictions include a standard article in the articles of companies under their management that releases the directors (essentially, the service provider) from liability for negligent breaches of duty.

A typical article, such as art.29.1 of Appendix 1B, provides a general exoneration to the effect that directors and officers of the company are exonerated from liability to the company for negligent acts or omissions to act, but can still be held liable to the company for acts or omissions that amount to fraud or dishonesty.

A further article, such as art.29.2 of Appendix 1B, also confers an indemnity upon the directors. By this article the company effectively promises that, if the directors become liable to third parties arising from negligent acts or omissions in the performance of their role as directors, the company will indemnify them. In other words, the company will reimburse the directors, or essentially pay any compensation ordered to be paid by them in favour of the third party, unless such liability is grounded upon the directors' fraud or dishonesty. As Paul Davies points out 'the director remains liable but the company picks up the financial consequences of that liability' . 122

A similar type of exoneration clause (such as para.9.1 of Appendix 21C) and similar type of indemnity clause (such as para.9.2 of Appendix 21C) may also appear in a DSA with the company.

These types of exoneration and indemnity provisions, whether contained in the company's articles or in the contractual provisions of a DSA, are outlawed in the UK by s.232 of the Companies Act 2006. They are also prohibited by companies legislation in some other common law jurisdictions. 123  Companies legislation of such jurisdictions provides that any provision, whether in the articles or in any contract between the directors and the company, which purports to exempt officers of a company from liability (or to indemnify them against liability to a third party) for behaviour that would otherwise amount to negligence, default or breach of their duty to the company, is void and of no effect.

This legislative provision does not exist in the major Caribbean offshore jurisdictions. Exoneration clauses and indemnification provisions in the articles of a company and in a DSA are expressly made lawful by the companies legislation of jurisdictions such as the Bahamas, BVI and Bermuda. 124  For example, s.98 of the Bermudian Companies Act 1981, as amended 2004, states:

- '(1) Subject to subsection (2), a company may in its articles or in any contract between the company and any officer ... exempt such officer or indemnify him in respect of any loss arising or liability attaching to him ... in respect of any negligence, default or breach of duty of which the officer may be guilty in relation to the company.

<!-- image -->

- (2) Any provision, whether contained in the articles or in a contract between the company and any officer ... exempting such officer from or indemnifying him against any liability which would otherwise attach to him in respect of any fraud or dishonesty of which he may be guilty in relation to the company shall be void.'

Nor does it prevent a third party from bringing a lawsuit against the service provider as administrator. The client beneficial owner merely provides an indemnity.
- There is always a risk that a court may strike down an exoneration and indemnity clause and declare them to be unenforceable. There is a line of cases 125 that confirm that these clauses must, firstly, exonerate a service provider from liability for acts of negligence only, and that clauses that are too wide and seek to exclude all liability howsoever caused will be struck down and rendered unenforceable. Secondly, the client is required to have consented to the inclusion of the clause. In other words, the clauses must have been specifically drawn to the client's notice at the time the document is executed, and the client must understand their meaning and consequences. Again, the consequence of default is that the clause will be struck down.

## 5.4 Indemnity insurance

Many (if not all) service providers on behalf of managed companies take out 'directors' and officers' insurance' policies to protect the directors from liability. These policies provide insurance cover for a company and/or a director in the event that the director is found liable, either to the company for breach of duty, or to a third party.

The precise terms of the insurance cover differ from policy to policy. A standard policy, however, will generally indemnify a company's director against liability incurred in acting as a director, provided the director has acted at all times in good faith. In other words, the director is protected against liability for negligent breaches of duty and inadvertent liability to third parties. Owing to what the insurance industry calls 'moral hazard' this type of insurance does not provide cover against liability incurred by a deliberate default, or a fraudulent or dishonest breach of duty.

There was a body of opinion in the UK that such insurance was void under the statutory provisions discussed in section 5.2 above. The Companies Act 1989 clarified the point, however, and s.232 of the Companies Act 2006 now confirms that directors' and officers' insurance is lawful. There is often express provision in offshore companies legislation 126 specifically permitting a company and/or a director to take out this sort of insurance cover. It may also be a contractual term of the director's service agreement (see cl.12 of the DSA illustrated in Appendix 21C of the online Study Resources), and a provision in the articles may confer a power to purchase and maintain indemnity insurance upon the directors (see art.53 of Appendix 1C).

In these times of increased regulation of offshore financial services, this type of insurance cover is made a mandatory requirement for the provision of directors for managed companies in jurisdictions that have regulated company management business.

## 6. Directors' personal liability to creditors for company debts

Section 2.2 above explains that if a company becomes insolvent and a liquidator is appointed, one of the duties of the liquidator is to investigate the actions of the directors in the period before the liquidation.

None of the provisions reviewed in section 5 above provide any protection for directors who have allowed a company to continue to trade while they knew (or, sometimes, ought reasonably to have known) that the company was technically insolvent. In such circumstances, when a company is being wound-up because of its insolvency, a court can declare that the directors are personally liable for all or part of the outstanding debts and liabilities of the company.

There is an interesting history to this principle which it is necessary to understand in order to determine whether it applies in the offshore financial centres.

Section 332 of the Companies Act 1948 (upon which a great deal of offshore companies legislation is based) created both criminal and civil liability when a person carries on the business of a company with intent to defraud creditors. 127  This became known as ' fraudulent trading' . The class of person who may be held liable is any person who carries on the business of the company, and this is likely to be the directors. The director of an insolvent company can only be held liable if it can be established that they were fraudulent. Fraud is difficult to establish, but it is usually inferred when a company continues to carry on its business and incur debts at a time when, to the knowledge of the directors, the company is insolvent and there is no prospect that the creditors will be paid. 128 In other words, creditors have to establish fraud on the part of the directors on a subjective basis.

(1) If in the course of winding up a company it appears that the business of the company has been carried on with intent to defraud creditors of the company ...

(2) the court, on the application of the liquidator, may declare that any persons who were knowingly parties to the carrying on of the business in that manner are liable to make such contributions to the company's assets that the court thinks proper.

<!-- image -->

The fraudulent trading provisions of s.332 of the Companies Act 1948 have found their way into most offshore companies legislation. 129

Unfortunately, creditors found it difficult to establish fraudulent trading in practice. This is because the fraudulent trading provisions required knowledge in order to establish actual fraudulent intent on the part of the directors on a subjective level. Clearly, many directors were able to wriggle out of liability on the grounds that they did not know that there was no prospect that the creditors would be paid. Accordingly, the provisions of s.332 of the Companies Act 1948 were extended in England by a further remedy, originally called 'reckless trading' but then re-branded ' wrongful trading' .

The remedy of wrongful trading was introduced not in the companies legislation but in the English insolvency legislation, in s.214 of the Insolvency Act 1986. The remedy is much the same as for fraudulent trading insofar as the court can hold the directors liable to pay corporate debts when they continue to trade whilst the company is insolvent. Subjective fraud does not, however, have to be established before a director becomes liable. A director becomes liable for wrongful trading when they 'knew or ought to have known that there was no prospect that the company could avoid going into insolvent liquidation' . This introduces an objective test. If a reasonably competent director in similar circumstances would have concluded that there was no prospect that creditors would be paid then the director of the insolvent company is liable. Accordingly, it is far easier to prove wrongful trading than it is to prove fraudulent trading.

The question is, has wrongful trading found its way into offshore companies or insolvency legislation? It has in some, but not others. 130
