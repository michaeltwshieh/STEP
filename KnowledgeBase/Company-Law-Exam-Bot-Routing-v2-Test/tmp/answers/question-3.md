The proposition is too broad. Its four labels, "stand-alone", "one-man", "asset-holding" and "offshore", describe the company's structure and use. They do not deprive an incorporated company of its own legal personality. Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd (1897) dealt with almost the same alter ego argument. Mr Salomon held 19,994 of the company's 20,000 shares, while his wife, daughter and four sons each held one share as his nominees. The business had previously been his own. When the company failed, the lower courts treated it as his alias and made him answer for its debts. The House of Lords reversed that result. Compliance with the incorporation legislation created a separate person, and a one-man company was neither an abuse nor a sham merely because one individual had the whole beneficial interest. That answer applies with particular force to the company described in the question.

Salomon also shows why the distinction has practical consequences. The company had assets of GBP6,000, unsecured debts of GBP7,000 and a GBP10,000 secured loan note originally granted to Mr Salomon. The security could take priority although the unsecured creditors recovered nothing. A member of a company limited by shares normally risks the agreed issue price and any amount still unpaid on the shares. The company remains fully liable for its own obligations. Treating every one-man offshore company as its owner's other self would discard that allocation of liability without any fraud, guarantee or statutory ground.

The asset-holding description makes separate ownership more, rather than less, relevant. An offshore company may hold real estate, shares, intellectual property, art or a yacht and may receive rent, dividends, interest, royalties or licence fees. Legal title and the resulting income belong to the company. The beneficial owner owns or benefits from the shares; that person does not directly own each corporate asset. Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd (1925) makes the line sharp. Macaura was the sole shareholder, but insurance taken in his own name did not cover timber owned by his company. His complete economic interest could not supply the personal proprietary interest required by the policy. The company can also contract, operate an account, sue and be sued in its own name, and its property survives the owner's death or a transfer of the shares. Those results cannot sensibly coexist with routine alter ego treatment.

There is, however, a practical reason for the quoted view. Some offshore asset-holding companies are used as a client's "money box". The client supplies the assets, expects payments on demand and may seek access to the bank account. A beneficial owner can also become a director, act as investment adviser, receive a special power of attorney or make recommendations to the board. A general power of attorney or unrestricted control of the bank account can leave that owner with nearly complete factual control. Such arrangements may create management-and-control tax exposure, weaken confidentiality and place directors at risk if corporate funds are used without proper authority. They support the description of the company as an alter ego in everyday speech. They still leave the legal acts to be attributed through the company's organs and authorised agents.

The question gives no formal capacity for the onshore beneficial owner. If that person is also the sole member and sole director, ownership and management may coincide in practice. Even then, the company remains the owner of the assets and the director exercises corporate powers. If an offshore service provider supplies the board, the articles determine how far the owner may direct it. Under a 1948 Table A style provision the board manages independently, whereas a 1985 Table A or English 2008 model-article structure may permit directions by special resolution. Section 109 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 places management under the direction or supervision of the directors; section 40 of the Bahamian International Business Companies Act 2000 takes a similar board-management approach subject to the memorandum and articles. No articles or jurisdiction are supplied here, so personal control cannot be presumed from beneficial ownership.

Director duties reinforce that conclusion. An owner who becomes a director does not acquire a personal licence to use the company's assets. By way of a specific offshore example, sections 120 and 122 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 require honesty and good faith in what the director believes to be the company's best interests, together with the care, diligence and skill of a reasonable director in the circumstances. A service-provider director is equally a director. The label "nominee director" does not reduce that office to obedience, and following the beneficial owner's instructions is no general defence to breach. The distinction between owner and company therefore has work to do even where one individual supplies the funds and makes every commercial recommendation.

Alter ego treatment becomes available on exceptional facts. Adams v Cape Industries and VTB Capital plc v Nutritek International Corp are presented as authority for a very confined power to disregard separate personality. The fraud category requires a company that is a sham or mere facade and is used to evade an existing legal obligation. In Jones v Lipman (1962), a person ordered to transfer property moved it to his solely owned company to defeat that obligation, so the court reached the company's property. Gencor ACP Ltd v Dalby (2000) concerned stolen money placed in a private company owned and controlled by the dishonest director. Wallersteiner v Moir (1974) involved a network of corporate fronts holding money obtained by deception. Each case contains misuse beyond one-person ownership, offshore incorporation or passive asset holding. The question supplies none of it.

Control and funding alone also fall short of automatic agency. In Atlas Maritime Co SA v Avalon Maritime Ltd, The Coral Rose (1991), the court treated a minimally liable subsidiary funded and directed by its parent as a common commercial structure, rather than an agency relationship arising merely from those features. That was a group-company case, but the point cuts against the wider assertion here. Separate boards, bank accounts, property, contractual rights, accounting records and financial statements make a single-economic-unit argument harder. A stand-alone company that observes those distinctions has still less resemblance to the facades in Jones, Gencor and Wallersteiner.

The hard edge of Salomon deserves criticism: its GBP6,000 asset pool went to the secured claim while GBP7,000 of unsecured creditors' claims went unpaid. Creditors can seek security or the owner's personal guarantee, and fraudulent or wrongful conduct may attract separate remedies, but weaker creditors may have little bargaining power. That unfairness does not supply a general power to recast every compliant offshore asset-holder as its owner's other self.

The better view is that the proposition is legally incorrect as a general rule. It may describe the factual control exercised over some one-man asset-holding companies, with tax, authority and director-duty consequences. Alter ego treatment requires a recognised and fact-specific ground, such as a sham used to evade an existing obligation, and no such ground is given here.
