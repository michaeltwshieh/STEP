## 1. Separate personality and corporate asset ownership - 4.00/4.00

### Marks gained

The opening is direct and legally focused: **“The proposition is too broad.”** The answer then distinguishes descriptive facts - **“stand-alone”, “one-man”, “asset-holding” and “offshore”** - from the legal consequence of incorporation. That is exactly the conceptual question being tested.

The treatment of *Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd* earns full credit. The answer does more than state the rule: it applies the near-identity between the quoted proposition and the historic alter-ego argument. It identifies Mr Salomon's overwhelming beneficial ownership, the nominee family shareholders, the lower courts' alias reasoning, statutory compliance and the House of Lords' rejection of the supposed sham. The sentence **“a one-man company was neither an abuse nor a sham merely because one individual had the whole beneficial interest”** captures the central answer to the proposition.

The explanation of liability is also accurate and useful. The company remains liable for its own obligations; a member limited by shares normally risks the issue price and any unpaid amount. The Salomon figures and secured-creditor outcome illustrate the practical consequence of respecting the company rather than its owner as the legal actor.

The strongest part of the essay is the asset-ownership application:

- **“Legal title and the resulting income belong to the company.”**
- **“The beneficial owner owns or benefits from the shares; that person does not directly own each corporate asset.”**
- *Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd* is applied to distinguish complete economic interest from personal ownership of company property.
- Contracting, bank accounts, proceedings in the company's name, survival of the owner's death and share transfers all demonstrate why routine alter-ego treatment is legally incoherent.

These points are substantively equivalent to, and in several respects fuller than, the KAP's separate-personality side. *Macaura* is a core course authority for the precise asset-ownership issue, not merely decorative additional citation.

### Marks lost

None. This criterion is complete, accurate and applied to the particular one-person asset-holding company in the question.

## 2. Practical alter-ego case - 3.50/4.00

### Marks gained

The answer does engage with the affirmative case. It does not use separate personality as a reason to avoid practical analysis. Its **“money box”** paragraph identifies the typical managed-company reality: the client supplies the assets, expects payments, may control the bank account, and may participate as director, investment adviser or attorney. It correctly recognises that a general power of attorney or unrestricted bank control may give the beneficial owner nearly complete factual control.

The answer also draws consequences from that practical control: management-and-control tax exposure, loss of confidentiality, use of corporate funds without authority and risk to directors. The distinction **“alter ego in everyday speech”** versus legal attribution through organs and agents is a sophisticated and convincing way to preserve both sides of the argument.

### Marks lost

**Development/application loss (0.50):** The affirmative case is slightly compressed. The answer says that the client supplies assets and expects payments, but it does not squarely develop the KAP's strongest practical formulation: the company may be used as a deliberate device to separate an asset, its income and associated liabilities (including tax exposure) from the onshore owner while the owner retains the entire economic benefit. Nor does it expressly complete the value chain by explaining that the owner ultimately participates in corporate profit through dividends or other properly authorised distributions.

This is not a legal error. The relevant ideas are implicit in **“money box”**, corporate income and tax exposure, but an examiner should not have to construct the full affirmative argument from separate passages.

## 3. Nominee/shareholder/director control - 3.50/4.00

### Marks gained

The director-control analysis is excellent. The answer notices that the stem describes a beneficial owner but gives that person no formal office. It then handles alternatives conditionally:

- if the owner is sole member and sole director, ownership and management coincide factually but the company still owns the assets and the director exercises corporate powers;
- if a service provider supplies the board, the articles and applicable legislation allocate management power;
- the 1948 Table A, later Table A/English model-article and BVI/Bahamian statutory examples are kept conditional because no jurisdiction or articles are supplied;
- BVI duties of good faith and care are correctly used to show that the label **“nominee director”** does not turn a director into the beneficial owner's obedient puppet.

The passage **“following the beneficial owner's instructions is no general defence to breach”** squarely answers the KAP's figurehead-director argument while acknowledging that factual control may exist.

### Marks lost

**Coverage/development loss (0.50):** The modern nominee-shareholder side is underdeveloped. The Salomon family nominees are discussed as facts of incorporation, but the essay does not separately explain the typical offshore arrangement in which a service provider is the registered/legal shareholder while the client is beneficial owner under a nominee agreement or declaration. It therefore omits the useful contrast that the nominee may be contractually required to vote as instructed and assign distributions to the beneficial owner, even though neither that arrangement nor the owner's economic control collapses the company's personality or gives the owner title to its underlying assets.

The answer is much stronger on “nominee director” than on nominee shareholder. Because the KAP expressly puts both shareholder and director figureheads into the affirmative argument, some deduction is warranted.

## 4. Veil lifting and jurisdictional angle - 3.25/4.00

### Marks gained

The answer accurately rejects automatic veil lifting and explains the narrow, fact-sensitive exception. **“A sham used to evade an existing obligation”** is a clear statement of the relevant fraud/evasion category. *Jones v Lipman*, *Gencor ACP Ltd v Dalby* and *Wallersteiner v Moir* are correctly used to show misuse beyond one-person ownership, offshore status or passive asset holding. The express application - **“The question supplies none of it”** - is concise and effective.

The additional discussion of *Adams v Cape Industries*, *VTB Capital* and *The Coral Rose* strengthens the answer's treatment of agency and single-economic-unit reasoning. It correctly explains that control and funding do not, by themselves, create agency and that separate boards, accounts, property, contracts and records make disregard of personality less plausible.

Jurisdictional discipline elsewhere in the answer is also strong: no unspecified statute or article is treated as governing, and BVI/Bahamian rules are identified as illustrations only.

### Marks lost

**Substantive omission (0.75):** The answer never directly gives the KAP/course comparison between English common-law reluctance and the more pragmatic US approach, particularly US willingness to pierce where a private company is hopelessly undercapitalised through minimal equity and heavy owner debt. The essay explains the English-style narrow approach very well, but statutory examples from BVI and the Bahamas do not replace the missing comparative veil-lifting point.

The multiple group-company authorities are correct, but they consume space without curing that omission. A concise English/US contrast would have added more marginal value than the extended single-economic-unit discussion.

## 5. Balanced critical discussion and conclusion - 3.75/4.00

### Marks gained

This is genuinely a discussion rather than a one-sided recital. The answer:

- gives the proposition practical force through the money-box and control analysis;
- acknowledges the creditor unfairness produced by *Salomon*;
- explains why one-person ownership, offshore formation and asset holding remain insufficient legal grounds;
- reaches a qualified conclusion rather than an absolute one.

The final distinction is particularly good: the proposition is **“legally incorrect as a general rule”**, but may describe factual control and carry tax, authority and director-duty consequences. That conclusion is reasoned, sensible and responsive to “whether you consider” the proposition correct.

### Marks lost

**Balance/weight loss (0.25):** The answer argues both sides, but it devotes substantially more depth and authority to rebutting the proposition. The affirmative side is principally one money-box paragraph, while the creditor-fairness paragraph addresses the harshness of limited liability more than the particular claim that the company is the beneficial owner's alter ego. A slightly fuller presentation of the nominee/shareholder/economic-benefit reality before rebutting it would make the discussion more evenly critical.

## Highest-value improvements

1. Add one compact comparative paragraph: English/common-law offshore courts pierce exceptionally for sham/facade plus evasion of an existing obligation; US courts may be more ready to pierce a hopelessly undercapitalised owner-funded private company.
2. State the modern nominee-shareholder structure expressly: registered/legal shareholder versus beneficial owner, contractual voting/distribution instructions, and why that practical obedience still does not transfer ownership of the company's assets to the beneficial owner.
3. Strengthen the affirmative case before rebuttal: explain the asset shelter, income stream, potential tax/liability separation and the route by which the beneficial owner ultimately receives economic benefit, including dividends or other authorised distributions.
4. Reallocate a little space from group-company authorities to the exact stand-alone, one-person managed-company facts in the quotation. This would improve balance and examination efficiency without changing the conclusion.

## Bottom line

The answer earns **18/20**. It is legally accurate, highly relevant and supported by strong course authorities. Full marks are withheld because the KAP's English/US comparative point is absent and because the affirmative alter-ego and nominee-shareholder sides are less fully developed than the separate-personality rebuttal.
