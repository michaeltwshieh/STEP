(1.2)

Under article 27.5 of the supplied articles, notice must be given to C, D and E as the registered members who have supplied an address or registered facsimile number for notices. It must also be given to A and B because each is a director who is not a shareholder. The bracketed auditor should receive a copy if the Company has an auditor or the applicable companies legislation requires auditor notice. Article 27.5 says that no other person is entitled to notice under the articles, so the auditor's position should be checked rather than assumed.

The notice must be in writing. The articles permit personal service, post to the member's registered address or email to the registered email address. Email is the most practical method here, provided the address is the registered address, and a properly addressed and prepaid posted copy is a sensible evidential follow-up. Email service is deemed effective on sending. A posted notice is deemed served on the fourth day after posting. For a meeting on 28 August, an email or personal notice should therefore be sent no later than 13 August, giving 14 clear days from 14 to 27 August. A posted notice should be posted no later than 9 August so that deemed service falls on 13 August. The year and time must be inserted before issue, since the notice must specify the place, day and hour.

Article 11.2 allows a shorter notice period only if every member entitled to receive notice consents. Any such consent should be obtained in writing and the minutes should record it. It cannot be inferred from the fact that the meeting is convenient for the registered members.

(1.3)

The meeting must be properly convened before its business can have effect. A failure to give proper notice to a person entitled to it will generally invalidate the proceedings, subject to the supplied articles and the applicable legislation. Article 11.3 saves an accidental omission to give notice to, or accidental non-receipt by, a member. It does not turn a deliberate omission, an incorrect notice period or an omission of the stated business into a valid notice, and its wording does not expressly extend the saving to a director who is not a member.

A member who attends in person or by proxy will ordinarily be treated as having received valid notice. The safer course where a defect is found before the vote is to obtain the unanimous member consent permitted by article 11.2 if the required notice period can no longer be met, or to reconvene after proper notice. The resolution should not be treated as safely passed merely because C, who holds 70 per cent, is present.

(1.4)

C cannot pass the resolution at the appointed time on 28 August. Article 12.2 requires two shareholders personally present before business can be transacted, and C is only one person. The meeting is therefore inquorate and no resolution can be passed at that sitting.

The question indicates that the meeting was called through the Company secretary on the directors' instructions, rather than on a member requisition. In that case, article 12.3 says that, if a quorum is still absent after half an hour, the meeting stands adjourned to the same day in the next week at the same time and place. If only C is present at the adjourned meeting after a further half hour, the members present form the quorum. If the meeting had instead been convened on a member requisition, the original meeting would be dissolved rather than adjourned.

At the adjourned meeting C could pass the special resolution, assuming C is entitled to vote and all sums presently payable on the shares have been paid. C may demand a poll because one member holding more than 15 per cent of the paid-up capital can do so under article 12.6. On a poll article 13.1 gives one vote for each share, so C's 70 per cent vote exceeds the two-thirds super-majority defined as a special resolution in article 1. Article 12.11 does not provide a shortcut on 28 August because a written resolution must be signed by all members entitled to notice and vote, including D and E.

(1.5)

On a show of hands each member present has one vote under article 13.1. C has one vote in favour and D and E have two votes against, so the chairman should declare the resolution lost on the show of hands. The percentages of the shareholdings do not alter that result while the vote remains a show of hands.

C should demand a poll before, or at the time of, the declaration of the result. Article 12.6 permits a poll demanded by one member holding at least 15 per cent of the paid-up capital, and C holds 70 per cent. The poll then counts one vote for each share. C would have 70 per cent of the votes in favour and D and E together would have 30 per cent against. The 70 per cent vote exceeds the two-thirds threshold for a special resolution under article 1, so the resolution would pass on the poll, subject to the statutory qualification in the question that permits the lesser super-majority in the articles. The poll and its result should be recorded in the minutes.

Question 3

The chosen jurisdiction is the British Virgin Islands. Company Ltd is treated as a BVI business company with the supplied articles in force. International Trust Company is treated as the trustee and holder of the Company's shares under the Redhouse Trust, while Company remains a separate legal person that owns the worldwide assets and the Midcity Bank account.

(3.1)

The Midcity Bank account belongs to Company, and the fact that International Trust Company holds its shares does not make the trustee the owner of that account. The usual corporate arrangement separates the member's ownership of shares from the board's power to run the business. Members provide or acquire the capital, attend general meetings, alter the constitution where the articles and legislation allow, appoint and remove directors and vote on major matters such as a voluntary winding-up. The board acts as a unit and takes the ordinary management decisions, enters contracts, employs agents and deals with company property. Directors owe their fiduciary and care duties to Company as a whole.

That division grew out of the limits of older business forms. A sole trader owned the business, made all decisions and bore its debts without limit. A partnership allowed risk and capital to be shared, but a partner's interest was hard to transfer and the partnership could not invite the public to invest in the same way as a joint-stock company. During nineteenth-century industrialisation, enterprises such as railways needed capital beyond one entrepreneur's wealth. The Joint Stock Companies Act 1844 allowed incorporation by registration, separate personality and public share finance. The Limited Liability Act 1855 then protected shareholders from open-ended liability, subject to safeguards such as capital maintenance.

The result was a bargain between capitalist shareholders and entrepreneurial directors. Investors could put money into the company, sell shares and leave the daily work to people chosen for their expertise. The board gave continuity when a member sold shares, and limited liability made investment more attractive, while the shareholders retained supervision through the constitution, votes and the power to change the board. The Companies Act 1907 later recognised that a small private company might have few owners who also manage it, so the practical boundary could become blurred. Offshore asset-holding companies often use that private-company form, but the legal division still depends on the particular articles.

(3.2)

Article 19.1 puts the management of Company's business in the directors and gives them all the powers of Company except powers that the Companies Legislation or the articles require the members to exercise in general meeting. It is the form associated with article 80 of Table A under the Companies Act 1948. On those terms, you and Ms Avril Nath, acting as the board, hold the management power. International Trust Company can use the rights of the member, including appointing or removing directors and altering the articles through the required resolution, but it cannot simply pass an ordinary resolution directing the board how to operate the Midcity Bank account or deal with a particular asset.

Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon [1909] 1 Ch 311 is the direct illustration. The shareholders tried to instruct directors to complete a property transaction after an article had placed the management power in the board. The court held that the shareholders could not usurp a power expressly conferred on the directors; they had to alter the articles if they wanted a different allocation. The same conclusion applies to Article 19.1. A member resolution is still required where the Companies Legislation or another article reserves a matter to the members. The position would differ under the Companies Act 1985 Table A form or the English 2008 model article, under which a special resolution may give binding directions, but those are not the words supplied for Company.

(3.3)

The trustee's control operates through the shareholding. International Trust Company can vote as the holder of the shares, alter the articles where the required threshold is met and appoint or remove directors if the constitution gives that power to the members. That influence is useful in an underlying company because ownership of the shares continues in the trustee despite changes in a beneficiary's circumstances. It also keeps the legal title to the shares with the trustee rather than requiring a transfer on the death of a beneficial owner. The arrangement does not transfer Company's assets or its Midcity Bank balance to International Trust Company. Those remain Company's property, and the board must control them under Article 19.1.

The trust structure therefore changes who can supervise the board, but it does not erase the board's legal duties. You and Ms Nath are directors of Company, and the appointment does not make either of you the trustee's agent. There is no separate legal category of a nominee director. Each director must act honestly and in good faith for Company, use powers for a proper corporate purpose and exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence. A trustee's recommendation about an investment or a bank payment can be considered, but a director should ask for the documents, understand the foreseeable consequences and refuse an act that would be unlawful or contrary to Company's interests. A general power of attorney that leaves the trustee or beneficial owner with unrestricted control would create a management-and-control risk and could expose the directors to liability for allowing Company's assets to be dealt with without proper oversight.

There is also a tax and confidentiality consequence. The place where high-level management and control is exercised can affect the residence of a company for tax purposes. If the trustee or the persons behind the trust make all real decisions outside the BVI while the board signs without independent consideration, the structure may be challenged on that basis. The service provider must also keep the registers, minutes, accounting records and beneficial-owner information required for the Company and comply with applicable anti-money-laundering duties. Those records should show the board's own consideration of instructions concerning the Midcity Bank account.

The Redhouse Trust instrument and the law governing it have not been supplied. The precise powers reserved to a settlor or beneficiary, the trustee's duties under the trust and the identity of any beneficial owner therefore remain conditional. The corporate answer is clear on the supplied facts: the trustee exercises member rights, while the directors retain management under Article 19.1 unless the articles are validly changed.

(3.4)

The members may remove a director before the end of the director's term by ordinary resolution under article 15.3, notwithstanding anything in a service agreement or other agreement with that director. No cause is required by that article. A general meeting must be properly convened, or the member may use a valid written resolution if the applicable articles permit it. Since International Trust Company is the holder of the Company's shares on the facts, its authorised trustee signatory can exercise the member's vote, subject to the Redhouse Trust and the Company's constitution.

Article 15.4 permits the members, by a further ordinary resolution, to appoint another person in place of the director removed. The incoming director should first consent to act, and the Company should update its register of directors and notify the Registrar where the BVI filing rules require it. The replacement takes the same retirement position as the director removed. Here Ms Nath remains in office, so the appointment resolution should identify the replacement and the date on which the new director takes office.

Removal is distinct from an automatic vacation of office. Under article 17.1 the office is vacated if, among other stated events, the director becomes bankrupt, becomes of unsound mind, resigns in writing, or is directly or indirectly interested in a Company contract or its profits. Article 17.2 preserves the office where the interest is only through membership of a corporation and the director declares the interest at the first board meeting after becoming interested, but the director cannot vote on that contract. If you are removed while Ms Nath remains, article 20.8 permits the continuing director to act only to restore the board to its quorum or call a general meeting. Company should therefore arrange the replacement promptly so that ordinary business concerning its worldwide assets and Midcity Bank account can resume.

Question 4

A directorship can produce fees and a continuing appointment, but the office also puts the director in the path of claims by Company, creditors and, in some cases, regulators. The risk is not reduced because the company is an offshore asset-holding company or because a service provider supplied the director. For a BVI business company, sections 120 and 122 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 state the two broad standards: honest good-faith action in the Company's best interests, and the care, skill and diligence of a reasonable director in the circumstances.

Authority is the first problem. Articles may give directors power to borrow, mortgage property, issue documents, appoint agents and bind Company, but the power may carry a limit. Article 19.3 of the supplied articles, for example, requires the previous ordinary sanction of the members before borrowing above $1 million. A director who signs a $5 million facility without that sanction may have breached the internal rules even though a good-faith bank can often enforce the transaction against Company. Section 31 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 prevents Company from asserting against a person dealing in good faith that an article was not complied with or that a director lacked customary authority. Royal British Bank v Turquand [1856] 6 E&B 327 supplies the common-law version of that protection. It does not protect the director from Company's internal claim for the loss caused by exceeding authority.

The fiduciary duty creates a wider risk than a simple signature defect. A director must honestly believe that the exercise of power benefits Company and must use the power for a proper purpose. Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver [1942] 1 All ER 378 shows the consequence where directors take for themselves a profit that should have gone to the company. Lee Panavision Ltd v Lee Lighting Ltd [1992] BCLC 22 shows the same point where a director uses a company management contract to favour an associated company. Corporate money is Company's money. A director should read the articles, understand the transaction, record the commercial reason for it and keep the benefit with Company unless an informed approval permits the benefit.

Conflicts create a separate exposure even where Company loses no money. The director must disclose an interest in a proposed contract and observe the voting and quorum rules. A director who sells property to Company, guarantees a personal debt with Company's assets, accepts a benefit from a lender or takes a corporate opportunity can be required to account for profits and the transaction may be set aside. Aberdeen Railway v Blaikie Bros (1854) 1 Macq 461 is the classic self-dealing authority. Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi [2005] 2 BCLC 91 shows the duty to disclose a competing opportunity. Article 18.2 of the supplied articles allows a director to contract with Company after disclosure at the board meeting, but the director cannot vote on the contract. For a BVI company, section 124 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 is the statutory disclosure route. The practical protection is full written disclosure, a disinterested decision where the rules allow it and member approval where that is required.

The no-profits and no-benefits rules catch less obvious conduct. Remuneration, a bonus, a gift from a counterparty, use of confidential information and a competing business all need proper authority. A director cannot cure a dishonest diversion of Company's assets by voting as a majority member to ratify it. Prior approval or later ratification by ordinary resolution, after full disclosure, can release a director for many conflicts, unauthorised benefits and negligent acts. The rule does not validate a fraudulent expropriation that harms Company, minority members or creditors. A director should ask for approval before taking the benefit, not after the dispute has begun.

Care and supervision are the next main problem areas. The old subjective approach in Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co. [1925] Ch 407 has been replaced in the modern cases by a standard directed at a reasonably competent director. Dorchester Finance Co. Ltd v Stebbing [1989] BCLC 498 treated failure to engage with the company's affairs as negligence. Re Barings plc (No.5) [2000] 1 BCLC 523 held that directors may delegate work but must retain oversight. A director should not sign blank cheques, approve a complex insurance form without reading it or accept an unexplained instruction from a person outside the company. Bishopsgate Investment Management (In Liquidation) v Maxwell [1994] 1 All ER 261 illustrates the danger of signing documents put forward by the person who controls the business without inquiry.

Section 123 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 permits reliance on an employee reasonably believed to be reliable and competent, a professional adviser within the adviser's competence or another director with the relevant authority. The director must act in good faith, make proper inquiry when circumstances indicate the need and have no knowledge that the reliance is unwarranted. In practice that means obtaining the facility, accounts, valuations and legal advice, asking the questions that the papers raise and recording the answer. Lexi Holdings plc (In Administration) v Luqman [2009] EWCA Civ 117 shows why family connection and suspicious records can defeat reliance. An informed business decision that turns out badly is not, by itself, negligence. An uninformed signature is a different matter.

Service-provider and corporate-director appointments carry their own risks. There is no legal defence called a nominee-director defence. A person or corporate entity that accepts the office owes Company's duties, and the directors of a corporate director can be linked to the managed company's affairs through the corporate chain. A beneficial owner, trustee or Relationship Manager may recommend a transaction, but the board must decide it independently. A broad power of attorney, uncontrolled bank signing power or a practice of signing whatever the client sends can transfer management and control to the client, create tax-residence problems and expose the provider to losses or anti-money-laundering consequences. The safer practice is a narrow written mandate, a clear division of functions, proper records and refusal of an unlawful act.

Insolvency produces personal creditor risk. A liquidator may investigate the period before the winding-up. Fraudulent trading can make a person who knowingly carries on the business with intent to defraud creditors contribute to Company's assets. In a BVI company, section 55 of the BVI Insolvency Act 2003 supplies the fraudulent-trading route and section 256 supplies wrongful-trading liability where the director knew or ought to have known that Company could not avoid insolvent liquidation. Section 332 of the Companies Act 1948 and section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 are the models described in the materials. A director should monitor cash flow and liabilities, stop incurring debts when the statutory test is met, preserve the books and take advice about a creditor arrangement, receivership or liquidation. Neither a DSA indemnity nor a client instruction makes continued dishonest or wrongful trading safe.

Several protections can reduce exposure if they are used with care. Shareholder approval or ratification requires full disclosure and the correct resolution; it is not a general release for fraud. The articles and a director's service agreement may contain exoneration and indemnity provisions. Article 29.1 of the supplied articles excludes liability for an error or oversight only in the absence of bad faith, fraud or dishonesty, and article 29.2 limits the indemnity to liabilities that may lawfully be indemnified and excludes wilful default, bad faith and fraud. Section 132 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 makes such provisions lawful in the BVI within those limits. The position differs in England under section 232 of the Companies Act 2006 and in jurisdictions such as Jersey, Guernsey and the ordinary Isle of Man company, where broad exoneration can be void.

A company management agreement can give the service provider a client indemnity for third-party claims, but it does not prevent the claim being brought and does not turn the director into the client's agent. The clause must be drawn to the client's attention and kept within the limits described in Rae v Meek (1889) 14 App Cas 558. Directors' and officers' insurance is another useful layer. The policy and the DSA in Appendix 21C can cover negligent or inadvertent liability, but a deliberate default, fraud or dishonesty remains outside the cover. The director should check that the policy, DSA and management agreement match the governing jurisdiction and the actual role.

The sensible approach is therefore active supervision backed by clean corporate records. The director should check authority, disclose conflicts, understand the papers, use experts where appropriate, retain independent judgment and watch the solvency position. A BVI article 29 clause and sections 120, 122, 123, 124 and 132 provide useful protection, but they leave the director exposed to the conduct that creates the greatest harm. The office can be worthwhile, but the protection follows disciplined decisions rather than the label attached to the appointment.

(5.1)

A members' voluntary winding-up is the solvent route. The directors first make a genuine and thorough inquiry into Company's affairs and sign a declaration that all debts can be paid in full, usually within 12 months from commencement. The members then pass the required special resolution to wind up Company and appoint a liquidator. The liquidator takes over the winding-up, notices are sent to the Registrar and published where required, the assets are collected and creditors are paid, and a final account is presented to the members and filed before dissolution. In BVI terminology, section 199 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 permits the members or directors to pass the voluntary-winding-up resolution where the memorandum or articles authorise it, but that flexibility does not make an insolvent company solvent. Section 211 applies the BVI Insolvency Act 2003 to an insolvent BVI business company.

A creditors' voluntary winding-up is used where Company cannot pay every debt in full. The directors call the members to pass the winding-up resolution but do not sign a declaration of solvency. They also call the creditors and present a statement of Company's assets and liabilities. The creditors have the greater say because they choose the liquidator by the prescribed vote. The liquidator then realises the assets, deals with the secured creditor, pays the liquidation costs and preferential debts, distributes the balance pari passu among ordinary unsecured creditors and holds final meetings or obtains the required approvals before filing the account.

Members' voluntary winding-up is unavailable on these facts. Blackacre is subject to a fixed legal charge of $2,400,000 but is worth only $1,700,000, so the Mortgagee has a $700,000 unsecured shortfall. The free assets total only $905,000 before the liquidator's $40,000 fees, while the ordinary unsecured claims include the Mortgagee shortfall, Lawyer, Accountancy Firm, Corporate Service Provider, miscellaneous trade creditors and Beneficial Owner's $500,000 loan. Company should therefore commence a creditors' voluntary winding-up, with the creditor process controlling the choice of liquidator and the distribution.
