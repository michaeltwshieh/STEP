#### 1.2

Article 27.5 requires notice to be given to every member who has supplied an address for notices, every person entitled to a share following a member’s death or bankruptcy, and every director who is not also a shareholder. On the stated facts, the notice must therefore be served on C, D and E as the registered shareholders and on A and B as directors. No death, bankruptcy or transmission is mentioned.

Article 27.1 permits personal service, post to the registered address or email to the member’s registered email address. I would use email, provided each recipient has supplied a registered email address, and retain evidence of transmission. It takes effect on sending under Article 27.2. Personal delivery with an acknowledgement of receipt is an equally sound alternative.

Article 11.1 requires at least 14 clear days’ notice, excluding both the date of service and 28 August. Email or personal service should therefore take place no later than 13 August. If post is used, Article 27.2 deems a meeting notice served on the fourth day after posting, so it should be posted no later than 9 August. The statutory notice requirement for the special resolution must also be checked because Article 11.1 is expressly subject to it. If there is insufficient time, Article 11.2 permits shorter notice only with the consent of all members entitled to receive notice.

#### 1.3

A deliberate or otherwise non-accidental failure to give proper notice to a person entitled to it normally invalidates the proceedings. Article 11.3 saves the meeting where there has merely been an accidental omission to give notice or accidental non-receipt. A shareholder who attends in person or by proxy will generally be treated as having received notice, but that should not be used as a substitute for compliance.

#### 1.4

C cannot transact the business at the meeting when it first convenes. Article 12.2 fixes the quorum at two shareholders personally present, except for a sole-member company. Company has three shareholders, so C alone is not a quorum even though C owns 70%.

Under Article 12.3, the meeting must wait for half an hour. If it was called by the directors, it then stands adjourned to the same day in the following week at the same time and place. If a quorum is still absent after half an hour at the adjourned meeting, the member present will constitute a quorum. If, contrary to the likely facts, the meeting was convened on a members’ requisition, the original meeting would be dissolved rather than adjourned.

#### 1.5

On a show of hands, Article 13.1 gives each member present one vote regardless of shareholding. C casts one vote for the resolution while D and E cast two votes against it. The chairman must therefore declare the special resolution lost.

C should immediately demand a poll, before or upon the declaration of the show-of-hands result. Article 12.6 permits one member holding at least 15% of the paid-up capital to do so; C holds 70%. On the poll, Article 13.1 gives one vote for each share. C’s 70% will defeat D and E’s combined 30% and will exceed the two-thirds threshold for a special resolution in Article 1. The question expressly permits the lower super-majority in the articles to apply instead of the legislative three-quarter default. The resolution will therefore pass on the poll.

## Question 3

### Candidate answer

For this answer I choose **the Bahamas** and treat Company as a Bahamian international business company. Section 40 of the International Business Companies Act 2000 and Company’s actual articles govern the division of management power.

#### 3.1

Company has two organs with distinct functions. International Trust Company is the registered shareholder and acts through member resolutions. The board consists of the trust officer and Ms Avril Nath, and acts collectively to manage Company's business.

The division grew from the nineteenth-century corporate model. Investors could contribute capital and obtain shares without having to conduct the enterprise themselves. Directors were selected for experience and continuity of management. A transfer of shares could then alter ownership without interrupting the company's business. Limited liability also left the shareholder's exposure normally confined to the investment, while the directors accepted management responsibility and the duties attached to it.

Members retain supervision of the board. They appoint and remove directors, alter the constitution and decide matters which legislation or the articles reserve to them, including major capital changes and voluntary winding-up. Day-to-day dealings, use of corporate assets and contracts belong to the directors. That allocation allowed numerous investors to fund an enterprise while a smaller body took informed decisions.

The model needs careful application here. International Trust Company owns the shares in its capacity as trustee of the Redhouse Trust. Company itself owns the worldwide assets and the Midcity Bank account. The trust company's ownership of the shares does not make the trust company the legal owner of each corporate asset.

#### 3.2

Article 19.1 confirms the traditional division and gives the directors the residual management power. The trust officer and Ms Avril Nath may exercise every Company power which the legislation or articles do not reserve to the members in general meeting. Section 109 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 points in the same direction.

Article 19.1 follows the older Table A form. It contains no wording allowing members to give binding directions by special resolution. Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon [1909] confirms that members cannot take a power assigned by the articles to the board merely by passing a resolution. International Trust Company could alter the articles through the required member procedure, remove directors or exercise a power expressly reserved to members. Until then, it cannot usurp the board's management decision.

The power belongs to the board as a unit. With two directors and an unfixed quorum, Article 20.4 requires both directors for a meeting. Alternatively, both may sign a unanimous written resolution under Article 20.15. Neither co-director has a free-standing power to operate the Midcity Bank account or dispose of Company assets unless the board has delegated that power.

#### 3.3

The Redhouse Trust arrangement narrows the practical distance between ownership and control but does not erase the legal division. International Trust Company is both trustee and registered member. It can exercise all member rights, including the appointment and removal of directors, but the board still controls Company's worldwide assets under Article 19.1. The Midcity Bank account belongs to Company.

As employees of the trustee, the trust officer and Ms Avril Nath will know the purposes for which Company is held and may receive recommendations from colleagues administering the Redhouse Trust. Each director still owes the directorial duties to Company. Sections 120 and 122 of the BVI Business Companies Act require honest, good-faith action in what the director believes to be Company's best interests and the care, diligence and skill of a reasonable director in those circumstances. Describing either director as a nominee would change nothing. Following an instruction from the trustee or beneficiaries is no defence if the resulting act breaches those duties.

International Trust Company has lawful indirect control. It can vote the shares, reshape the board and alter the articles. Routine instructions about Company assets are different. The directors should review the proposed act, the Redhouse Trust purpose, Company's financial position and any conflict, then reach their own decision. If the trustee wants a reserved power to direct transactions, the articles would have to be amended to a form that grants it.

Management and control also has a tax consequence. If effective decisions are taken outside the BVI by persons who treat the directors as puppets, Company's tax residence may be challenged elsewhere. Bank mandates, minutes and custody of records should therefore match the decision actually taken in the BVI. The older wording of Article 19.1 is demanding, but it keeps responsibility in the same hands as the directorial duties.

#### 3.4

International Trust Company may remove the trust officer by ordinary resolution under Article 15.3 before the end of the term of office. No misconduct or other cause has to be proved, and the article applies despite any agreement between Company and the director. As the sole registered member, International Trust Company can use a written resolution signed by its authorised signatory under Article 12.11. A general meeting is also possible after the required notice and corporate-representative formalities.

Removal should be distinguished from other ways office can end. Article 17.1 provides for vacation on bankruptcy, unsoundness of mind, written resignation, an unauthorised office of profit or certain undisclosed interests. Article 16 also provides retirement by rotation. None of those facts is stated here, so ordinary member removal is the practical route.

Article 15.4 permits Company, again by ordinary resolution, to appoint another person in place of the removed director. The replacement is subject to retirement at the same time as if appointed on the date when the removed director was last elected. The actual examination articles do not give the remaining director a general power to fill this removal vacancy. The new director's consent should be obtained, followed by an update of Company's register of directors and the required notice to the BVI Registrar.

The result is that International Trust Company can change the personnel of the board but, while the trust officer remains in office, Article 19.1 and the BVI duties require independent management with Ms Avril Nath.

# Question 4

## SUBMIT THIS

The reward attached to a directorship comes from control, remuneration and commercial influence. The other edge is personal exposure. Wide management powers carry duties owed to the company, and an offshore director supplied for a fee is subject to those duties in the same way as any executive director.

The first risk is acting outside the power conferred by the company's constitution or using a power for a collateral purpose. A director who signs a transaction without board authority may have internal liability even where the transaction remains binding on the company. Royal British Bank v Turquand [1856] protects an honest outsider who had no notice of the internal defect. Section 31 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 gives similar statutory protection. The company may therefore have to perform the contract while the director compensates it for the loss.

A director must act honestly, in good faith and in the interests of the company. Section 120 of the BVI Act states that duty directly. The power must be used for a proper corporate purpose. In Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver [1942], directors took for themselves an opportunity which should have belonged to the company and had to account for the profit. Lee Panavision Ltd v Lee Lighting Ltd [1992] shows the same problem where a director caused one company to contract on terms favouring another company he controlled.

Loyalty produces several recurring hazards. A director who contracts with the company, procures a company guarantee for a personal debt or has an interest in the counterparty is in a conflict position. Full disclosure and informed approval must be obtained before the transaction. The statutory disclosure provisions include section 124 of the BVI Act, section 57(2) of the Bahamian International Business Companies Act 2000 and sections 162-167 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008. Depending on the governing rule, approval comes from a disinterested board quorum or the members.

A director must account for an unauthorised profit made from the office. That includes unauthorised remuneration, use of confidential information, diversion of a corporate opportunity and competition with the company. A gift from a third party can breach the no-benefits rule even where it did not alter the decision. A corrupt payment is more serious and may also give rise to criminal liability. Early disclosure is far safer than trying to repair the transaction after money has changed hands.

The duty of care supplies a different risk. Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co [1925] used a lenient subjective standard, but Dorchester Finance Co Ltd v Stebbing [1989] applied the care expected from an ordinarily competent person and Norman v Theodore Goddard [1991] focused on a person carrying out a director's functions. Section 122 of the BVI Act asks what a reasonable director would have done in the same circumstances, taking account of the company, the decision and the responsibilities undertaken.

That standard bites on ordinary administration. Directors in Dorchester Finance signed blank cheques and were liable when the third director misused them. Re D'Jan of London Ltd [1994] concerned failure to check an insurance proposal, which allowed the insurer to reject a later claim. Re Barings plc (No.5) [2000] required directors to inform themselves about company affairs and maintain a proper system of supervision. Delegation can divide the work, but it does not permit the board to abandon oversight.

A director may rely on competent staff, another director within assigned authority or professional advice where the reliance is reasonable. Section 123 of the BVI Act requires good faith, proper inquiry when circumstances call for it and no knowledge that reliance is unwarranted. Lexi Holdings plc v Luqman [2009] shows why warning signs matter: the company's records and the managing director's history made passive reliance unreasonable; Madoff Securities International Ltd v Raven [2013] drew a line between directors who could reasonably rely on expertise and an inner circle who could not. The board must supervise, ask questions where warning signs appear and keep a record of the information on which it acted.

A calculated commercial loss does not by itself prove negligence. The director should record the information reviewed, alternatives considered, advice obtained and reasons for deciding that the risk benefits the company. Minutes prepared before the outcome turns sour are far more persuasive than a reconstruction produced after liquidation.

The misleading expression "nominee director" creates a particular offshore risk. A service-provider director is not the beneficial owner's agent or puppet. Instructions may be considered, but the director must decide independently. "The client told me to do it" is no defence where corporate assets are lost, the company becomes tax-resident elsewhere or a transaction is dishonest.

Insolvency sharpens the exposure. Fraudulent trading can make a person who knowingly carries on business with intent to defraud creditors contribute personally to the assets. The BVI regime applies section 55 of the Insolvency Act 2003 for fraudulent trading and section 256 for wrongful trading. Wrongful trading reaches a director who knew or ought to have known that insolvent liquidation could not be avoided. Similar wrongful-trading provisions appear in section 434 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008 and article 177 of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, while Bermuda, the Cayman Islands and Hong Kong retain fraudulent trading without the same wrongful-trading remedy described in the course material.

Once insolvency is probable, the board should obtain current accounts and cash-flow forecasts, stop incurring debts which Company has no reasonable prospect of paying and seek insolvency advice. If rescue is unrealistic, prompt winding-up reduces both the deficit and the period which a liquidator may investigate. An indemnity does not protect a director from fraudulent or wrongful trading.

The company's remedies are substantial. It may set aside a director-interested transaction, recover a secret profit, require an indemnity for loss or claim compensation for negligent management. Foss v Harbottle (1843) ordinarily makes the company the proper claimant, although a minority member may bring a derivative claim where wrongdoers control the company and fraud is alleged. Cook v Deeks [1916] also prevents directors who control the voting shares from ratifying their own dishonest appropriation of corporate property to the prejudice of minority members or creditors.

Risk reduction begins with sound conduct: stay within the constitution, obtain proper authority, disclose interests, abstain where required, read documents before signing, supervise delegates, keep accurate records and obtain advice in unfamiliar transactions. Prior member approval or later ratification can release many breaches after full disclosure. It may approve a self-dealing transaction, benefit or negligent management decision. It cannot cleanse a dishonest expropriation which prejudices minority members or creditors.

Protection can also be contractual. In the BVI, section 132 permits lawful exoneration and indemnity provisions, usually excluding fraud, dishonesty, bad faith or wilful default. The Bahamas permits comparable protection under section 58 of its International Business Companies Act 2000 and Bermuda under section 98 of its Companies Act 1981. By contrast, section 468 of the Hong Kong Companies Ordinance, section 157 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008, article 77 of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991 and section 172 of the Singapore Companies Act restrict such provisions. The director must read the governing jurisdiction rather than assume that an offshore clause works everywhere.

A director's service agreement may repeat exoneration and indemnity terms. A company management agreement can require the beneficial owner to indemnify the service provider, but that promise may not cover the individual director and does not stop a third party suing. Rae v Meek (1889) also supports close construction of an exclusion clause and requires the clause to have been brought properly to the client's attention.

Directors' and officers' insurance is the practical final layer. Section 133 of the BVI Act expressly permits it, as do section 59 of the Bahamian International Business Companies Act and section 158 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law. Cover usually meets negligence and inadvertent third-party liability, subject to the policy terms. Deliberate default, fraud and dishonesty remain outside the cover.

The risk side of the directorship is manageable where the director takes the office seriously, keeps control of the decision process and records the basis of each act. Approval, carefully valid indemnities and insurance reduce the financial exposure. None replaces honest, informed management or protects a director who continues an insolvent company at its creditors' expense.

### 5.1

A members' voluntary winding-up is the solvent route. The directors first make a declaration after a proper investigation that Company can pay all its debts in full. A supporting statement of assets and liabilities is normally attached. The members then authorise the winding-up and appoint a liquidator under the applicable BVI procedure. The liquidator collects and realises the assets, pays every creditor in full and returns any surplus to members. Notices and Registrar steps follow, and a final account is approved before dissolution. A knowingly or negligently false declaration exposes the directors to penalties.

A creditors' voluntary winding-up is used where the company cannot pay all its debts. The directors cannot give the declaration of solvency. They procure the corporate decision to wind up and convene a creditors' meeting, presenting the assets, liabilities and creditor list. The creditors have the stronger voice and choose the liquidator by the required majority. The liquidator realises the assets, applies the statutory order of priority and presents the final account to the creditors and members.
