## Module 2:

## Answers to self-assessment questions

1. A company is a group of persons who organise themselves together as a separate legal entity designed to pursue a common objective or purpose, which usually involves the carrying on of a trade or business for profit.
2. The above definition does not wholly apply to companies incorporated offshore, which often:
- are owned by a single person, rather than a group
- do not trade, but merely hold and invest corporate assets
- exist for purposes other than pure profit, e.g. asset protection, confidentiality, tax efficiency, estate planning.
3. The earliest form of operating structure used by businesspersons in medieval England was the sole proprietor. The main disadvantages included:
7. (a) undue risk insofar as the business owner is solely responsible, in full, for the debts of, and liabilities incurred by, the business; and
8. (b) although sole proprietors can borrow funds from third parties, they are unable to raise capital for the business from outside investors.

These drawbacks were overcome initially by traders combining together to conduct business as a partnership (known as societas ), which spread risk and joined the capital of the partners together for their mutual benefit.

4. Partnerships ( societas ) suffered from three major drawbacks:
- there was often a contractual restriction upon a partner's right to transfer their part of the business insofar as the remaining partners would have to unanimously agree to a proposed transfer
- it did not allow the business to raise capital by inviting the investing public to invest in it (only the individual partners)
- it did not have a legal personality and therefore could not own property or enter into contracts in its own name (except in the name of all the partners).
5. In the 16th century there was an expansion of the merchant shipping fleet in England. The merchants initially operated as sole traders but later formed 'companies' of merchants to trade particular overseas commodities. The Crown granted Royal Charters to such 'companies' of merchants enabling members to trade in a particular territory. Investors would contribute capital to these companies in consideration for the company to issue them with joint stock (shares in the enterprise), thereby becoming members of the company and sharing in its profits.

6. International trade increased throughout the 16th and into the 17th century and, in 1711, a group of merchants obtained a Royal Charter to trade in the South Pacific under the name of the South Seas Company. As an add-on to its usual business, this company subsequently acquired the UK Government's national debt and persuaded government bondholders to convert their bonds into shares of stock. But then in 1720 the stock crashed. An investigation revealed fraud and Parliament questioned the legality of corporate structures.
7. It was thought that the company structure could be used by dishonest directors as a vehicle to defraud investors. The Bubble Act 1720 therefore prohibited the use of companies as a legal structure and made their activities illegal. What remained were unincorporated partnerships, companies incorporated by Royal Charter and those companies incorporated by private Acts of Parliament.
8. The onset of industrialisation in the century after the Bubble was passed and, in particular, the growth in railway companies meant that measures were needed to allow corporate structures to exist legally. Businesspersons lobbied parliament to relax the prohibition upon the use of companies as an operating structure, but to regulate their operations to prevent the corporate structure being used as a vehicle for fraud. In this way growing businesses could more easily raise capital from investors to finance expansion, leading to a growing economy. Finally, in 1844 the Joint Stock Companies Act was passed which put the modern company structure on a firm legal footing. The Limited Liability Act 1855 subsequently introduced the concept of limited liability for shareholders
9. In a limited company, the liability of the members is limited to the amount they are required to pay for their shares or the amount that they guarantee to contribute on a winding up. The liability of the company to pay its own debts is unlimited. Arguments for the introduction of limited liability include the following: it makes investment in a company less risky and therefore more attractive to the public; capital therefore becomes more available and cheaper for the general good of business, commerce and the country as a whole. Arguments against it are that it encourages a company to be reckless and trade beyond its means, because shareholders know that, if the company fails, they do not have to pick up the pieces and pay the company's creditors themselves; lawful creditors doing business with a company in good faith may be left without redress if the company fails.
10. These types of industry were highly specialised and required a considerable amount of capital investment. Accordingly, the incorporated business entity carried significant benefits:
- it allowed such companies to invite public participation, by selling shares to the public
- it allowed investors to inject capital into an enterprise without the risk of them incurring any further liability in the future
- it enabled the public to transfer their shares to a willing buyer without formality (for instance, on the stock exchange)
- it enabled the business of the company to be managed by entrepreneurs and businesspersons with specialist expertise.

11. Drawbacks of operating a small- or medium-sized company that had been incorporated under the 1944 Act include publicity requirements and loss of confidentiality and the general expense of compliance with detailed regulations designed to curtail the possibility of a rogue board of directors defrauding investors, such as: accounting requirements, publication of financial statements, annual general meetings, etc. The Companies Act 1907 allowed smaller 'private' companies to opt out of some of these more burdensome compliance requirements when there was considered little risk of the directors stealing corporate assets.
12. English company law has been used as a model by the common law countries and offshore financial centres.  In particular, the Companies Act 1948 tends to be used as an initial template and then amended or updated over time as company law has evolved.
13. i. A company becomes an artificial person, and can therefore, in its corporate name,     own property, enter into contracts, sue and be sued in a court of law.
ii. The liquidator of A Salomon &amp; Co Ltd (on behalf of the company's creditors) commenced a lawsuit against Mr Salomon in his personal capacity, claiming that the company (and hence its mortgage of corporate asset in favour of Mr Salomon) was a sham, void and of no legal effect. The court held that:
  - the company was validly incorporated
  - its corporate structure, whereby all the shares were held by the Solomon family members, complied with the requirements of the companies legislation
  - the company was not, therefore, a sham, and the security conferred by the mortgage of corporate assets in favour of Mr Salomon was valid.
- iii. If a companies legislation requirement is for a minimum of, say, two (or seven in the Salomon case) shareholders, these may be made up of a majority controlling shareholder and their nominee(s).
14. A company is considered to be an artificial person which has its own legal existence and legal personality that is distinct from its members. A third party can't go behind the 'veil of incorporation' and attack the members personally for acts done or debts incurred by the company (except in exceptional circumstances).
15. According to Adams v Cape Industries Plc [1990] Ch 433 an English court (and, therefore a court of a common law jurisdiction that follows English legal principles) is prepared to make an order that lifts the veil of incorporation, and thereby allow a creditor of a company to impose personal liability upon the company's shareholder when: (a) the company is clearly a 'sham' and it has been used fraudulently to avoid an existing obligation. See Appendix 3 in your online study resources: 'Lifting the veil of incorporation' . The lifting of the veil has two consequences: firstly, it allows creditors of a company to impose personal liability upon the company's shareholder and, secondly, it permit the creditors of a shareholder to enforce payment against the assets of the company. For example, in Jones v Lipman [1962] 1 WLR 832 a court ordered A to transfer certain property to B whereupon, in order to avoid such legal obligation, A transferred the property to a company of which A was sole shareholder. The court was prepared to lift the veil of incorporation and permit B to enforce the original obligation against the property of the company. In Gencor ACP Ltd v Dalby [2000] EWHC 1560

(Ch) a director of a public company (Company A) dishonestly stole money from the company and transferred it to Company B, a private company owned and controlled by him. Company A obtained judgment against the director, but the court permitted it to enforce the judgment against the assets of Company B. It was held that Company B was a mere façade and had been formed by the director in order to fraudulently avoid his existing liability to Company.

16. i. Limited liability is prima facie unfair to, and operates against, the interests of creditors of a company. The risk of corporate collapse is taken by creditors rather than the owners of the enterprise.
- ii. It is justified on the grounds of economic expediency - the overall good to the company and the wider economy outweighs the prejudice suffered by a few creditors.
- iii. By either:
4. (a) obtaining security such as a mortgage or charge over corporate assets for moneys owed by the company; and/or
5. (b) obtaining personal guarantees from the shareholders/directors.
- iv. A company may be limited by shares or by guarantee (usually the former).
17. A company can exist forever, until terminated by a formal winding-up procedure which can be voluntary or compulsory.
18. i. A partner may sell their interest in a partnership if the remaining partners agree. The main practical drawback of such a sale is that the outgoing partner remains liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership incurred while they were a partner.
- ii. By transferring their shares as a voluntary gift to a donee or by sale to a willing buyer which are effected by the transferor and transferee executing a simple share transfer and lodging it with the company secretary (or equivalent) to update the register of members of the company.
- iii. There may be restrictions upon a transfer of shares in the articles of a private limited company. This is usual when the number of shareholders is small and they have a personal involvement in the business insofar as each shareholder also sits on the board of the company. In these circumstances, Table A articles state that any transfer of shares requires the consent by board resolution of the directors.
- iv. A more unusual restriction placed on share transfers is a pre-emption right conferred by the articles upon the shareholders. If a shareholder wishes to transfer their shares they first must offer them for sale at a fair price to the other shareholders.
19. To hold and invest corporate assets, and to allow those assets to be managed by the directors of the company on behalf of the shareholders. When the shares are held by nominees for a single beneficial owner we say the company is 'free-standing' or stand-

alone; when the shares are held by a corporate trustee we say the company serves as an 'underlying company' to hold the trust fund on behalf of the trustee.

20. The nationality of a company is determined by the jurisdiction where it is incorporated. A company cannot choose which law governs its internal management and administration. So, for example, a company incorporated in State A is governed by the laws of State A (although a company incorporated in State A can amend its constitutive documents (Module 5) and it may be able to migrate to State B (Module 4).

- ii. A promoter must not make a profit out of the incorporation process without disclosing it to the directors or to the members of the company, or to potential members of the company if it is intended to issue additional shares to investors.
- iii. The sale of the land by A to ABCD Ltd for $2 million is proper provided A makes full and frank disclosure of their initial purchase of the asset for $1 million) to the company's board of directors and to B, C and D. If they fail to do so, they have made a 'secret profit' and amounts to a breach of their fiduciary duty to the company. Accordingly, the company ABCD Ltd may rescind the contract or affirm it but compel A to turn over their profit of $1 million to the company or sue them for compensation.
- iv. No, although typical Table A articles confer a power upon the board of the company to reimburse such expenses when the company has been formed (usually exercised onshore so that the costs of incorporation become a company expense to set-off against revenues for corporation tax purposes).
2. i. At common law, the contract is not binding on the company, nor can the company ratify and unilaterally adopt the contract. If the company wishes to be bound by the contract it must enter into a new contract with X after the date of its incorporation (which therefore requires X's consent) for it to be enforceable by or against the company. The promoter is liable under the terms of the original contract, not the company.
- ii. Under modern companies legislation, the company by board resolution may unilaterally adopt a pre-incorporation contract (which does not require X's consent), whereupon the company may sue and be sued for breach and the personal liability of the promoter under the contract is discharged.
3. a. Public companies are appropriate when shares are to be offered for sale to the investing public or the company is to be listed on a stock exchange.
- b. Unlimited liability companies are used mainly for incorporated professional associations of lawyers or accountants.
- c. Guarantee companies are used for 'not-for-profit' business activities where any profits of the company will not be distributed to its members but will be retained and re-invested in the business of the company.
- d. In those jurisdictions where the distinction still persists, so-called 'Local' or 'Domestic' or 'Resident' companies are used to carry on business within the local economy of the geographical territory of the centre of incorporation, whereas 'IBCs' , 'Exempt' or 'Non-resident, tax-exempt' companies are designed

for international offshore use insofar as they cannot trade or carry on business within the local economy of the geographical territory of incorporation nor typically to hold local real estate. They are generally used to hold 'foreign' assets.

- e. In those jurisdictions where LLCs are permitted, an LLC may be used for general asset-holding as well as trading or operating purposes. However, as an LLC is very flexible in terms of its management, organisation and allocation of profits and losses, it is often more typically used as a vehicle for investment funds, joint ventures, private equity funds, securitisation and international financing.

8. (vi) the appropriate fee.

The name of a company must distinguish it from other companies incorporated in that jurisdiction; it must contain the word 'Limited' (or equivalent) if the liability of its shareholders is limited to the unpaid amount payable when shares were acquired; the name must not be misleading or suggest a connection with government or that it performs a regulated activity. 9. (a) IBCs

The IBC is very common in offshore financial centres located in the Caribbean and other 'nil-tax' centres. Examples of the centres that offer IBCs include Antigua, Anguilla, the Bahamas, Barbados, Belize, British Virgin Islands (now called BVI BCs), Niue, St. Vincent and the Seychelles. In the Cook Islands, Mauritius and Samoa, corporate vehicles that have characteristics similar to those of the IBCs are known as International Companies. In Bermuda, the Cayman Islands and Turks and Caicos Islands there is a similar vehicle known as an 'exempted company'. In this context 'exempted' refers to the fact that they are exempt from the requirement to be majority owned by Caymanian citizens. The following are some of the significant features of these vehicles:

- (i) single and corporate directors are allowed;
- (ii) single members are allowed, subscribers do not have to take up any shares, and shareholder resolutions are passed by majority vote;
- (iii) a company secretary is not required (but if one is voluntarily appointed it may be a corporate secretary);
- (iv) directors need not be resident in the offshore centre of incorporation and there are no rules regarding frequency and location of director's meetings;
- (v) annual general meetings of shareholders are not required, nor are accounts required to be laid before the members at an AGM;
- (vi) a formal annual return is not required, but an annual duty is payable to the Registrar;
- (vii) a registered agent is required, that is, an agent who must reside in the jurisdiction of incorporation and who is responsible for ensuring that the local company law requirements are adhered to;
- (viii) limited information is required by the Registrar of Companies and even less information is public;
- (ix) they are not permitted to trade in the offshore centre, although this prohibition is being relaxed under OECD pressure; and
- (x) they enjoy modern flexible capital maintenance provisions, reviewed in Module 6.

- (b) LLCs

An LLC is a separate legal entity offering its shareholders limited liability, but it differs from an ordinary company in three ways. Firstly, an LLC does not have a board of directors. Instead, the company is managed by its shareholders, called members, of which there are two types: the ordinary members and the managing member. The managing member is appointed by the ordinary members and is given all powers of management of the business of the company on a day-to-day basis. Secondly, its constitutive documents are somewhat unique insofar as they have Articles of Organisation and, in addition, it is typical for the members and managing member to enter into an Operating Agreement - this is an agreement that sets out internal rules governing such matters as membership rights, powers and duties of the managing member and their operating procedures, matters relating to distribution of profits, etc. Finally, when formed an LLC can elect to be taxed for US tax purposes as either: (i) a 'C Corporation' and thereby become liable to US corporation tax upon its worldwide profits from every source; or (ii) an 'S Corporation' and be treated for US tax purposes as a partnership. Most LLCs elect to be taxed as an S Corporation, so report their profits but are not taxed upon them. Instead, the LLC's profits are treated as pass-through so all annual profits are attributed to the members.

- (c) LDCs

A limited duration company (LDC) enjoys the usual characteristics of a registered company but has a limited duration, generally 30 years or until the completion of a specified task or activity set out in the company's articles of association. An LDC has its origins in the company laws of the US where the Internal Revenue Service treat an LDC as a partnership for tax purposes, providing some tax planning opportunities for US citizens and corporations.

10. Protected Cell Companies (PCCs) were developed in Guernsey in response to the requirements of the local investment fund industry and are now also available in other jurisdictions by slightly different names. They share the usual characteristics of a limited company, save for one significant respect: their capital is divided up into separate parts, or cells, with assets held in each cell being segregated and insulated from assets in other cells. In theory, if the assets held in one cell are attached by creditors of the company, the assets in the other cells are protected because of the segregation of the capital. This can create benefits for those who create offshore collective investment funds, as the capital may be divided into separate cells. Each cell can then be operated as a separate sub-fund within the collective investment fund, thus enabling parts of the fund to invest in high-risk assets while preserving the protection of the other parts of the fund where increased risk is not acceptable. PCCs can also be used to avoid onshore controlled foreign company anti-tax avoidance legislation, which is dealt with in the STEP Advanced Certificate in Principles of International Taxation, AML and Compliance.

## Module 4:

## Answers to self-assessment questions

1. A foreign company (incorporated for instance in State A, say America) may wish to establish a place of business in an offshore jurisdiction (State B, say, the Bahamas). The companies legislation of a number of common law offshore jurisdictions with legislation based on the 1948 Act model permits this under what is known as a registration procedure applicable to foreign (or so-called 'overseas') companies. Alternatively, a foreign company (incorporated in State A) may wish to migrate from State A and become re-domiciled as a company in another jurisdiction (say, State B, the Bahamas). The company thereby becomes a State B company governed by the laws of State B. The companies legislation of a number of offshore jurisdictions with modern and flexible company legislation permits this under what is known as a continuation procedure applicable to foreign companies.
2. Whether a company incorporated in State A has 'established a place of business' in an alternative jurisdiction (State B, and therefore must comply with the registration requirements of State B) is a question of fact. There must be a specific place of business and identifiable persons conducting it. In Artemis v Artemis Transportation Corp [1982] HKCA 29 it was held that for a company to establish a place of business in a particular place it requires:
3. (a) some fixed place of business (such as an office or factory);
4. (b) human agents who reside in the jurisdiction for the purposes of carrying on the business of the company; and
5. (c) the conduct of such business should endure for a sufficiently substantial period of time to be regarded as a 'permanent establishment' .
3. Prior to, or within x months (usually one) of 'establishing a permanent place of business' in State B, a foreign company (incorporated in State A) must apply to become registered in State B. The application requires the foreign company to file the following documents with the Registrar of Companies of State B:
7. (a) a certified copy of its constitutive documents (typically its memorandum and articles);
8. (b) a certified copy of its certificate of incorporation, or other evidence of incorporation;
9. (c) particulars of its directors and other officers of the company;
10. (d) the name and address of the company's 'authorised representative' (a.k.a. 'authorised agent'), namely a person, resident within the offshore jurisdiction where the foreign company intends to operate, who is authorised to accept service of process and submit thereby to the jurisdiction of the courts of State B; and
11. (e) the appropriate registration fee. If content that the application is proper and in order, the Registrar will enter the name of the company on the Register of Foreign Companies (or equivalent) and issue a certificate of registration.

4. The foreign company's local authorised agent/representative has two functions:
2. (a) the authorised agent/representative is designed to accept service of legal process on behalf of the company so that it thereby submits to the authority of the court of the offishore centre. For example, a writ (complaint or similar legal process) issued by the court of State B is deemed sufficiently served upon the foreign company if it is properly addressed and delivered to the company's authorised agent/representative in State B. The foreign company will not then be able to ignore the proceedings by claiming that the court has no jurisdiction over it and arguing that it is only subject to the laws and the courts of its jurisdiction of incorporation; and
3. (b) the authorised agent/representative should ensure that, so far as they are able, the foreign company complies with applicable local laws and regulations, so there is someone on local soil for the authorities to question and hold accountable if thing go wrong.
5. Statutory migration provides a way to transfer a business from one jurisdiction to another and, thereby, avoids winding-up the company in the first jurisdiction and transferring each and every asset to a new company incorporated in the second jurisdiction. There are a number of reasons for doing this, for instance, to:
5. (a) take advantage of a more favourable regulatory or tax regime or more conducive business environment;
6. (b) cut costs and streamline ongoing administration of the company;
7. (c) consolidate or merge with existing group structures;
8. (d) obtain access to new business opportunities; and/or
9. (e) obtain access to specialist capital markets.
6. The application to migrate a company from State A to State B typically requires a service provider acting for the company to prepare and file the following documents with the Registrar of Companies in State B:
11. (a) a copy of an appropriate (often, special) resolution of the shareholders of the company confirming the company's intention to migrate (e.g. Appendix 10A or 10B of your online study resources);
12. (b) a certified copy of the constitutive documents (memorandum and articles of association, and translations, if appropriate);
13. (c) articles of continuation, if necessary, containing amendments to the memorandum and articles that are to be effective upon re-registration and that will leave the company compliant with local companies legislation requirements;
14. (d) satisfactory evidence that the company is in good standing in the jurisdiction of incorporation;
15. (e) satisfactory evidence that the company is solvent (a requirement that exists to ensure that the company is not trying to run away from payment of its debts and liabilities incurred in State A); and
16. (f ) most jurisdictions state that the foreign company must be authorised to migrate and transfer its domicile according to the law of the state of incorporation (or at least is not prohibited from doing so), and has complied with the requirements of the laws of the state of incorporation to de-register in such jurisdiction.

Note that the 'satisfactory evidence' referred to above is typically an affidavit by one of the board members of the company which confirms that each of the statutory requirements are complied with and, where necessary, exhibits verifying documentation (e.g. Appendix 10C of the online Study Resources). If the application is proper and in order, the registrar will issue a 'certificate of continuation' .

7. Upon the issue of the certificate of continuation referred to in the answer to SAQ 6, above, the (former foreign) company is deemed to be a company incorporated under and existing according to the offshore companies legislation of State B, and in particular:
2. (a) its memorandum and articles, as amended by the certificate of continuation, continue to apply;
3. (b) all property, including the benefit and obligations of all contracts, remains vested in the company;
4. (c) the company continues to be liable for all its existing debts, claims and liabilities;
5. (d) all shares owned by shareholders are deemed issued in conformity with local companies legislation and continue to be held by the registered shareholders; and
6. (e) in a nutshell, the company continues to exist but has simply migrated to, and become incorporated in, another jurisdiction.
8. See the specimen shareholders' resolution illustrated in Appendix 10B of the online Study Resources.

## Module 5:

## Answers to self-assessment questions

1. The broad corporate constitution, comprising the memorandum and articles of association, is used for all forms of company, incorporated for many different purposes: public/private ownership, onshore/offshore, large/small, operating a business/passive asset-holding. Nonetheless, companies legislation permits considerable flexibility. The contents of the constitutive documents of a BVI company which is listed on the NYSE would not be the same as a BVI company that is owned by a single beneficial owner, with a sole director, whose purpose is simply to hold and shelter family wealth.
2. The articles of association contain internal rules of management that affect only insiders within the organisation of the company, namely shareholders, directors, and other officers. Within the broad framework of the companies legislation, a company is free to adopt whichever articles containing whatever rules of management and administration of its business that the promoters desire and consider appropriate. Matters contained in the memorandum, such as the name of the company, its registered office and objects, the extent of the liability of its shareholders and the capital of the company are of fundamental importance to outsiders - these matters are therefore contained in the memorandum, which is registrable and open to inspection by the public. (In those jurisdictions such as the UK, Hong Kong and Singapore, where the memorandum has been abandoned, the usual content as described above is communicated to the Registrar in the application form submitted upon incorporation of the company and is available online to the public.)
3. The memorandum contains clauses dealing with the company's name, registered office, liability of its members, its objects and capital of the company. For IBCs there is an additional requirement to provide the name of the registered agent.
4. The registered office of a company is required to facilitate formal communications with the company; establish the nationality of the company; and as a physical place within the jurisdiction of incorporation to keep the corporate records of the company.
5. i. In the case of Ashbury Railway Carriage Co Ltd v Riche (1875) LR 7 HL 653 the objects of the company were to make, sell and repair railway carriages.
- ii. The company agreed with Mr Riche to build a whole railway system.
- iii. When that contract was broken and the parties to it tried to sue for compensation the court held that it was void as the company had acted ultra vires , namely beyond its powers contained in its objects. Neither party could enforce the contract - both were without redress against the other in the event of breach.
- iv. The rationale behind the ultra vires rule is investor protection, i.e. to protect investor shareholders, specifically by preventing directors from using company assets for purposes wholly different from those purposes contemplated by the shareholders when they invested in the company.

6. The ultra vires doctrine was considered unsatisfactory for two reasons. Firstly, it caused companies to adopt very wide and lengthy objects so the rule served little practical purpose and, secondly, it was a trap for unwary third parties. The doctrine has therefore been reformed by modern company by legislation enacted offshore by following one of four models:
2. (i) some jurisdictions follow the model found in the English 1985 Act which states that provided the third party enters into a contract with a company 'in good faith' but the contract is outside the scope of the company's objects clause, the contract is nevertheless valid and binding;
3. (ii) the 1989 Act model removed the requirement of 'good faith';
4. (iii) the 2006 Act model made the objects clause optional, so the law in either sub-para (i) or (ii) applies whenever an objects clause exists, but a company may instead elect in its objects clause to undertake any type of business activity; and
5. (iv) some jurisdictions such as Jersey and the Isle of Man have repealed the doctrine altogether, so all companies automatically have capacity to enter into any business activity.
7. i. $100,000 and $50,000 respectively.
- ii. $100, $150 and $50 respectively.
8. As a general rule the memorandum may be altered by the shareholders of the company by passing a special resolution in general meeting (i.e. requiring a supermajority to vote in favour of the amendment). The amendment must be filed with the registrar. However, note:
9. (i) the address of the registered office can be changed by directors resolution (see section 3.2);
10. (ii) if the liability of shareholders is changed from limited to unlimited, the amendment requires the unanimous approval of the whole body of shareholders (see section 3.4); and
11. (iii) IBCs do not distinguish between ordinary and special resolutions of shareholders unless specific articles state otherwise and it is lawful for specific articles to confer the power to amend the constitutive documents upon the directors, requiring a simple board resolution.
9. Model articles are standard articles, sometimes annexed to companies legislation (not the IBC Acts of the Bahamas or the BVI), which a company's promoter may adopt in whole or in part during the incorporation process.
10. See section 4.3 of the course manual and the specimen articles in Appendix 2 and 3 of your online Study Resources.
11. As with the memorandum, the general rule is that a company's articles may be altered by the company by passing a special resolution in general meeting - although see the notes contained in the answer to SAQ 8 above.
12. An alteration to the articles is subject to the implied rule that any alteration must be bona fide in the interests of the company as a whole (not prejudicial to the minority shareholders nor solely for the benefit of the majority shareholder). See the cases of

Greenhalgh v Arderne Cinemas Ltd [1951] Ch 286 and Dafen Tinplate Co Ltd v Llanelly Steel Co (1907) Ltd [1920] 2 Ch 124 to illustrate the restriction in section 5 of the course manual.

## Module 6:

## Answers to self-assessment questions

1. 'Capital' in the non-technical sense is a sum of money or other property that may be used by a business to make profits. 'Equity finance' refers to the process of a company that raises capital through the sale of shares. By selling shares a company essentially sells an ownership interest of the company in consideration for cash to finance expansion of its business operations.
2. A public company may raise capital by selling its shares to the investing public. The issue of shares to the public may be made in one of two ways, namely by way of a 'public offer' or 'private placement' . A public offer is made when a company invites the general public to subscribe for its shares and the procedure is heavily regulated for investor protection purposes - see section 1.1 of the course manual. A placement is a private arrangement, the terms of which are negotiated by the directors of a company and a specific investor, whereby the investor agrees to take a certain number of shares in a company for an agreed price.
3. When the subscribers sign the memorandum and submit it to the registrar to incorporate a company, they agree to take the number of shares opposite their names see the final paragraph of Appendix 1 of your online Study Resources. Accordingly, once the company has been incorporated, the subscribers become, by operation of law, the first shareholder(s) of the company. The subscribers take what are known as the 'subscriber shares' .
4. The procedure for a private company to issue additional shares is described in section 1.2.2, Stages 1-6, namely:
5. (i) ascertain who has the power to decide whether the company should issue shares (usually the directors);
6. (ii) the directors seek expressions of interest;
7. (iii) investors apply to subscribe for shares at the stated issue price - see Appendix 16B of the online Study Resources;
8. (iv) the directors resolve to allot the shares - see Appendix 16C of the online Study Resources;
9. (v) the directors issue the shares in consideration for the investor paying the company the agreed price, a share certificate is prepared and issued and the name of the new shareholder included in the register of members; and
10. (vi) in some jurisdictions, the company must make a return of allotment to the registrar.
5. Holders of fully-paid shares of a limited liability company are not liable for the debts of that company. Accordingly, creditors of the company may not be paid what is properly due to them if the company suffers financial collapse and becomes insolvent - they cannot impose liability upon the shareholders personally. In an attempt to reduce the potential prejudice suffered by creditors by the introduction of limited liability,

the capital maintenance doctrine tries to ensure that a company maintains a capital fund so that, if it is subsequently wound up, this fund can be used to pay creditors. Once a shareholder has been issued shares in the company, the price paid for them becomes the 'capital' of the company. This capital fund cannot be distributed back to the shareholders. It must be maintained in the business as a reserve to be used, if necessary, for the payment of the company's creditors.

6. The capital maintenance doctrine has an effect at two stages:
- when capital is initially introduced into a company - the doctrine ensures that shareholders make full and proper payment for their shares and the consideration amounts to a price of not less than the 'par value' of each share; and
- after start-up, during the lifetime of a company, the doctrine ensures that the capital of a company (initially introduced by shareholders in exchange for shares), is not distributed to the shareholders so as to deplete the company's reserves of capital.
7. (a) A company must not sell its shares at a discount, below the shares' par value, as this would dilute its capital (to the prejudice of future creditors). If the 500 shares were sold and issued for a total consideration of £1,000, this would amount to £2 per share, and therefore the sum of £8 below their par value. The directors have, in these circumstances, committed a criminal offence.
5. (b) A company may issue shares for their par value or above it, at a premium. If the 500 shares were sold and issued for £100,000, it would amount to £200 per share. A company may sell its shares at a price equal to their par value in which case the consideration received by the company is credited to its share capital account. Alternatively a company may sell its shares at any price above the par value of the shares, in which case the premium must be credited to a 'share premium account'. The share premium account is treated like capital, and its credit balance cannot be distributed back to shareholders. Accordingly, if the shares are issued for £100,000, XYZ Ltd must allocate £5,000 to its 'share capital' and allocate the remaining £95,000 to the company's 'share premium' account.
8. The consideration paid by shareholders for their shares is credited to different accounts of the company which are subject to slightly different rules. The issued share capital (the aggregate of the number of shares multiplied by the par value of each share) is credited to the company's 'share capital' account. Whereas, the premium (the number of issued shares multiplied by the premium, i.e. the price paid in excess of the par value of each share, is credited to the 'share premium' account (known in the US as 'capital surplus').
9. A company's 'capital' must be maintained by the company as a notional fund to pay creditors. It cannot be used to distribute to shareholders as a dividend as this would deplete the company's capital to the prejudice of creditors. Capital in this context is the sum allocated to the company's share capital account as well as the sum allocated to its share premium account. The same rule, except expressed in the positive, is that a company can only finance distributions to shareholders by way of a dividend out of earned 'profits' . Profits in this context is defined as:
8. (i) the profit generated by the company in the latest financial year as shown in the company's income statement as compiled according to generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP); and

- (ii) profits generated in prior years (less losses) that were retained in a reserve of undistributed retained earnings.
10. The capital maintenance doctrine never really achieved its purposes of protecting creditors for several reasons. Firstly, a company was not required to issue a minimum number of shares, so a company's capital may be a very modest sum if only few shares are issued. Secondly, the par value of a share does not have to correspond to the commercial market value of the share, so can be fixed artificially low. Finally, the share capital and share premium account are accounting entries in the company's balance sheet and the funds do not have to be segregated in a ring-fenced bank account, So the funds can actually be depleted by the company paying creditors in the usual course of its business, not just upon the company being wound-up.
11. The issued share capital (monies or other property received by a company in exchange for the issue of shares) must be maintained in the company and must not be distributed back to the shareholders save upon the dissolution of the company on a winding-up. However, that capital fund may be used by the company to pay creditors of the company in the normal course of business. Accordingly, if a company makes a loss in a financial year, it may have to pay its debts by resorting to capital (unless it has some other liquid reserves). If the company continues to make a loss for several years it is inevitable that it will ultimately become insolvent. If it is wound up at this stage, some creditors will not be paid in full. Accordingly, the capital maintenance doctrine may prevent the transfer of capital out of a company to shareholders, but it does not prevent it being transferred out of a company to pay creditors.
12. The capital maintenance doctrine does not satisfy the requirements of the owners of an asset-holding company (the beneficial owner or corporate trustee) who, not only wish to transfer unlimited sums of money into the company, but also to take unlimited sums out of the company when the need arises. The capital maintenance doctrine restricts the amount of money that a company can lawfully distribute. (For this reason, the wealth management industry has devised methodologies to get around the doctrine, as explained in subsequent questions.)
13. A typical asset-holding company incorporated in a jurisdiction which has retained the capital maintenance rules is financed by:
- a nominal share capital of no more than $1 or $2, comprising of the funds received by the company from its shareholder(s) who take just one or two shares with a par value of $1 each (so the share capital is a nominal $1 or $2)
- significant borrowing (or debt), namely the funds lent by the beneficial owner/ trustee and borrowed by the company as a loan in consideration for the issue of a short-term promissory note.
14. Most offshore asset-holding companies are financed by a nominal share capital and enormous shareholder loan from the beneficial owner. The advantage of financing an asset-holding company in this way is distribution flexibility. A company that has borrowed money must comply with the agreed terms of the borrowing. Accordingly, if the lender (namely the beneficial owner, wearing their hat not as beneficial owner of the shares of the company, but as creditor) makes a written demand for payment, in whole or in part, of the outstanding balance of the loan, in accordance with the terms of the original lending, the company must comply. Repayment of debt is not subject to the capital maintenance doctrine.

15. Loans are often documented by a promissory note repayable without interest upon demand - see Appendix 20A of your online study resources.
16. By AB calling in the loan by making a demand to repay it in whole or in part (as it is typically repayable upon demand) or by dissolving the company. A specimen demand for repayment is illustrated in Appendix 20B of the online Study Resources and a directors resolution to repay the loan in accordance with such demand is illustrated in Appendix 20D of the online Study Resources.
17. Under ss.35 and 36 of the IBCA 2000 of the Bahamas, dividends may be paid out of distributable profits (which include unrealised gains) and out of capital surplus (what Britons call the share premium account) namely the moneys paid by shareholders upon the issue of their shares attributable to the premium - the amount of the price in excess of their par value. The authority to distribute dividends out of unrealised gains and from the share premium account is subject to an overriding pre-condition namely that the company is solvent and its realisable assets exceed the sum of its total liabilities plus share capital. The directors must certify that the company satisfies the solvency test before the distribution is made - see Appendix 20E. Similar provisions exist in the Cayman Islands, Bermuda and Jersey. For the BVI, Guernsey and Man equivalent, see the answer to SAQ 17 below.
18. The concept of capital has been completely replaced. See section 7 of the course manual. Distributions of any amount, in cash or other property, can be made whenever the BVI BC is solvent and remains so immediately after the distribution. A declaration of solvency must be made by the directors, which is usually recorded in the resolution authorising (or recommending) the distribution, an example is given in Appendix 20E of your online study resources.

## Module 7:

## Answers to self-assessment questions

1. i. Ownership of a company is vested in its shareholders. However, the business of a company must be managed and governed. The traditional model was that this could be achieved if there was a separation of ownership from the power to manage the business. Based on this, specific roles were developed in relation to the ordinary shareholders and the board of directors. The board of directors manage business and are accountable to, and therefore supervised by, the shareholders. Ownership of a company is therefore vested in the shareholders, but a company usually assigns power to manage and control the business of the company to its directors.
ii. Reasons for the separation of ownership and control are as follows:
  - a) Capital : to enable entrepreneurs to raise capital from the investing public.
  - b) Expertise : shareholders are traditionally pure investors, usually numerous and without the specialist knowledge or expertise to run a company, whereas directors are usually executives/entrepreneurs and well qualified to manage the business of the company on a daily basis.
  - c) Continuity : if shareholders sell their investment, the management of the business continues regardless of a change of membership.
  - d) Risk and efficiency : shareholders assume little risk if the company is limited by shares, whereas the entrepreneurial managers have a far greater incentive for the enterprise to succeed.
- iii. The separation of ownership and control is not absolute. Shareholders in general meeting retain the right to exercise key powers, such as to amend the constitution, remove and appoint new directors and voluntarily wind up the company. Beyond these strategic matters, however, the day-to-day management and control of the business is, by the articles, usually delegated to the directors. The division of power is, however, a matter for the articles and, in this regard, note the terms of Table A articles:
8. (a) art.80 of Table A of the 1948 Act;
9. (b) art.70 of Table A of the 1985 Act;
10. (c) art.70 of the current English Companies (Model Articles) Regulations 2008, which allows the shareholders, by special resolution, to give written and binding directions to the directors obliging them to exercise their powers of management in the manner specified.
11. iv The directors owe their fiduciary duties and duty of care to the company. They are therefore obliged to run the company for the ultimate benefit of all the shareholders. A number of other persons and entities associated with a company are effectively disenfranchised, and are not given a voice to affect the

course of a company in any significant way (unless they are also directors and/ or shareholders), for example: non-voting preference shareholders, debenture and bond holders, creditors, employees and executives, auditors, regulators and customers. Indeed, in some of the more sophisticated onshore jurisdictions such as England, directors are now required to not only serve the interests of the company, but also have regard to: the interests of employees, suppliers, customers and others, the community and environment; maintain a reputation for high standards of business (s.171 of the Companies Act 2006). But this type of modern provision has not been exported to the offshore common law jurisdictions which maintain the traditional model.

- iv. Because the traditional onshore corporate model is used as a template by the legislature of the common law offshore jurisdictions when drafting companies legislation, and by corporate service providers when drafting articles of association of companies incorporated in those jurisdictions.
2. i. The division of power is essentially governed by the articles rather than prescribed by legislation. As the articles are originally drafted on the instructions of the promoter/subscribers, who upon incorporation become the first shareholders, and as the articles can be amended by the shareholders by passing a resolution of the company in general meeting, this means that the division of power is effectively controlled by the shareholders.
- ii. The role of the directors is set out in the articles. A typical article based upon art.80 of Table A of the Companies Act 1948 in the UK, provides that 'the business of the company shall be managed by the directors, who may exercise all powers of the company as are not, by the companies legislation or the articles, required to be exercised by the company in general meeting' .
- iii. If art.80 of Table A of the Companies Act 1948 is adopted, the shareholders cannot control the exercise of the powers of the directors, A and B, to manage the company. The company in general meeting cannot compel the directors to do something, such as enter into a transaction, that they do not otherwise wish to do. Nonetheless, although the shareholders D-J cannot give binding instructions to the directors, they are generally empowered to remove the directors and appoint new ones (and thereby control the board) or wind-up the company. As a final alternative, they can, of course sell their shares.
- iv. Under the terms of art.70 of the Companies Act 1985, a super-majority of shareholders may pass a special resolution of the company in general meeting to compel the directors to comply with directions given to them by the shareholders in connection with the management of the business of the company.
- v. As a general rule, shareholders can expect typical articles to confer a power upon them, in general meeting to:
7. (a) amend the constitution of the company (memorandum or articles)
8. (b) alter the company's capital structure
9. (c) remove the directors and appoint new ones
10. (d) migrate to another jurisdiction
11. (e) voluntarily wind up the company.

3. Article 20 - the companies legislation does not usually require a director to hold shares in the company, however it may be a requirement under the articles.

Article 21 - the minimum number of directors of a company is prescribed by statute (it used to be two, although a sole director is now permitted by most jurisdictions); however, the terms of the articles may prescribe otherwise; the articles may fix a minimum quorum necessary for the directors to transact business.

Article 22 - companies legislation does not require a director to retire every third year.

Article 23 - it is usual for the articles to confer upon the shareholders a power to set the remuneration of the directors, rather than state a fixed annual fee. It is quite lawful for the articles of a company to do all these things, however, in which case the provisions, set out in arts 20-23, bind the company and the directors. It would, however, be unusual to find these particular articles in the articles of association of an offshore asset-holding company.

4. i. See section 3.3.1 for the initial appointment of directors by the subscribers to the memorandum, which may be prescribed by companies legislation. If it is not prescribed by legislation, similar provisions are typically contained in a company's articles. The procedure is: for the director to give their consent to the appointment (Appendix 21A); the subscribers then appoint the director(s) by resolution (Appendix 21B); the director(s) may enter into a DSA (Appendix 21C); and their names must be entered into the register of directors and officers (Appendix 7B) and notice sent to the registrar.
- ii. Subsequent directors are appointed by the method prescribed in the company's articles. Traditional Table A 1948 articles state that subsequent directors are appointed by the company in general meeting - an ordinary resolution of the shareholders at the company's AGM. A 'casual vacancy' may be filled by resolution of the board of directors, but only until the next AGM when the shareholders appoint a permanent board member. More modern articles confer the power upon either:
3. (a) the shareholders in general meeting; or
4. (b) upon the board itself, which the remaining directors can exercise by simple board resolution.
- iii. For an example of straight voting as opposed to cumulative voting see the final paragraph of section 3.3.2.
5. i. Resignation and retirement (as opposed to by rotation) is a voluntary act effected by the director whereby they step down from the board. Such resignation, if prior to the expiry of a fixed term of office, must comply with any contractual provision agreed between the company and the director, otherwise the director may find themselves in breach of contract. See section 4.1.
- ii. Mandatory vacation from the office occurs when a director is removed from office for just cause on the grounds that are set out in the articles, such as bankruptcy, being of unsound mind, or being convicted of an indictable offence, or simply being required to vacate office by the other directors. See section 4.2.

- iii. There may be a statutory provision (based on the UK's Companies Act 1948) permitting a director to be removed from office without cause by ordinary resolution of the shareholders in general meeting. Further, or alternatively, it is common for an express article to provide for removal by the company in general meeting, again without the shareholders having to justify their action by having reasonable grounds. Section 4.3.
- iv. Disqualification occurs pursuant to modern companies legislation whereby, in the public interest, regulators may take proceedings in a court of law for a 'disqualification order' in circumstances where a director is unfit to act.
6. The issues raised include whether:
- Z is prepared to sell their shares to X and Y, and if so whether the articles contain any restrictions upon transfer
- the articles allow X and Y, who command 66.6% of votes, to convene a general meeting of the company to remove Z as director by ordinary resolution; if a special resolution is required, the articles may allow the directors to allot further shares to Y and X to increase their shareholding and dilute Z's shareholding (but this may amount to an act of oppression that prejudices the minority shareholders)
- the minimum number of directors, and the quorum requirements, are satisfied
- as a last resort, X and Y may voluntarily wind up the company if a 66% majority is enough to pass a special resolution (or if a resolution to voluntarily wind-up the company may be passed by board resolution or by a simple majority of shareholders).

## Module 8:

## Answers to self-assessment questions

1. In reaching decisions, the directors must follow the procedure laid down in the company's articles. Usually:
2. (a) the directors must convene a meeting of the board in accordance with the articles, by reasonable notice to all directors giving the time, place and date of the meeting and details of motions to be discussed;
3. (b) the meeting must be quorate;
4. (c) directors attend to discuss motions on the agenda; and
5. (d) the directors vote and pass a board resolution when the majority of directors present vote in favour of the motion. If the directors are in unanimous agreement, a written resolution may be passed, dispensing with the need to hold a formal meeting.
2. i. Even though the directors of XYZ Ltd have acted beyond their powers (i.e. their action is ultra vires ), the contract between XYZ Ltd and AB is valid and enforceable, provided AB acted in good faith, i.e. without knowing that the directors had acted beyond their powers or that the company's internal management requirements had not been complied with. This is the position both at common law ( Royal British Bank v Turquand (1856) 6 E&amp;B 327) and under modern companies legislation enacted in most jurisdictions that codify the rule in Royal British Bank by following the statutory model found in s.40 of the Companies Act 2006.
- ii. AB may commence legal proceedings against XYZ Ltd for breach of contract and seek damages.
- iii. The directors of XYZ Ltd are liable to the company for any loss suffered by the company, which was caused by them acting ultra vires , in excess of the powers conferred upon them.
3. Directors owe the following duties to the company:
- i. the equitable fiduciary to:
11. (a) exercise their powers of management in good faith in a manner that benefits the company (described in section 2.1.1 of the course manual); and
12. (b) with integrity and loyalty, by observing the various rules relating to no-self-dealing, no-conflicts, no-profits unless authorised and no-benefits from third parties (section 2.1.2).
- ii. the common law duty to use reasonable care and skill in exercising their powers of management of the business of the company (described in section 2.2).

4. The fiduciary duty requires a director:
- i. to act in good faith in manner that benefits and is in the best interests of the company (see the examples in section 2.1.1 of Hutton v West Cork Railway Co. (1883) 23 Ch D 654, Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver [1942] 1 All ER 378, Lee Panavision Ltd v Lee Lighting Ltd [1992] BCLC 22, Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi [2005] 2 BCLC 91)
- ii. to act with scrupulous loyalty by complying with the various sub-species of fiduciary duty, namely: no self-dealing, no-conflicts of interests, no profits unless authorised; no benefits from third parties (see the various examples in section 2.1.2).
5. i. Director A is in breach of their fiduciary duty owed to AB Ltd, namely in breach of:
5. (a) the implied duty to exercise good faith and use their powers for their proper purposes to benefit the company, rather than for the benefit of themselves or their associates, and to avoid conflicts; and
6. (b) the no-conflicts rule, the no-profits and no-benefits from third parties rules.
- ii. Whenever a director enters into a transaction with their company (on a personal basis or via an associate), the transaction may be set aside by the company. The company, AB Ltd may, therefore, rescind the contract with AC Ltd, or enforce it, at its option.
- iii. AB Ltd may rescind the agreement (as above) or elect to retain it but seek an order requiring A to turn over all the profits they make to AB Ltd.
- iv. Director A should have obtained the informed consent of the company AB Ltd, by shareholders' resolution in general meeting following full disclosure of their interest in the management agreement with AC Ltd. This is the position at common law. Companies legislation in some jurisdictions, and certain articles of association, may allow the director to disclose their interest and obtain approval from a disinterested quorum of the directors of AB Ltd. For a specimen article, see art.18.2 of Appendix 1B.
6. i. The directors of XYZ Ltd must comply with their fiduciary duties:
11. (a) exercise their powers over corporate assets in a manner that is beneficial to the company, not for their own benefit; and
12. (b) must observe the no-conflicts rule and refrain from entering into a contract with the company in their personal capacity. An interest-free loan has no corporate benefit and is therefore in breach of the directors' fiduciary duty to the company. Making the loan is lawful and proper only if the directors obtain the prior approval of, or ratification by, the shareholders in general meeting.
- ii. If the loans were made at a commercial rate of interest the transaction could still be set aside no matter how fair the terms were in respect of the company (unless prior approval of/ratification by the shareholders in general meeting is obtained).

7. Under the principle laid down in Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co. [1925] Ch 407, a director must perform their duties with the care and skill reasonably expected of a person with their knowledge and experience. This is a subjective test - the less knowledgeable and less experienced directors owe a lower standard of care! Under Dorchester Finance Co. Ltd v Stebbing [1989] BCLC 498 the test was strengthened and made an objective one. A director must use the care and skill reasonably expected of an ordinary and reasonable person. Under Norman v Theodore Goddard [1991] BCLC 1027, a director must use the care and skill of a reasonably competent business person. Most modern companies legislation adopts the Dorchester Finance test. A director is only liable for breach if their actions fall below the standard expected of a 'reasonably prudent person' . The BVI BCA 2004 codifies Norman in so far as a director owes a duty to the standard of a 'reasonable director' .
8. i. You have breached the duty of care and may be held liable to the company AB Ltd, if it suffers loss as a result of your conduct (as in Dorchester Finance ) in failing to protect the company's property and failing to enquire into corporate dealings.
- ii. It is not negligent to rely on information given to you by attorneys retained by the company, provided there are no grounds to suspect that the information is not accurate. If you make the payment as requested, you may be held liable for any loss that may be suffered if the court considers the factual matrix to be suspicious. If in doubt, you ought to protect yourself from any potential liability by obtaining the prior approval of the company in general meeting by shareholders resolution - see sections 2.2.3 and 5.1.
- iii. If you comply with the request, such conduct will almost inevitably fall below the standard of skill and care expected of a prudent person. A director ought not to rely on information received from third parties unconnected to the company without independent verification of its accuracy. If the company suffers loss you become liable to pay damages. Obtain prior approval as in part ii. above.
9. It is a well-established common law principle that only the company itself may commence legal proceedings in a court of law against the errant director (the so-called 'proper plaintiff' rule in Foss v Harbottle (1843) 2 Hare 461). A minority shareholder may only bring what is referred to as a derivative action in their own name against a director if it is alleged that the director has acted fraudulently. In that case the shareholder/claimant must establish as a preliminary issue that they have proper legal standing to commence the lawsuit - the court's gatekeeper role.
10. A director may obtain lawful protection from liability for a negligent error or mistake amounting to a breach of duty (but not fraudulent or wrongful trading):
- by obtaining the prior approval of their actions from the shareholders of the company in general meeting, or a ratification thereof (section 5.1)
- through exoneration provisions in the articles, in those jurisdictions that have not outlawed them (section 5.2)
- exoneration provisions of the director's service agreement, but not the company management agreement unless the director is a party (section 5.2 and 5.3)
- by obtaining indemnity insurance (section 5.4).

11. i. A provision such as the one quoted exonerates the directors from liability to the company for negligent error or mistakes. These provisions are prohibited by companies legislation in the UK and some other common law jurisdictions such as Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man, and are therefore void and of no legal effect in those jurisdictions. The prohibition does not exist in most of the offshore common law jurisdictions, where they are presumably valid. See section 5.2.
- ii. An exoneration clause, purporting to exempt a director from liability to the company, found in a separate document such as a director's service agreement, is subject to similar statutory provisions as discussed in Part i of this question above. Furthermore, common law rules in some jurisdictions say that such clauses must be expressly brought to the attention of the company at the time the contract is entered into.
12. Directors can be made personally liable for debts incurred by a company if the directors allow a company to continue to trade when the company is no longer solvent. A company is regarded as no longer being solvent if its liabilities exceed its assets or the company is unable to meet its debts as they fall due. So, if directors know that the company will be unable to pay its suppliers at the end of the month, or that the next repayment instalment of a long-term loan cannot be paid because the company does not have sufficient funds, the directors need to consider carefully whether to allow the company to continue to operate in business, or whether they should take steps to put the company into voluntary liquidation. If the directors continue to allow the company to trade, knowing that the company cannot pay all its debts as they fall due for payment, they run the risk of being made personally liable to pay the company's debts if the company is subsequently put into liquidation. It is one of the duties of a liquidator to consider the actions of the directors in the period prior to liquidation. If the liquidator believes that the directors knowingly allowed the company to trade while it was insolvent, the liquidator has a duty to the company's creditors to bring an action against the directors personally to recover compensation for the benefit of the creditors.
13. (a) To impose liability upon a director for fraudulent trading , a creditor must establish:
5. (i) actus reus - that the director continued to trade whilst the company was insolvent
6. (ii) mens rea - that director continued to trade with intent to defraud creditors, knowing that there was no prospect of them being paid.
7. (b) To impose liability upon a director for wrongful trading, a creditor must establish:
8. (i) actus reus - that the director continued to trade whilst the company was insolvent
9. (ii) mens rea - the director knew or ought to have known that there was no prospect of creditors being paid.
14. The distinction between fraudulent trading and wrongful trading turns on the mental attitude held by the director. A director can be held liable to pay creditors of the company for fraudulent trading when they continue to operate the business whilst

it is insolvent if they subjectively appreciate that there is no hope or prospect of the creditors being paid. A director can be held liable to pay creditors of the company for wrongful trading when they continue to operate the business whilst it is insolvent in circumstances when a reasonable person would appreciate that there is no hope or prospect of the creditors being paid. See section 6.

## Answers to self-assessment questions

1. The requirements, if any, for a company to have a secretary, varies between centres. In some jurisdictions a secretary is mandatory; in others a secretary is optional - see the footnote in section 1.1 (for example, a secretary is not required for IBCs, BVI BCs or the New Manx Vehicle, but companies incorporated in these jurisdictions do require a registered agent). You should be familiar with the rules that apply in the centre where you reside. In most offshore centres there are no restrictions upon who may be appointed as secretary - any individual who is sui juris or corporate secretary may be appointed. In Jersey a company secretary must comply with qualification requirements (but only in respect of public companies). In Bermuda only natural persons who reside within the jurisdiction may be appointed.
2. It is illegal according to companies legislation based upon the 1948 Act and also contrary to traditional articles (such as art.21.1(a) of Appendix 1B) for a sole director to also serve as company secretary. Although such a situation may create some administration efficiencies, there are certain corporate governance risks associated with one person having too much control in relation to the decision-making process.
3. The leading case at the time was Barnett, Hoares &amp; Co. v South London Tramways Co. (1887) 18 QBD 815 which confirmed that the company secretary was a mere servant and required to do the bidding of the directors. However, over the years the role of the company secretary expanded and the more modern view was that the role carries with it extensive duties and responsibilities (refer to Panorama Developments (Guildford) Ltd v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics Ltd [1971] 2 QB 711). However, the position is now largely redundant for private companies according to modern legislation, such as the Companies Act 2006 enacted in England. (See section 1.3).
4. When appointed, a company secretary usually has a delegated duty imposed upon them by the board of directors to maintain the corporate registers, issue share certificates, execute documents, ensure that local statutory filing requirements are met and, if required, make representations on behalf of the company.
5. Such a requirement is an attempt to ensure that there is a person who resides locally, within the jurisdiction of incorporation, who is accountable for the actions of the company. In respect of IBCs, etc., this function is assumed by the registered agent.
6. The company secretary is usually required to send to the members notices of meetings as well as copies of minutes of meetings.
7. This will vary between centres but could include:
- an annual return (where applicable, as an annual return is not required for IBCs or Cayman exempted companies, although a declaration must be filed each year by 31 January together with the annual fee)

- notification of changes in the directors and other officers
- changes in the capital, including the allotment of shares
- copies of special resolutions that have been passed by the members, such as amendments to the constitution of the company.
8. The first secretary may be named in the articles; in which case they are appointed by the promoters/subscribers as part of the incorporation process. It is more usual, however, for the secretary to be appointed by the directors at their first meeting following incorporation. Subsequent to this, the directors usually have the power to appoint and remove the company secretary.
9. As an officer of the company, the secretary is in the same position as a director. They must therefore exercise their powers in the best interests of the company and with an appropriate level of care and skill. If they fail to discharge these duties, they can be held liable to the company. A secretary can also be held liable for fraudulent/wrongful trading.
10. Much like for directors, the liability of a secretary may be reduced if suitable provisions are included in the articles of association or if the contract appointing the secretary contains exoneration provisions intended to limit their liability. There are, however, statutory restrictions in some jurisdictions preventing an officer of a company from contracting out of liability. In all jurisdictions a secretary cannot lawfully obtain an exoneration for certain acts of default, such as wilful default, fraud or dishonesty. See module 8, section 5 of the course manual in relation to exoneration clauses generally.
11. Registered agents are required for all IBCs, such as those incorporated in the Bahamas, also for BVI BCs and the New Manx Vehicle.
12. A registered agent must:
- (a) be resident in the jurisdiction of incorporation; and
- (b) hold a proper license issued by the regulator.
13. The registered agent is selected by the promoter on incorporation, and must sign the memorandum and agree to their or its appointment.
14. Registered agents may retire by notice to the directors of the company. But if the board do not appoint a replacement, a complicated statutory procedure described in section 2.2 ensures one is appointed - essentially the outgoing agent must give notice to the registrar who must give notice to the company to appoint a new registered agent and threatening to strike the company off the register and, if such notice is not complied with, the company will be struck off.
15. The registered agent usually:
- (i) will keep and maintain the company's statutory registers and other corporate documents;
- (ii) accepts service of process;
- (iii) notifies the registrar of any changes to the board;
- (iv) keeps the common seal, if the company has one;
- (v) attests contracts and other documents on behalf of the company; and
- (vi) complies with the reasonable requests of the board. See section 2.3 of the course manual.

- d. directors' resolution.

## Module 12:

## Answers to self-assessment questions

1. Whenever creditors of a company realise that the company is in financial difficulties and may be approaching insolvency they have several alternative options beyond simply suing for recovery:
2. (i) if a creditor has security, they can appoint a receiver (section 1.1)
3. (ii) they could induce the board to propose a compromise arrangement (section 1.2)
4. (iii) they could apply to the court to appoint an administrator in certain jurisdictions where this is available, such as BVI, Guernsey and Mauritius (section 1.3).
5. 2 A fixed charge is a legal mortgage over specified assets, usually real estate, which essentially allows the company to retain possession and use of the secured property but precludes the company from dealing with such property while the mortgage subsists. A floating charge is an equitable charge on some or all of the company's present and future movable assets, which permits the company not only to retain possession and use of such assets, but also the right to sell or deal with the secured assets in the ordinary course of business. On the occurrence of an act of default (for the fixed charge) or crystallising event (for the floating charge), the secured creditor will typically appoint a receiver and manager over the secured assets.
3. The receiver will take control of the mortgaged assets (which will typically consist of all the company's real and personal property) and determine whether the business is viable. If it is, the receiver will run the business and apply any profit to pay down the debt due to the secured creditor. If the business is not viable, the receiver will sell the business as a going concern to a willing buyer, or simply sell the assets piecemeal for what they will fetch, applying the proceeds to pay down the debt due to the secured creditor. The surplus, if any, belongs to the company and is available to the ordinary unsecured creditors.
4. The usual grounds are that:
- the company is insolvent and cannot pay its debts
- the court believes it would be just and equitable to wind the company up.
5. The typical procedure is:
11. (a) a creditor applies to court, citing one of the statutory grounds, usually that the debtor company is insolvent
12. (b) an early hearing takes place where, unless the company can prove it is solvent, the court makes a winding-up order and appoints a liquidator
13. (c) the powers of the directors' cease, and the liquidator assumes control of the assets of the company

- (d) the liquidator collects debts due to the company, sells its assets and puts the proceeds into a single pot
- (e) the liquidator asks creditors to prove their debts
- (f ) a statement of affairs is prepared, and the liquidator pays corporates debts according to a statutory order set out in section 4 of the study manual, with claims in the same category abating pari passu (being paid x cents per dollar owed)
- (g) final accounts are drawn and submitted to the court with a request for the court to issue of a certificate of dissolution.
6. The usual reasons for a members' voluntary winding-up are that:
- the articles specify that the company shall be wound up upon the expiry of a certain time or on the happening of an event and the event occurs
- the company is no longer needed and the shareholders want their capital to be returned to them.
7. a. Compromise arrangement with creditors in accordance with ss.14-45 of the BVI BCA (per section 1.2 of the course manual)
- b. Administration in accordance with ss.76-114 of the BVI Insolvency Act 2003 as amended (per section 1.3)
- c. Voluntary winding-up in accordance with ss.197-208 of the BVI BCA (per section 3.1)
8. The procedure is described in section 3.1. It involves:
- (a) the directors making a declaration that the company is solvent
- (b) the shareholders passing a (special) resolution that the company be wound-up and appointing a liquidator
- (c) the liquidator collects debts due to the company, sells its assets and puts the proceeds into a single pot
- (d) the liquidator advertises for creditors and asks those creditors to prove their debts
- (e) the liquidator calls a meeting of creditors, unless dispensed with, and pays creditors the amount due to them, and any surplus to the shareholders
- (f ) a final statement of account is drawn and submitted to the registrar with a request to dissolve the company.
9. a. If the creditor has security, they can enforce a fixed or a floating charge by appointing a receiver who will exercise a power of sale over the mortgaged asset (section 1.1).
- b. If the creditor does not have security they have three options. Firstly, although a creditor cannot propose a compromise arrangement, they can induce the board to do so - unlikely in BVI because of the administration procedure described in (c) below (section 1.2).
- c. Secondly, an unsecured creditor can apply to the court for an Administration Order and for the judge to appoint an administrator under Pt 3, ss.76-114 of the BVI Insolvency Act 2003 (per section 1.3).
- d. Thirdly, an unsecured creditor can commence compulsory winding-up proceedings (section 2).

10. The usual grounds for a creditors' voluntary winding-up are that the company is insolvent and the members (and directors) wish to cease business and dissolve the company.
11. The typical procedure for a creditors' voluntary winding-up is described in section 3.2. The main difference to a members' voluntary winding-up is that the creditors select the liquidator at a preliminary meeting of the company's creditors.
12. A liquidator must comply with the duties described in section 2.4, namely:
- to review the affairs of the company and, if necessary, bring a lawsuit against errant directors (in particular those engaged in fraudulent or wrongful trading) in order to 'swell' the assets available to pay the creditors
- to collect debts due to the company and take assets of the company under their control and sell them and convert them to money
- to pay creditors of the company according to the statutory order
- to conclude the winding up by preparing accounts, convene final meetings, and make the necessary filings to formally dissolve the company.
13. The cause is generally failure to make filings, pay annual fees or respond to letters that have been sent by the Registrar of Companies, giving rise to a suspicion that a company is defunct and has ceased to carry on business. The process involves the registrar serving notice of their intention to strike off. If the default is not remedied, the registrar removes the company from the register of companies. Any assets held by the company when struck-off the register automatically vest in the government of the state of incorporation. See section 6.1.
14. A voluntary striking off is less formal, less time consuming and less expensive than a voluntary winding up, which involves the appointment of an independent professional liquidator who must follow statutory procedures. A voluntary striking off is effected by the directors of the company and is only permitted when a company is in a 'zero state' , with no assets and no debts or outstanding liabilities.
15. The procedure is described in section 6.2:
11. (a) the board closes the company's business, liquidates its assets and pays all of its debts
12. (b) when satisfied the company has reached a 'zero-state' the directors apply to the registrar on a prescribed form supported by an affidavit asking for the company to be struck-off
13. (c) the registrar publishes details of the proposed strike-off in the Gazette
14. (d) if no objections are received within a prescribed period, the registrar with strike the company off the register and issues a certificate of voluntary striking off and the company is dissolved.
16. A company that has been compulsorily struck off the register can be reinstated to it upon an application being made by a former director, member or creditor of the company. The procedure may involve an application to the court, which is a judicial process, or an application to the registrar, which is an administrative process. The court or the registrar, depending upon the jurisdiction, will readily reinstate the company provided it pays all its outstanding governmental fees plus interest. See section 6.3.
