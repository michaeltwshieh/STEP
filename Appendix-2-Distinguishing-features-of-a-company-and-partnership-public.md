# Appendix 2: Distinguishing features of a company and partnership, public and private companies

## Appendix 2:

## Distinguishing features of a company and partnership, public and private companies

## 1. Company compared to a partnership

The essential differences between a company and a partnership are as follows.

- Unlike a company, a partnership is not a separate legal entity.
- A partnership is usually created by a private contract whereas a company has to be created by legal process (called incorporation) prescribed by statute.
- Partners share the profits generated by the partnership equally (unless the partnership agreement provides otherwise) and are jointly and severally liable for the partnership's debts. In a company the profits of the company may be retained and ploughed back into the business or distributed. If distributed the distributable profits are shared between the members according to the number and type of shares owned. The liability of shareholders for the debts of a company is limited; it only extends to the amount (if any) that they agree to pay for the shares they have received when issued. The company is liable in full for its own debts.
- A partnership is automatically dissolved following the death or retirement of one of the partners whereas a company has perpetual succession and its continuation is not affected by the death or retirement of a member or by the transfer of member's shares.
- Partnership property is vested in the names of the partners. In a company the property is vested in the name of the company (not in the name of the members or officers).
- The partners usually manage the business of the partnership as agents and generally have equal rights of management. In a company the management is exercised by the board of directors.
- A partnership often has limited borrowing powers whereas a company has unlimited powers to raise capital from the public to finance its business. A knock-on effect of this is that there are usually no restrictions on the withdrawal of capital from a partnership but in a company there are specific restrictions and requirements relating to the distribution of capital (the so-called capital maintenance doctrine, reviewed in detail in Module 6).
- Income tax is often imposed on the profits of the partners of a partnership and not on the partnership itself. Corporation tax on the profits of a company is usually imposed upon the entity itself and not on the members or officers of the company.

In this preliminary analysis we have distinguished companies and partnerships generally.

Before we leave the subject of partnerships, a brief word should be included about limited partnerships. In recent years a new form of partnership has developed, mainly in offshore centres, which provides an element of limited liability. Limited partnerships are unincorporated partnerships that generally comprise two types of partners.

- General partners: the general partners manage the partnership business and their liability for the debts of the partnership is unlimited (in the usual way).
- Limited partners: the limited partners take a passive role in the management of the partnership. Their liability is limited to the amount set out in the partnership agreement.

A limited partnership is usually treated as a separate entity in the centre where it is created, although it retains an element of tax transparency that can create possible financial planning benefits.

Limited partnerships are used as collective investment vehicles and as a quasi trust, where the limited partners assume a role similar to that of beneficiaries and the general partners have powers and duties similar to those of a trustee.

A limited liability partnership ('LLP') is a further variation on the theme of a general partnership. The liability of the partners is limited to the assets of the partnership, which means that the individual partners are not liable for the debts of the LLP .

## 2. Public compared to a private company

The essential differences between a public company and a private company relate to ownership and management.

## a. Ownership

In general terms a public company is one whose shares are offered for sale to the general public. There is no restriction on ownership. Public companies are often listed on a recognised stock exchange and their shares are freely traded. In view of this, certain regulatory and accounting requirements have to be fulfilled by public companies in order to provide an element of investor and shareholder protection.

Private companies, on the other hand, are companies that impose a restriction on share ownership and, in general terms, ownership is not made available to the general public. There is often a restriction upon a shareholder's freedom to transfer his shares. There is therefore no ready market for the shares of a private company.

As a result the regulations and requirements imposed on private companies are not as onerous as those imposed on public companies.

## b. Management and control

A public company is often very large. It has a significant number of shareholders, but they do not run the business. This is undertaken by a full time board of executive directors and sometimes also part time non-executive directors).

In contrast, a private company is much smaller and the directors and shareholders are often the same persons.

The vast majority of companies incorporated in offshore financial centres are private companies. These provide considerable financial planning opportunities for international clients. Offshore public companies do exist but they are not widely used. Corporate issues relating to public companies are probably best handled by attorneys.
