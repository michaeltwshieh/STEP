1.3

The starting position is that proper notice must be given to every person entitled to receive it. A failure that is not covered by the articles can invalidate the meeting and the business transacted at it. The result is particularly serious here because the notice concerns special business, and Article 11.1 requires the place, day, hour and general nature of that business to be stated.

There is an express qualification. Article 11.3 provides that an accidental omission to give notice to, or the non-receipt of notice by, any member does not invalidate the proceedings. That protection should not be treated as permission to omit C, D, E, A or B deliberately. If the shorter notice route is used without the consent of all members entitled to receive notice, the protection in Article 11.2 is absent. A member who attends in person or by proxy will ordinarily be treated as having received notice, but the safer course is to retain proof of service and any consent to short notice.

1.4

C cannot transact business at the meeting on 28 August. Article 12.2 requires two shareholders personally present, unless the Company has a sole shareholder. C is only one of the three registered shareholders, so the meeting is inquorate even though C holds 70 per cent of the issued share capital. No vote can cure the absence of a quorum.

Because the meeting was convened by the directors rather than on a members' requisition, Article 12.3 requires it to stand adjourned to the same day in the next week, at the same time and place. If a quorum is still absent within half an hour at that adjourned meeting, the members present then constitute the quorum. C could therefore proceed alone at the adjourned meeting.

At that adjourned meeting the safer course is for C to demand a poll under Article 12.6. She holds more than the required 15 per cent of the paid-up capital, and Article 13.1 gives her 70 votes on a poll. The two-thirds threshold for a special resolution in Article 1 is the lesser super-majority permitted by the question, so C's 70 per cent would pass the resolution. The answer is therefore no on 28 August, but yes at the properly adjourned meeting, subject to the notice and poll being dealt with correctly.

1.5

The three shareholders are present, so Article 12.2 is satisfied. On a show of hands Article 13.1 gives one vote to each member present in person. C votes for the resolution and D and E vote against it, giving one vote in favour and two against. The motion is therefore lost on the show of hands. The chairman has no casting vote because there is no equality of votes.

C should demand a poll before, or on, the chairman's declaration of the result. Article 12.6 permits a poll to be demanded by one member holding at least 15 per cent of the paid-up capital, and C holds 70 per cent. Article 12.8 then treats the poll result as the resolution of the meeting. On the poll C has 70 votes and D and E together have 30 votes. C therefore obtains more than the two-thirds super-majority in Article 1, and the resolution passes.

3.1

The structure of Company Ltd shows the usual split in a compact form: International Trust Company holds the shares, while the board consists of the trust officer and Ms Avril Nath. In the usual corporate model the members supply capital, appoint the directors and use general meetings to supervise them. The board then acts collectively as the company's management organ. It decides how the business is run, employs the company's capital and assets and enters contracts for the company, subject to matters reserved to the members by legislation or the articles.

The arrangement grew out of shortcomings in earlier business forms. A sole trader owned the business and made all decisions, but bore its debts without limit and could raise capital only from personal wealth. A partnership spread risk and provided more capital, but property and decision-making remained tied to the partners. The joint-stock company offered a way to gather contributions from many investors and to hold property and contracts in the company's own name.

The Joint Stock Companies Act 1844 made registration, separate personality, public share capital and transferability workable without a Royal Charter or private Act of Parliament. The Limited Liability Act 1855 then protected investors from open-ended liability, subject to safeguards such as public information and capital maintenance. The Companies Act 1907 recognised the private company, where a small body of owners could use a less burdensome structure and could often dispense with formal meetings by written resolution. These changes made it practical for capitalists to invest without running the enterprise themselves.

The separation therefore served several concrete purposes. Directors could bring specialist knowledge to a railway, manufacturing or other enterprise that numerous investors could not manage from a general meeting. Share ownership could change without interrupting management. The company could raise capital from a broad investing public, while limited liability made that investment less risky and more attractive. A shareholder unhappy with management could vote to change the board or sell the shares, rather than attempt to run the business personally.

The model also supplied supervision. Members could alter the constitution, appoint or remove directors and decide major matters such as a voluntary winding-up. Directors owed the company fiduciary duties and a duty of care, so the authority given to them was accompanied by accountability. For a private asset-holding company such as Company Ltd, where International Trust Company controls the shares and the board is small, ownership and control can sit close together. The historical reasons for the division remain the explanation for the legal structure, although the practical administration is less like a widely held public company.

3.2

Article 19.1 gives the directors of Company Ltd the general management power. They may exercise all powers of the Company except powers which the Companies Legislation or the articles require the Company in general meeting to exercise. The article is in the form of the old Article 80 of Table A under the Companies Act 1948.

That wording matters. It gives the board the power to decide ordinary management questions and does not permit the members to replace a board decision with a contrary instruction merely by passing a resolution. In Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon [1909] 1 Ch 311, the court treated a power expressly given to directors by the articles as a power for the directors to exercise; the shareholders could control the position by altering the articles, but could not usurp the power with an ad hoc direction.

The members still retain the matters which Article 19.1 leaves to the general meeting. These include constitutional alterations, capital decisions, appointment and removal of directors and the voluntary winding-up of the Company, subject to the particular legislation and articles. Article 19.3 is a direct example of a qualification: the directors may borrow and mortgage property, but a borrowing above the stated $1 million limit needs the previous sanction of an ordinary member resolution. Article 19.2 also permits the directors to appoint an agent, but that is a delegation by the board, not a transfer of the whole management power to the shareholder.

Article 19.1 would have a different practical effect if it contained the later Table A Article 70 or the 2008 model wording, under which a special resolution could direct the board. It contains no such direction power. International Trust Company can therefore use its member powers and can seek an alteration of the articles, but it cannot direct the board to manage Company Ltd in a particular way under the present Article 19.1 alone.

3.3

International Trust Company is the shareholder holding the shares according to the Redhouse Trust, but that does not make the trust or the trustee the owner of the Company's assets. Company Ltd is a separate legal person. Its worldwide assets and its Midcity Bank account belong to Company Ltd, and the trustee's rights arise through its shareholding and the trust arrangements. The result follows the separate-personality rule in Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22.

As registered member, International Trust Company can vote on member decisions, alter the constitution where the required majority is available, and appoint or remove directors under the articles. It can therefore affect who controls the board. Article 19.1 still leaves day-to-day management with the directors. The trust officer and Ms Avril Nath must decide matters as directors of Company Ltd, not as uncritical delegates of the trustee. Section 109 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 expresses the same management starting point for a BVI company.

The dual role creates a real duty question. A director owes the fiduciary duty and duty of care to Company Ltd. Under sections 120 and 122 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004, the director must act honestly and in good faith in what the director believes to be the Company's best interests, and must exercise the care, diligence and skill of a reasonable director in the circumstances. A direction that protects the trust but prejudices Company Ltd cannot be followed simply because the trustee controls the shares. The board should ask for the relevant terms, financial effect and authority, record its decision and refuse a transaction that is dishonest, improper or careless.

The board can delegate a defined task under Article 19.2, and a passive asset-holding company will often use a corporate service provider, bank signatories or investment advisers. A narrow special power for a specified transaction is safer than a general power over Company Ltd's affairs. Giving the beneficial owner or trustee unrestricted control of the bank account or a general power of attorney can shift management and control to the client, create tax-residence difficulties and expose the directors to liability for failing to protect corporate assets. The service provider must also keep the corporate records and comply with applicable anti-money laundering and counter-financing requirements.

There is no separate category of nominee director that permits the trust officer to escape those duties. The fact that International Trust Company owns the shares for the Redhouse Trust, and that the directors administer assets for that trust, explains the commercial setting but does not change the legal actor in a board decision. The board remains responsible for the Company's account, its assets and the effect of its decisions.

3.4

Article 15.3 permits Company Ltd to remove a director before the end of the director's period of office by ordinary resolution, notwithstanding any agreement between the Company and that director. No cause or reason has to be shown under that article. International Trust Company, as the registered member, can exercise that power through a properly convened general meeting or through the written-resolution procedure in Article 12.11. Since it is the sole member, its signed written ordinary resolution is the practical route, provided the decision is recorded and any required notice or filing is dealt with.

Article 15.4 permits the Company, by ordinary resolution, to appoint another person in place of the director removed under Article 15.3. The replacement serves subject to retirement at the same time as if he or she had become a director on the day on which the removed director was last elected. The proposed replacement's written consent should be obtained before the resolution is passed. The Company's register of directors should then be updated, the change notified to the Registrar where required and a directors' service agreement considered.

The removal of one of two directors also affects the board's ability to act. Article 20.4 fixes the ordinary board quorum at two unless the number of directors has been fixed at one. After removal, Ms Nath is below that quorum. Under Article 20.8 the continuing director may act to increase the number of directors to the quorum or to summon a general meeting, but not to conduct unrelated business while the board remains below quorum. The member's ordinary resolution appointing the replacement is therefore the safer method. If the member meeting route is used, Article 12.2 treats one member as a quorum where the Company has a sole shareholder; alternatively Article 12.11 avoids a meeting altogether.

The power in Article 15.3 is distinct from a court disqualification or a compulsory vacation of office caused by bankruptcy, incapacity or another specified event. Those events may end office under legislation or the articles without a member's removal resolution, but they are not needed on the facts given. The proposed removal is an ordinary member decision under Article 15.3.

4

A director appointed for a fee receives a contractual reward, but the office also places the director between the Company's assets, its members and the people who deal with it. The fee does not change the director's fiduciary position. A director owes duties to the Company as a whole, and some duties continue after departure, including the restriction on using confidential information acquired through office.

The first exposure is misuse of the management power. The director must act honestly, for the Company's interests and for a proper purpose. Hutton v West Cork Railway Co (1883) 23 Ch D 654 distinguishes expenditure reasonably related to the Company's business from a personal or irrational use of company money. Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver [1942] 1 All ER 378 required directors to account for profit diverted to themselves when a corporate opportunity was placed in front of the company. Lee Panavision Ltd v Lee Lighting Ltd [1992] BCLC 22 and Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi [2005] 2 BCLC 91 show the same risk where a director favours an associated business or tries to take a company customer personally.

The sensible protection is to keep the purpose of each decision in the board papers, identify the benefit to the Company and refuse a use of corporate money that is really for the director, a family member, an associate or a client. If a director may profit from a transaction, a corporate opportunity or information, full disclosure and informed approval should be obtained before acting. A director must not accept a gift or other benefit from a third party because it may distort the decision even if the payer cannot be shown to have intended a bribe.

Self-dealing and other conflicts create a separate exposure. A sale to or purchase from a director can be set aside even when the terms appear fair. The director should disclose the interest fully in writing, leave the vote to a disinterested quorum or obtain prior member approval where that is the governing route. The traditional Article 18.2 form allows a director to contract with the Company only after disclosure and without voting on the transaction. A failure to disclose can lead to rescission, an account of profit and compensation.

The second broad problem is care, skill and supervision. Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co [1925] Ch 407 applied a forgiving standard to inexperienced non-executive directors. Dorchester Finance Co Ltd v Stebbing [1989] BCLC 498 and Norman v Theodore Goddard [1991] BCLC 1027 state the modern objective expectation more firmly. Signing blank cheques, approving an unread insurance form or accepting an unexplained transaction without enquiry is unsafe. A director may rely on a reliable employee, professional adviser or fellow director when the circumstances give no reason for suspicion, but must make the proper enquiry where suspicion is indicated.

Re Barings plc (No.5) [2000] 1 BCLC 523 treated monitoring as part of the director's responsibility even where functions had been delegated. Lexi Holdings plc (In Administration) v Luqman [2009] EWCA Civ 117 refused reliance on accounts that should have raised suspicion, whereas Madoff Securities International Ltd (In Liquidation) v Raven [2013] EWHC 3147 (Comm) allowed reliance by some directors but not those who knew more and formed the inner circle. Bishopsgate Investment Management Ltd v Maxwell (No.2) [1994] 1 All ER 261 is a warning against signing documents presented by a powerful third party without reading or questioning them. The reduction in exposure is practical: read the papers, obtain advice, supervise delegated work, record reasons and keep proper financial and corporate records. An honest informed business judgement that later loses money is not by itself a breach.

Managed-company directorships carry a further problem. There is no legal defence in saying that the service provider was only a nominee or was following the beneficial owner's instructions. The director must refuse a direction that is unlawful, dishonest or contrary to the Company's interests. A narrow power of attorney for a named transaction, controlled bank signing and documented shareholder approval are safer than a general power over the Company's affairs. The provider must also be alert to tax-residence and sham risks created by transferring management and control to the client, and to criminal exposure under anti-money laundering and counter-financing rules.

Insolvency creates personal exposure to creditors. When a company fails, the liquidator can investigate whether the directors continued to incur debts while there was no realistic prospect of creditors being paid. Section 332 of the Companies Act 1948 addresses fraudulent trading where the business was carried on with intent to defraud creditors. Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 introduced the objective wrongful-trading test in England. The course materials record that offshore adoption differs, so a director must check the law of the Company's jurisdiction, monitor cash flow and take advice or stop trading when the prospect of payment has disappeared. Article 29-type exoneration wording cannot protect fraud, dishonesty or wilful default.

There are lawful ways to reduce financial exposure, but each has limits. A director can make full disclosure and obtain prior approval or later ratification by the members for a conflict, an unauthorised benefit or negligent management. The majority cannot ratify a dishonest expropriation of Company property to the prejudice of minority members or creditors. A director can also negotiate an exoneration and indemnity in the articles and a directors' service agreement where the jurisdiction permits it. The course distinguishes the Bahamas, BVI and Bermuda, where negligence protection is generally permitted subject to fraud and dishonesty, from England, where section 232 of the Companies Act 2006 restricts such clauses.

An indemnity in a company management agreement can protect the service provider against some third-party liability, but it does not automatically protect the director or prevent the claimant from suing. Rae v Meek (1889) 14 App Cas 558, West v Lazard Brothers [1993] JLR 165 and Roywest Trust Corp (Bahamas) Ltd v Savannah NV (Unreported 22 July 1987) illustrate why an exclusion must be narrowly drawn, brought to the client's attention and accepted. Directors' and officers' insurance is another protection for good-faith negligent or inadvertent liability. It does not cover a deliberate default, fraud or dishonesty. Re Barings plc (No.5) also treated remuneration as relevant to the degree of responsibility, so the fee cannot be used to treat the appointment as nominal.

The risks are real, but they are capable of being managed by disciplined decision-making. The director who reads the documents, keeps independence from the client, discloses conflicts, monitors solvency and arranges limited contractual and insurance protection is in the best position to retain the reward without taking an unmanaged exposure. Fraud, dishonesty and a failure to enquire remain personal risks that an indemnity or policy cannot remove.

The assets total $2,605,000: Blackacre $1,700,000, artwork $550,000, cash $40,000, securities $310,000 and the $5,000 receivable from PB Oil Co. A members' voluntary winding-up is the solvent route. The directors first make a declaration, after a full enquiry, that the company can pay its debts in full within 12 months from commencement and attach an assets and liabilities statement. The members then pass the required resolution to wind up the company and usually appoint the liquidator in the same resolution. The liquidator collects the assets, pays creditors, calls any required members' meetings, lays a final account and seeks dissolution.

That declaration cannot honestly be made for Company. The asset total is below the liabilities before liquidation costs, and the Mortgagee's shortfall alone is $700,000. A creditors' voluntary winding-up is the appropriate route. The members first resolve to wind up, but the directors do not sign a solvency declaration. A meeting of creditors is then convened. The directors present the Company's assets and liabilities, and the creditors choose the liquidator by the prescribed vote in person or by proxy. The liquidator thereafter takes control of the winding-up, realises the assets and distributes the proceeds in the statutory order, with meetings and a final account following the course applicable to a creditors' winding-up.

On the figures given, Blackacre's $1,700,000 realisation is applied to the Mortgagee's fixed charge. The remaining $700,000 Mortgagee claim ranks with the ordinary unsecured claims on the course model. The liquidator's $40,000 costs are paid ahead of ordinary unsecured claims, the $55,000 tax liability is a preferential government claim and the remaining ordinary creditors share the balance pari passu. Company should therefore use the creditors' voluntary process. Under the course's Companies Act 1948 model, sections 317 and 319 put the fixed-charge claim, liquidation costs, preferential claims and ordinary unsecured claims in the order used below.
