## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- explain the role, duties and rights of a promoter
- raise awareness of pre-incorporation contracts and relevant modern offshore companies legislation
- review the theoretical and practical aspects of incorporation of a private limited company
- introduce the skill of drafting minutes

## Introduction

Each offshore centre has its own requirements governing, regulations affecting, and procedures connected with, the incorporation of companies that are permitted to exist and carry on business in or from their jurisdiction. Rather than attempt to cover the exact incorporation or registration process that applies in each of the main centres, we have included a summary of the basic steps. You are expected to obtain a working knowledge of the incorporation process that applies in the jurisdiction where you work or have some other connection and of the regulatory corporate requirements affecting companies that transact business from that jurisdiction.

In addition to incorporation, we will review the following matters:

- the role, rights and duties of promoters
- pre-incorporation contracts that promoters may seek to create before a company is formed
- the process of incorporation
- practical matters following incorporation
- registration and migration of foreign companies.

## 1. Promoters: their role in the traditional incorporation process, their duties and rights

Upon incorporation, a company takes on an independent legal personality. Before incorporation, the company does not exist. Someone must get the company off the ground. This is the role of the promoter.

Companies legislation in the UK, and elsewhere in the common law jurisdictions, does not provide a precise definition of a promoter. The promoter has been described as 'the midwife of the birth of the business' , 27  and the person who 'undertakes to form a company with reference to a given project and to set it going and who undertakes the necessary steps to accomplish that purpose' . 28 As we shall see, however, this definition, is largely applicable to onshore, rather than offshore, company management practice.

## 1.1 Promoters: definition and description

A promoter is the person who, onshore at least, forms the business plan, arranges the initial capital, lines up professionals to incorporate and set up the undertaking, organises the initial business structure and negotiates preliminary contracts, etc. This person helps create the company and then often sells it something (such as an existing business, capital assets, investments and/or services) in exchange for the issue of shares or other securities.

The role of the promoter has an interesting place in the evolution of companies. In the 19th century there were some spectacular abuses of the position, caused principally by the ability of a promoter to obtain, from the new company, a consideration far greater than the value of the thing transferred to it.

A person who promotes a private company (for instance, in order to take over their own unincorporated business, intending to continue to run the business in more or less the same manner but as an incorporated entity, and to act as its director and majority shareholder) is unlikely to defraud the company at this initial stage. It would amount to defrauding themselves. On the other hand, a professional promoter who intends to sell their shares to third parties soon after incorporation clearly does have an opportunity to defraud investors, as we will illustrate.

Professional promoters of public companies in 19th-century England were often reputable businessmen. Some were not, however, such as one colourfully described by Gower 29 as:

'[a] character of dubious repute and antecedents, who infest the commercial demi-monde with a menagerie of bulls, bears, stags and sharks as his familiars, and who, often rising to affluence by preying on the susceptibilities of a gullible public, finally retires from the scene in a blaze of sensational suicide or Old Bailey trial. (It is perhaps a tribute to the law that we definitely picture [the rogue promoter] as coming to a sticky end).'

Nowadays, very few companies, if any, seek investment from the public before the company has been formed. Public companies start off as private companies and 'go public' having traded successfully for some years and built up the business. The first step is to ensure that the company is a public company. Then if the company wants to have its shares listed on an onshore stock exchange, complex and, stringent investorprotection and stock exchange regulations have to be complied with. For this reason the vast majority of onshore IPOs are undertaken by reputable investment banks, and the traditional, individual, 19th-century-style promoter has disappeared. Accordingly, there is nowadays little, if any, abuse of the position of promoter. The public is protected by comprehensive regulation and the integrity of reputable professionals.

## 1.2 Promoters: their offshore role

Who fulfils the role of the promoter in the offshore environment? Where does the promoter fit into the offshore incorporation process of a private limited company?

The promoter, you will recall, undertakes the steps that are necessary to form a company, and immediately after incorporation assists or facilitates the company in obtaining capital and funding. The role of promoter is not occupied by those professionals who act in a purely administrative capacity, such as lawyers, accountants or corporate service providers.

Accordingly, for the purposes of our nomenclature, the promoter is the pre-incorporation name given to the beneficial owner of the shares of the company. In the offshore world, where public companies are rare, we do not have to concern ourselves with professional promoters. The promoter is the client, upon whose instructions the private company is formed.

## 1.3 Duties of promoters

Owing to the possibility of abuse referred to in section 1.1, certain aspects of company law evolved in England in the 19th century to protect potential victims against the actions of a rogue promoter. This was not something carried out by legislation. Instead, the common law imposed a fiduciary duty on the promoter, thereby affording a victim a remedy in law for breach. This fiduciary duty is impliedly owed by every promoter to the company when formed. In effect it means that a promoter must not make a profit out of the incorporation process without disclosing it to the company.

The duty to refrain from making a 'secret profit' is illustrated by the facts of Erlanger v New Sombrero Phosphate Co. 30  A, heading a syndicate comprising A-Z, acquired the right to mine phosphate deposits in the Caribbean at a cost of £55,000. A then formed a company on behalf of the syndicate and the right to mine phosphate was sold to it for £110,000. A thereby made a secret profit of £55,000.

The case of Erlanger confirmed that an owner of property may, as promoter, incorporate a company and sell property to it, but one who does so must disclose any profits. The court in Erlanger said that A should have ensured that the company, or a disinterested quorum of the board of directors, were made aware of the terms of A's purchase of the property and subsequent sale thereof to the company and thus were aware of any profit made by him as the promoter of the company.

Where independent directors do not exist to satisfy the requirement of a disinterested quorum, the promoter must make full and frank disclosure of any profit they make to the members of the company. A should have disclosed his profit of £55,000 to the shareholders of the company, namely B through Z. They could then make an informed decision as to the bona fides of the deal.

Disclosure to the members of the company may still not offer protection to third parties if the promoter is the initial majority shareholder of the company but intends to sell his or her shares at the first opportunity or intends to 'go public' imminently and invite investment from the public. This is exactly what happened in Gluckstein v Barnes 31  where A, B, C and D bought the Olympia exhibition hall for £140,000. They then incorporated a company (in which they each held 25% of the shares) to which they sold the property for £180,000. Thereafter they invited the public to buy shares in the company to own and manage the exhibition hall without fully disclosing their 'secret profit' . It was held that a promoter who makes a profit upon a sale of assets to the company must disclose that profit to the company by making a full and frank report to an independent board of directors, or to existing and potential shareholders (for instance, in the prospectus) so that persons who are contemplating becoming members are fully aware of the terms of the transaction between the company and the promoter.

So, for example, a promoter who, in the process of setting up a company, buys land for $1 million on its behalf, may only sell that land to the company for $1 million. To sell it for $1.5 million, the promoter must disclose the profit to a disinterested quorum of the directors or to the existing shareholders and any potential members of the company. If the promoter fails to make full and frank disclosure, the company may (by resolution of its shareholders):

- (i) rescind the contract that gave rise to the profit and recover the purchase price paid ($1.5 million) and restore the property (the land) to the promoter; or
- (ii) compel the promoter to account to the company for the profit, and turn over the $0.5m to the company; or
- (iii) sue the promoter for compensation for breaching the common law fiduciary duty, or for misrepresentation. 32

Finally, we ought to put issues relating to promoters into perspective. These issues are of clear historical interest in the onshore world of commerce where public companies proliferate. Promoter issues are not common, however, in relation to private companies incorporated as investment vehicles on behalf of a single person or small group of HNWIs.

The promotion and incorporation of offshore private companies is usually undertaken by the same persons who become the registered shareholders or the beneficial owners of the shares (or are the trustees of a trust for which the company serves as the underlying asset-holding company). Accordingly, there is little scope for fraud, and indeed little point, as the promoters would, in effect, be doing no more than defrauding themselves. Even so, the opportunity for abuse, and therefore potential problems, may arise where promoters either do not take shares in the company (in their own names or through a nominee on their behalf), or sell their shares shortly after incorporation to third parties.

In such circumstances a situation may arise whereby the promoters must be advised to disclose details of any profit made by them in the incorporation process.

Prima facie , a promoter is not entitled to be paid a fee or be reimbursed for expenses unless the company has agreed to pay. As a company cannot enter into a binding agreement until incorporated, it is difficult to envisage any situation whereby it would be conceivably possible for a company to enter into an agreement to reimburse or remunerate a promoter (a director yes, but not a promoter).

In practice, however, the reimbursement of fees and expenses is usually covered in the articles. A typical provision, 33  such as the one below, authorises the directors to repay expenses properly incurred by the promoter in incorporating the company.

'The business of the company shall be managed by the directors, who may pay all expenses properly incurred in promoting and registering the company ... .'

An actual fee or remuneration paid to a promoter is unusual. Nowadays, in the onshore environment, promoters are often compensated for their efforts by being given options, entitling them to purchase shares in the public or private companies that they has been instrumental in forming, at a stated price and within a specified time - if the shares have, in that time, gone up in value the option constitutes a recompense for the initial services of the promoters.

## 2. Pre-incorporation contracts

In certain circumstances a promoter may purport to enter into a contract on behalf of a company before the company has been incorporated. These contracts are often referred to as pre-incorporation contracts.

This may occur, for instance, where the promoter is concerned that a delay in signing the contract may jeopardise the proposed deal, or may not have been aware that the company had not yet been incorporated; or it may be desirable (for lawful or, unfortunately, unlawful reasons) for a contract to be back-dated or to bear a specific date.

## 2.1 The position at common law

According to common law principles, until it has been incorporated, a company cannot enter into any binding agreement. It cannot, therefore, enforce a pre-incorporation contract, nor can it be held liable thereunder. A pre-incorporation contract is unenforceable by or against the company.

Furthermore, at common law, a company cannot, after its incorporation, ratify a contract made on its behalf before the date of incorporation.

So, for example, in the case of Kelner v Baxter , 34  A, B and C decided to incorporate a company in order to acquire a hotel. Before the company was incorporated A, B and C negotiated a contract to buy a substantial amount of wine to stock the cellar, and executed the contract 'on behalf of [proposed name of company] by A, B and C' . The company was later formed, the wine delivered to the hotel and most of it was consumed by guests. Unfortunately, the company soon went into liquidation with the price of the wine still outstanding. It was held by the court:

- that the company was not liable to pay for the wine as it had been ordered pursuant to a pre-incorporation contract which was not enforceable against the company
- that the company could not, after incorporation, ratify the agreement and thus become liable under its terms
- that A, B and C were personally liable under the contract in these circumstances, not the company.

Accordingly, at common law, a promoter who cannot wait for the incorporation process to be completed is well advised either:

- simply to seek a letter of intent or non-binding agreement from the other contracting party or
- to enter into the agreement in a personal capacity, and rely upon the fact that the company and the other contracting party will, after incorporation, enter into a new contract in similar terms and discharge the promoter from the original liability. This is called 'novation' , whereby the original contracting parties and the new company agree to enter into a completely new contract. The consent of all parties, especially the original contracting parties, is therefore required for a novation.

## 2.2 The position under modern statutory provisions

The provisions set out above represent the position at common law. The companies legislation of a number of offshore jurisdictions, however, now contains modern provisions, whereby a newly formed company may adopt a pre-incorporation contract by way of ratification, without the need to obtain the consent of the other contracting party.

Accordingly, on the facts of Kelner v Baxter , the new company, upon adopting the contract entered into on its behalf by A, B and C, would become liable under its terms to pay for the wine, rather than A, B and C in their personal capacities. A novation (or new contract) requiring the consent of the other contracting party (the supplier of the wine) is not necessary before the company becomes liable.

The statutory provisions may also state that, following the adoption of a pre-incorporation contract by the newly incorporated company, the personal liability of the promoter (A, B and C, who originally entered into the contract on behalf of the company) ceases.

Whether a newly formed company adopts a pre-incorporation contract is a matter for the company's board of directors to decide. A simple board resolution to do so is sufficient, similar to the one illustrated in Appendix 4 of the online Study Resources.

## 3. Process of incorporation

The legal registration process required to incorporate a company may vary slightly from jurisdiction to jurisdiction, but any variations are to a common theme.

We cannot summarise the incorporation procedures that apply in every major offshore centre, but we are able to outline the broad steps required. We shall leave it to you to acquire a detailed knowledge of the process of incorporation applicable to the jurisdiction where you work. It is also important to have an understanding of the process required in the jurisdiction where most of the companies formed on behalf of your clients are incorporated (if different).

## 3.1 Take instructions

A corporate service provider must initially take instructions to incorporate a company from the client (the promoter) or the client's advisers. This may involve a face-to-face meeting, or instructions may be received in writing or by phone. The purpose of the initial 'meeting' is to obtain all the requisite information at this early stage to incorporate the company and to comply with anti money laundering (AML), Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) and Common Reporting Standard (CRS) due diligence.

## 3.1.1  Corporate information

The service provider must obtain full and detailed particulars relating to the proposed company. This often involves completing a 'New Company Questionnaire' (basically an aide-memoire to ensure that you obtain all the necessary information to enable you to proceed). It is also an opportunity to give preliminary advice about basic corporate principles and to agree fees.

## 3.1.2  AML requirements

Applicable AML legislation imposes new statutory duties upon service providers to carry out proper due diligence and establish (a) the identity and bona fides of the client; (b) the provenance of the funds introduced to the structure; and (c) the purpose for which the company is to be used and anticipated activity. 36

## 3.1.3  FATCA requirements

FATCA is a US Act of Congress that applies and is given statutory effect in non-US jurisdictions, including the common law financial centres, by way of a series of inter-governmental agreements. These FATCA Regulations require foreign (non-US) financial institutions (FFIs) to disclose to a new competent authority prescribed account information:

- (i) the identities of holders of an account who are US persons; and
- (ii) the identities of the 'controlling persons' of a passive non-financial foreign (non-US) entity (NFFE) who are US Persons.

As a preliminary requirement, the FATCA Regulations require the FFI to obtain from the 'account holder' or 'controlling person of an entity account holder' , a self-certifying tax status disclosure document: Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Form W-9 if the holder of a personal account or controlling person is a US Person; or IRS Form W-8BEN if the holder of a personal account is not a US person; or IRS Form W-8BEN.E when the holder of an entity account has a controlling person who is not a US Person.

FATCA Regulations then require FFIs to verify their clients' self-certification of their tax status by searching for facts that suggest 'US Indicia' . 37

## 3.1.4  CRS requirements

The success of FATCA in obtaining tax-related information on US taxpayers from foreign banks and financial institutions caused the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) to roll-out a global version, i.e. the CRS. The OECD drafted its model Competent Authority Agreements (CAAs) and then exerted pressure to persuade governments to give them statutory effect. Over 100 jurisdictions, including the major offshore financial centres, have signed up to comply with CRS by entering into a CAA, and more jurisdictions are expected to follow.

CRS requires a 'reporting financial institution' (RFI) located in a participating jurisdiction that has signed up to CRS, to disclose to a new competent authority prescribed account information, namely the identity of:

- a. holders of an account who are 'reportable persons' (who reside in a participating jurisdiction that has signed up to CRS); and
- b. the controlling person of a passive non-financial entity (NFE) that are reportable persons insofar as they reside in a participating jurisdiction.

As a preliminary requirement, the RFI must ascertain tax-related particulars of account holders and controlling persons of entity account holders, such as their name, address, date of birth, jurisdiction of tax residence and their Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN) or equivalent. The starting point for establishing such particulars is for the RFI to solicit from its client beneficial owner a self-certification of tax residency. As CRS is a global initiative there are no prescribed forms, just prescribed particulars. The RFI is then required to verify the accuracy of the self-certification by obtaining a written confirmation of tax residency, or equivalent and looking for alternative 'Indicia' .

## 3.1.5  Economic substance analysis

New so-called 'economic substance' requirements implemented in 2019 establish a test by which companies must demonstrate that they are properly tax-resident in the offshore jurisdiction of their incorporation:

- a. the company must be 'directed and managed' in the offshore jurisdiction; and
- b. its 'core income-generating activities' must be undertaken in such jurisdiction.

This requires an analysis of the type of business the company is to undertake upon incorporation and how it is to be managed and controlled.

## 3.2 Decide upon type of company

The first decision that a promoter has to make is the type of company they wish to incorporate, as this affects the contents of its constitutive documents. A company may be formed:

- as a private or public company
- with unlimited liability or with the liability of its members for company debts limited by shares or, sometimes, by guarantee
- for onshore/resident/local/domestic purposes or for offshore/non-resident/ international purposes
- as a special type of company.

## 3.2.1 Private or public (and listed)?

This text will not review the requirements for incorporating a public company or a company whose shares are to be listed on a stock exchange. Instead, we shall concentrate on the requirements for incorporating a private company, as this is the type of entity that you are likely to be asked to incorporate as an asset-holding vehicle for a client.

A public company is generally any limited company that does not specifically qualify as a private company. The companies legislation of some jurisdictions still follows the English template and states, for example, that:

- (i) a private company must have fewer than 50 shareholders; and
- (ii) the right of such shareholders to transfer their shares must be restricted; and
- (iii) the private company cannot invite investment from the public.

A public company on the other hand, has multiple shareholders and can offer securities (stocks and shares or debt instruments such as bonds) to the wider investing public and is therefore more tightly regulated. The two kinds of company are used for completely different purposes, the public company being designed to raise capital from the public to finance its business, while the private company is designed to obtain a separate legal personality and confer limited liability upon its owner.

In practice the choice is obvious. A sole beneficial owner consisting of a family who wish to hold family wealth in a company will require a private company. For the same reasons, a corporate trustee that wishes to hold the trust fund in a self-contained special purpose vehicle will require a private company.

A significant number of offshore jurisdictions have modernised their laws and no longer distinguish between public and private companies. 38  In these jurisdictions, organisational and internal rules of management can be made in the constitutive documents to cater for companies that do, or do not, propose to offer securities to the public.

Finally, we should consider listed companies. A listed company is one whose shares are listed on an exchange. A listing on an exchange is generally sought to facilitate trading and make the securities more liquid and marketable. A number of offshore financial centres have developed local stock exchanges with their own regulations and listing requirements. Companies can apply for a listing provided they satisfy the prescribed listing requirements. The vast majority of companies listed on one of the offshore exchanges are investment companies or 'hedge funds' . The advantage of obtaining a listing has nothing to do with liquidity and everything to do with regulation avoidance. A number of sophisticated onshore jurisdictions, such as the US and Western European countries, restrict the investment powers of institutional investors (such as insurance companies, pension funds and retail investment funds) to marketable or listed securities. This means that investment in an offshore hedge fund is prohibited. But when the offshore hedge fund obtains a listing on a recognised exchange it becomes

## 3.2.2 Unlimited or limited liability?

A company may be incorporated so that its shareholders incur unlimited liability for the company's debts. Such a company is, in the first instance, liable for its own debts but, if it cannot pay, the shareholders are liable. So if a company is insolvent and unable to pay a creditor in full, the creditor may seek payment from the shareholders without any restriction on the amount of their liability.

Why might a client require a company with unlimited liability? It is most unusual, and very few are formed, but there are a couple of logical reasons.

- To obtain a corporate vehicle with all the features identified in Module 2 save for the limited liability of its members. If the company is to have a number of non-director shareholders and carry on a trade or business with inherent risk, clear disadvantages accrue to the shareholders who bear unlimited liability.
- Certain professions, notably lawyers and accountants, permit their members to practise as an incorporated company, but only if the directors and shareholders accept unlimited personal liability (to protect clients and put the professionals on a level playing field with their colleagues who have associated under a traditional partnership structure). Most of these professional partnerships nowadays elect to structure themselves as a limited liability partnership.

## 3.2.3  Limited by shares or guarantee?

As discussed in Module 2 section 4.2, the liability of shareholders for the debts of a company may, by the memorandum of association, be limited by shares or guarantee.

Companies that are limited by shares are, by far, the more popular type of company. They are the usual corporate structure used for trading purposes (both onshore and offshore) where members invest in a business for profit and require the protection of limited liability. They are also used offshore as a vehicle to hold income-producing assets, i.e., when the passive assets of the company are expected to generate an income such as rent, dividends, interest, royalty payments, licence fees, etc. Ownership of the company and, moreover, the distribution of its earned profits are divided between the shareholders of the company according to the number of shares owned of the company is merely to hold real estate or other assets, rather than to trade, however, it is unlikely to incur liabilities and become insolvent. The disadvantage of unlimited liability is therefore negligible. On the other hand, why not simply incorporate with limited liability, just in case?.

Companies limited by guarantee are structured, by their memorandum, so that each member promises to guarantee to pay the debts of the company up to a certain amount. A typical provision found in a memorandum of a company limited by guarantee is that each member agrees to contribute, upon a winding up, 'such amount as may be required, not exceeding $100' .

A guarantee company does not have shares. This is therefore an appropriate corporate structure to be used wherever there is no intention of distributing the profits of the company but rather of ploughing the profits back into the business. If there is to be no distribution of profits there does not have to be a division of the company into shares. Each member is simply entitled to a portion of the capital upon winding up.

This type of corporate structure is widely used for not-for-profit associations, such as educational establishments, hospitals and charities, where profit-sharing does not occur. For example, s.5(3) of the Companies Act 1981 of Bermuda provides that a company limited by guarantee can be used only if:

- '(a) its purpose is to promote art, science, religion, charity, sport, education or any other social or useful purpose, and its profits, if any, and other income is to be used in promoting its purposes and no dividends are to be paid to its members, or
- (b) it is a mutual company' [defined in Pt 12 of the Act as a company authorised to carry on insurance on the mutual principle, i.e. for the sole benefit of its policyholders].

The members of a guarantee company are under no liability to pay the company's debts so long as the company remains solvent. They only become liable, to the extent of their guarantee (which is usually a fairly nominal sum such as $100), if the company is wound up.

## 3.2.4 Resident/local/domestic (for trading purposes) or offshore (for asset holding purposes)?

It is important to establish the purposes for which the company is intended, as this may influence its type and structure.

In some jurisdictions, only certain types of company are permitted to carry on business within the geographical territory of the offshore centre where they are incorporated. In other words, only local companies can trade within the domestic economy. These are known by various names in different jurisdictions, as the following examples illustrate.

- A 'domestic company' in the Bahamas, incorporated under the provisions of the Companies Act 1992 as amended, may conduct business within the Bahamas and hold land in the Bahamas, but must have majority Bahamian ownership.
- An 'ordinary resident company' in the Cayman Islands, incorporated under the provisions of the Companies Law, 2020 Revision where at least 60% of the share capital must be owned by Caymanian nationals or the company must be licensed to trade locally under the Local Companies (Control) Law - for this reason they are also known colloquially as 'local companies' .

- A 'local company' in Bermuda, incorporated under the Companies Act 1981, as amended, the shares of which must be predominantly owned by Bermudian nationals.
- Before the BVI Business Companies Act 2004, a 'CAP 285 Company' incorporated under the Companies Act, Chapter 285, was used for companies carrying on business or owning real estate within the BVI. Post-2004, they no longer exist.
- Before the abolition of exempt companies in Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man over recent years, these jurisdictions permitted the so-called 'resident company' to trade within the domestic economy.

These local, resident and domestic companies are distinguished from a different type of non-resident corporate vehicle that is not permitted to carry on business within the local economy. The alternative type of company is the one typically used by foreign clients for offshore asset-holding purposes. Such companies go by different names in different jurisdictions, but are usually known as 'exempt companies' or 'international business companies' (IBCs).

The attraction of the exempt company and IBC for the typical international high-net-worth or institutional investor is that they are not subject to corporation tax (or corporate income tax) in the jurisdiction of incorporation. This is fair enough in the nil-tax jurisdictions that do not impose corporation tax upon either the local resident company or the non-resident, tax-exempt IBC. But when a so-called low-tax jurisdiction imposes corporation tax on the local resident company that carries on business in the local economy, but confers tax exemption upon the non-resident exempt company that carries on business abroad, outside the jurisdiction of incorporation, the dichotomy of tax treatment is considered abusive or 'unfair tax competition' by the OECD. The nature of the abuse is that these low-tax jurisdictions have one set of company and tax laws for locals (to protect their local tax base) and another more favourable set of corporate and tax rules for foreigners (designed to attract foreign capital while completely disregarding the protection of the tax base in the foreign jurisdiction where such capital originates). The term adopted to describe this dichotomy is 'ring-fencing' . Having these two parallel systems of corporate tax liability within the same jurisdiction is regarded as harmful to global economic development as it is bound to attract capital from the high-tax onshore developed jurisdictions of the world.

Accordingly, around the turn of the millennium, the OECD and EU launched a series of initiatives aimed at 'tax havens' in order to curb abusive 'unfair tax competition' . Both nil-tax and low-tax jurisdictions were in the firing line, but it was the low-tax jurisdictions that held the untenable position. At least the nil-tax jurisdictions could argue that their tax laws did not discriminate between locals and foreigners.

In order to placate the international community and the OECD in particular, a number of offshore financial centres repealed these parallel, ring-fenced corporate tax regimes. A selection of both nil-tax jurisdictions (that do not impose corporate taxes) and low-tax jurisdictions (that hitherto had conferred tax exemption upon companies that did not carry on business within the jurisdiction of incorporation) harmonised the tax treatment of companies irrespective of whether they carried on business locally or abroad, within or outside the jurisdiction of incorporation, as the instances in Example 3A illustrate. 40

## Example 3A

Historically, before 2004, the hugely popular BVI IBC, incorporated under the old International Business Companies Act 1984 (IBCA) could not carry on business within the BVI, whereas the Cap 285 company, incorporated under the Companies Act (Cap 285), could carry on business locally. To harmonise the company law and regulation of all BVI corporate entities, the legislature enacted the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 (BVI BCA), replacing the IBCA and the Companies Act (Cap 285). This single statute provides for the incorporation of a single type of company which can carry on business locally within the BVI or elsewhere. In other words, the new BVI BC is subject to the same corporate and regulatory requirements, irrespective of whether it conducts business in the domestic economy or simply holds foreign assets offshore.

Low-tax centres, such as Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man, suffered the most international criticism. This was because their exempt companies were subject to a tax regime that was completely different to the regime they applied to their local resident companies: resident companies were subject to corporation tax (or corporate income tax) at the local rate, whereas exempt companies enjoyed a nil rate. The international community demanded that these jurisdictions harmonise the tax treatment of all types of entity. These jurisdictions, therefore, abolished the exempt company and its tax exempt status. Clearly OECD expected that they would then impose corporation tax at the local rate upon those companies that had hitherto qualified for tax exemption on the grounds that they did not carry on business locally and had no local source profits. In a twist, however, and in order to preserve their offshore financial services industry, the low-tax jurisdictions of Jersey, 41 Guernsey 42 and the Isle of Man 43 became quasi nil-tax jurisdictions (or, more accurately, nil-corporate-tax jurisdictions) insofar as they replaced the local rate of corporation tax with a non-discriminatory nil rate of corporate tax for all companies (except banks). This became known as the 'Zero/10' tax regime. 44  It refers to the fact that the new tax regime introduces a zero rate of tax for all corporate entities incorporated in those jurisdictions and a 10% rate of tax for banks and other financial service providers. In view of the lost tax revenues, and in order to protect their tax base, these jurisdictions continue to levy income tax on individuals and some have introduced a type of sales tax.

46.

Gibraltar 45 has an even more interesting story to tell of its efforts to comply with the EU's concept of fair taxation principles and yet at the same time protect its offshore financial sector and flourishing gambling business.

The low-tax centres of Hong Kong 46  and Singapore 47  are in a different league to Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man. They were not under the same pressure to curb their 'unfair tax competition' as they were not responsible for 'ring-fencing' . This is because these jurisdictions do not confer tax exemption upon non-resident companies that carry on business outside the geographic territory of the state of incorporation. Instead, these jurisdictions impose tax on the territorial principle, i.e. taxes are only levied upon income or profits arising or derived from the conduct of business within the jurisdiction, and not on income or profits generated from a source located outside the jurisdiction. Accordingly, Hong Kong and Singapore did not follow other low-tax jurisdictions by introducing a qualified nil-tax regime.

## 3.2.5  Any special type of company?

A number of different types of company may be incorporated in, and managed from, offshore financial centres. Although these entities generally possess the same basic characteristics and qualities associated with a private limited company, each contains its own features that distinguish it from what would otherwise appear to be a similar vehicle.

A special purpose vehicle may simply be structured as an ordinary private company but with special articles that cater for a specific transaction or asset-holding purpose. In addition, there are some innovative types of company that may be incorporated in the more liberal offshore jurisdictions under specific statutory provisions, including the protected cell/segregated portfolio company, the limited liability company (LLC), the limited duration company and international company.

## Protected cell/segregated portfolio company

This type of company has the characteristics of a typical company: in particular, it is a single and separate legal entity. Its defining feature is that it can segregate assets and liabilities into different 'protected cells' (sometimes known as 'segregated portfolios').

- i. a company the management and control of whose business is exercised in Gibraltar; or
- ii. a company which carries on business in Gibraltar and the management and control of which is exercised outside Gibraltar; or
- iii. an investment company which is domiciled anywhere outside Gibraltar, where control of the company, through shares or voting powers, is exercised by persons ordinarily resident in Gibraltar.

A non-resident company is defined as a company that is incorporated in Gibraltar and does not trade or earn income in Gibraltar. The dichotomy persists.

- Many commentators consider Hong Kong to be an offshore tax haven. While its tax rates are low by OECD standards, and a number of the taxes that are imposed by most onshore jurisdictions do not exist in Hong Kong (so there is no CGT, withholding tax, sales tax and no accumulated earning taxes on companies that retain earnings rather than distributing them), it is not a 'tax haven' in the traditional sense in that it does not confer tax exemption upon non-resident companies. Instead, it imposes taxes according to the territorial principle, i.e. taxes are only levied upon income derived from or arising within Hong Kong and not on income generated from a source located outside the Territory. While unsophisticated, this does not offend the international community by ring fencing - a tax regime which is considered to be improper and unfair in a global context. See Appendix 6B section 7.

Each cell or portfolio is insulated from the others. Assets of one cell or portfolio are not available to meet liabilities of another cell or portfolio. For example, creditors of Cell/Portfolio A cannot attach assets of the company that are held in, or constitute Cell/Portfolio B. These structures are typically used by the offshore investment funds industry when an asset management firm offers a family of different funds, each with different investment strategies and associated risks within the 'umbrella' of a single entity. They are available, among other places, in the Bahamas, Bermuda, BVI, the Cayman Islands, Guernsey, Isle of Man and Jersey - see Appendix 6B.

## Limited liability company (LLC)

The defining feature of the LLC is the way in which it is managed (and often the way it is taxed). An LLC does not have a board of directors. The company is managed by its shareholders, called members , of which there are two types: the members and the managing member. The managing member is appointed by the members and is given all powers of management of the business of the company on a day-to-day basis. The LLC has Articles of Organisation but, in addition, it is typical for the members and managing member to enter into an Operating or LLC Agreement - this is an agreement that sets out internal rules governing matters such as membership rights, management powers and duties, operating procedures, matters relating to distribution of profits, etc. These companies are not usually liable to pay corporation tax (for example, if they are managed and controlled in a high-tax onshore jurisdiction, such as the US). Instead, profits are treated as pass-through so that the company's members become liable to income tax on their share of the profits in much the same way as partners of a partnership. The Delaware LLC is a popular type of company (see Appendix 6B section 10.3) and they are also available in the Isle of Man and the Cayman Islands (see Appendix 6B sections 4.8 and 8.6).

## Limited duration company (LDC)

As the name suggests, these are companies that have a limited duration, usually up to 30 years. The exact duration must be specified in the company's memorandum. On the termination date, the company is automatically dissolved, its assets sold and reduced into money, liabilities paid and the surplus distributed among its shareholders. These companies are designed for holding assets on behalf of beneficial owners who are subject to specific tax regimes. In particular, under the Internal Revenue Code of the US, an LDC is treated as an LLC and the company does not become liable to corporation tax at the corporate level. Instead, its annual profits flow-through to the shareholders, where they are subject to income tax. LDCs are available in the Bahamas and the Cayman Islands (see Appendix 6B sections 1.5 and 4.4).

## International company

The defining feature of an international company (not to be confused with the IBC) did not relate to its corporate structure, but to the fact that it could elect to become subject to a 'designer rate' of corporation tax (or corporate income tax). In other words it could agree the rate of tax it paid to the revenue authorities of the jurisdiction of its incorporation, anywhere between zero and 30%. In practice, the international company would agree to pay such rate of tax that would be just enough to avoid any tax-avoidance rules that applied in the state in which it carried on its main business. The international company was available in Gibraltar, where it was known as a Qualifying Company, Guernsey, Isle of Man and Jersey, where it was confusingly known as an international business company. However, under OECD and EU pressure the international company has been abolished (see Appendix 6B sections 5.5, 6.6, 8.9 and 9.9).

## 3.3 Obtain name approval

## 3.3.1  Name approval

The promoter must choose an appropriate name for the proposed company as it must be stated in the company's memorandum of association or, in the more progressive jurisdictions that have essentially abandoned the memorandum, in the application for incorporation.

The name of a company must distinguish it from all other companies incorporated in that jurisdiction. No two companies may have the same or similar name unless they are part of the same group.

In addition, no name may generally be used which, in the opinion of the registrar, could cause offence or mislead the public.

The company's name must as a general rule appear in its memorandum, on its company seal, be affixed outside the registered office and appear on company stationery. There is no point in making these preparations until name approval has been obtained. Accordingly, once a service provider has received instructions to proceed with the incorporation of a company, the next step is to check whether the required name is available. This usually involves contacting the local registrar of companies and asking whether the name is available (and, in some cases, suitable) and obtaining prior approval to use it. Names can often be reserved for up to 90 days.

In some centres it is possible to check name availability with the registrar online. Accordingly, in those jurisdictions a service provider must search the index of company names and decide whether a proposed name is suitable. If it is not, the application to incorporate will, of course, be rejected.

## 3.3.2  Words that must be used

Companies legislation usually specifies that the name of a company must contain the word 'Limited' if the company affords its members limited liability. 48  Other words that may be used instead of 'Limited' , depending on the centre concerned and the type of company include: 'Corporation' , 'Incorporated' , 'Société Anonyme' , 'Sociedad Anonima' or 'avec responsibilité limitée' or their abbreviations 'Corp' , 'Inc' , 'SA' or 's.a.r.l' .

## 3.3.3  Words that must not be used

The companies legislation of the major offshore centres imposes restrictions on, or controls the use of, certain words in the names of companies. Controlled words (as opposed to prohibited words) require the consent of the registrar of companies or of other local government authorities, such as the Financial Services Supervision Commission/Authority (or equivalent), as they often signify a particular activity which is subject to regulatory controls, or a connection with government or a public entity. Examples of controlled words include 'National' , 'Council' , 'Ministry' , 'Royal' , 'Insurance' , 'Assurance' , 'Bank' , 'Chartered' , 'Investment' , 'Trust' or 'Trust Company' , 'Cooperative' , 'Financial' and 'Municipal' , 'Charity' or 'Charitable' .

Guidelines published in Hong Kong 49  provide some interesting and helpful criteria. The registrar is likely to reject:

- names that are substantially the same, e.g. Cameron Realty Pty Ltd and Cameron Real Estate Pty Ltd 50
- names that are phonetically identical, e.g. Lifecity (Birmingham) Ltd and Lyfeciti (Bermingum) Ltd
- names where a slight variation in spelling does not make a significant difference, e.g. Consulair Ltd and Consuler Ltd
- grammatical variations that do not make a significant difference, e.g. Advance Travel Ltd and Advanced Travel Ltd
- names with the same distinctive element, e.g. Factomatic Computers Ltd and Factomatic Printing Ltd.

The registrar is often given power to make a company change its name if it is the same as or similar to another, or is otherwise inappropriate.

## 3.4 Decide upon an appropriate constitution

One of the most important preliminary decisions that a promoter must make relates to the company's constitution, in particular the content of its articles of association. The articles contain the rules dealing with the internal administration of the company. These internal rules are not prescribed by companies legislation but are matter for the promoter to consider, draft and adopt. Examples are illustrated in Appendix 1B and 1C and the issue is discussed further in Module 5.

## 3.5 Submission of documents

A regulated service provider is required to prepare and deliver prescribed documents on behalf of the promoter to the registrar of companies, usually in paper form. A number of the more sophisticated jurisdictions allow applications for incorporation to be made online. These documents usually comprise the following.

## 3.5.1  An application form

In England, s.9 of the Companies Act 2006 requires a formal application form (Form IN01) to be prepared, signed and delivered, either in paper form or electronically, to the registrar. It contains information that used to be found in the company's memorandum, namely: the company's name, registered office, liability of members, whether public or private and statement of proposed officers. Some other offshore jurisdictions have a similar statutory requirement, most notably Hong Kong and Singapore. 51  Other jurisdictions require the submission of a form that has been approved by the registrar, often containing similar details.

In those jurisdictions where online applications for incorporation are catered for, it is typical for the service provider to populate a form prescribed by the software designed by, or on behalf of, the registrar. 52

## 3.5.2  The memorandum of association

According to the 1948 Act model, a company is required to have a memorandum of association containing prescribed information such as the company's name, registered office, objects, a statement that the liability of its members is limited, and details of the share capital. Following the prescribed information, usually set out in numbered paragraphs, the memorandum typically states that the person or persons whose names are subscribed to the memorandum wish to form a company and, upon formation, become its first shareholders. 53 In those jurisdictions that use the 1948 Act model, as well as the IBC jurisdictions, the memorandum must be signed by at least one subscriber and delivered to the registrar upon incorporation. An example is illustrated in Appendix 1A of the online Study Resources.

When the registered office clause of the memorandum does not give the precise physical street location of the registered office but merely states that it is situated within the jurisdiction of incorporation, it is usual for the incorporator to deliver a statement of the location of the registered office.

The 2006 Companies Act in England has essentially made the memorandum redundant as the prescribed information is now set out in the application form. Certain jurisdictions, such as Hong Kong and Singapore, have followed this modern approach and abandoned the memorandum.

## 3.5.3 The articles of association (or by-laws)

The articles contain the internal administrative regulations affecting the management of the company. They are generally drafted with the assistance of precedents, i.e., in-house forms generally in use for certain types of company, which may be altered or re-drafted to reflect a client's particular requirements. Examples are set out in Appendices 1B and 1C. Alternatively, companies in some jurisdictions may simply adopt a standard set of articles published as a schedule to, or according to regulations made under, the appropriate companies legislation. 54

## 3.5.4  Payment of the fee

The appropriate fee payable to the registrar of companies covers the cost of local registration and any incorporation duty payable. The aggregate fee may be calculated on an ad valorem basis upon the authorised capital of the company. For instance, in Hong Kong duty is payable at the rate of HK$6 per $1,000 of authorised capital. In most jurisdictions, however, it is often the case that companies with an authorised capital below a certain level are required to pay a flat fee regardless of the amount of capital involved. Alternatively, the fee may be calculated on the basis of the number of shares the company can issue. For example, a BVI BC that is authorised to issue 50,000 shares or fewer pays an incorporation fee of $350, whereas if it is authorised to issue more than 50,000 shares the incorporation fee increases to $1,100. This is why most offshore companies have an authorised capital or maximum number of available shares that is far in excess of the issued share capital. It is cost effective to incorporate a company with the maximum amount of capital permitted or maximum number of permitted shares for the minimum fee payable.

## 3.5.5 Declaration or certificate of compliance

Local legislation or practice require some sort of declaration or certificate of compliance to be filed by the licensed service provider engaged to incorporate the company (or, in jurisdictions such as Hong Kong and Singapore, by the founding member or initial directors of the company). The purpose of the so-called declaration of compliance is to confirm compliance with all the statutory requirements for the incorporation process laid down by applicable companies legislation. It is an example of a modern trend whereby the regulator delegates prima facie responsibility for enforcement of the legal requirements to licensed service providers. 55  An example of a declaration of compliance is illustrated in Appendix 8 of the online Study Resources.

## 3.5.6  Other filing requirements

To comply with modern OECD and Financial Action Task Force (FATF) recommendations, a number of jurisdictions have introduced additional filing requirements for particulars of directors and beneficial owners of companies, i.e. the natural persons who ultimately own or control 25% or more of the shares, or exercise control of the management, of the company. Regulated service providers that incorporate and manage companies, and the registered agents for IBCs, are therefore nowadays required to enter particulars of the company's beneficial owners in a database maintained either by the registrar or by the relevant competent authority upon, or shortly after, incorporation.

See section 4.1.5 below for a discussion of the evolution of this requirement and how it has been complied with as a compromise of the tension between the public interest of increased transparency and the private interests of client confidentiality.

## 3.6 Review of documents and grant of certificate of incorporation

The registrar of companies reviews the papers that have been submitted (to fulfil an administrative requirement only, rather than as a judicial act). Assuming that there are no errors or omissions and that no further details are required, the registrar proceeds to register the company, allocates it a number, enters its name upon the register of companies and issues a certificate of incorporation.

The certificate of incorporation provides conclusive evidence that the company has complied with all matters relating to the process of incorporation and that the company has been registered under, and exists pursuant to, local companies legislation. Thereupon, the subscriber(s) become the holder(s) of the so-called subscriber share(s) and appoint the first board of directors; then the company is ready to commence business.

## 4. Practical matters following incorporation

Once a company has been incorporated it can commence its business and operations.

The exact nature of a company's activities after it has been incorporated depends on the purpose of the company concerned. A company may enter into contracts, acquire assets, employ staff. Regardless of the specific business to be undertaken, certain organisational actions must be taken immediately upon the incorporation of an offshore company. The following is an outline of the acts that are commonly performed by service providers upon incorporation.

## 4.1 Creation of the corporate records

It is a requirement of applicable companies legislation in the major common law offshore centres that a company must maintain proper corporate records. These records, sometimes known as 'statutory registers' , may have to be kept at the company's registered office. Specimen registers are illustrated in Appendix 7. Registers usually comprise the following documents:

## 4.1.1 A register of directors 56

The register of directors must contain prescribed information, generally the names, addresses and occupations of the directors (perhaps also with a note of their nationality) and the date on which they were appointed to the board, together with the date of resignation, if applicable; in the case of corporate directors, the register must contain its corporate name and registered office. In most jurisdictions, the register or particulars of directors must be filed with the registrar (see Module 7 section 3.3), where it remains private rather than open to public inspection.

## 4.1.2 A register of the other officers 57

The register of other officers is usually restricted to details of the company secretary; it includes that individual's name, address, nature of the role held, and the date appointed, together with the date of resignation, if applicable. For IBCs this is called a register of registered agents and, guess what?, it must contain particulars of the identity of the company's registered agent and, as for directors, particulars of the registered agent must be filed with the registrar.

## 4.1.3 A register of members 58

The register of members contains the names and addresses of all the shareholders, the date on which they became members and also ceased to be members, if applicable, together with details of the shares subscribed and allotted and the class of shares involved. Typical legislation enacted across the jurisdictions states that trusts or, moreover, the beneficial interests under a trust, should not be entered in the register of members, just particulars of the legal holder(s) of the share(s).

Bearer shares were permitted in some jurisdictions provided they were immobilised, most notably the BVI and Cayman Islands. However, they have now been completely abolished - see s.10 of the BVI Business Companies (Amendment) Act 2022, which repeals ss.67-77 of the original BVI Business Companies Act 2004; and s.229 of the Companies Act 2022 Revision, which was amended in 2022 to prohibit a company incorporated in the Cayman Islands from issuing bearer shares.

## 4.1.4 A register of charges

The register of charges contains details of any mortgages that the company has created over its assets. The 1948 Act model made this mandatory, but it is no longer a statutory requirement under current English legislation, 59 nor in the majority of the offshore financial centres. 60  When applicable, it should record details of all mortgages and floating charges over or affecting the whole or part of the assets of the company.

## 4.1.5 A register of beneficial ownership (or equivalent)

There is a history to this new requirement. Its starting point is the famous 'Forty Recommendations' relating to AML policies propounded by the FATF. The Forty Recommendations include Recommendation 24, calling for increased transparency to enable outsiders to ascertain the true owners of corporate entities. The FATF subsequently issued Guidance on Transparency and Beneficial Ownership in October 2014, 61  recommending that jurisdictions ensure that information relating to the beneficial ownership of corporate entities be readily available to law enforcement agencies and revenue authorities. This could be achieved by either:

- i. requiring each jurisdiction to maintain a central public register of prescribed information on the beneficial owners of companies incorporated in such jurisdiction, or
- ii. requiring that similar prescribed information be held in a register maintained by each company incorporated in such jurisdiction, with such information being available to the law enforcement and other authorities of such jurisdiction when a lawful request is made.

The EU made this FATF Recommendation a part of the Fourth Money Laundering Directive, which was implemented in the UK in 2016, whereby UK companies are now required to maintain a Register of Persons with Significant Control, a copy of which must be submitted to the registrar upon incorporation and which is publicly accessible online via the registrar at Companies House.

The UK government exerted significant pressure upon Her Majesties Crown Dependencies and British Overseas Territories to create similar centrally held, fully public registers of beneficial ownership. Most offshore financial centres were reluctant to do so. Instead, the progressive jurisdictions sought to develop the means of centralising beneficial ownership information in a way that balances client confidentiality with enabling law enforcement agencies to have access to information when criminals abuse the corporate veil. Accordingly, at the date of publication of this edition of the study text, beneficial ownership registers are not open to the public, but maintained by a competent authority in each jurisdiction in a confidential manner where only law enforcement and regulators can obtain access. The footnotes provide an overview of a sample of jurisdictions, namely the BVI, 62 the Cayman Islands, 63 Guernsey 64  and Singapore. 65 In respect of beneficial ownership registers required in other financial centres, you are referred to Task 3.5.

## 4.5.8  Company seal

Historically, companies would have a 'common seal' , which would be used by the company to execute formal documents such as deeds, transfers, loans and debentures and some contracts that are required to be executed under seal as deeds.

Modern companies legislation makes it optional for a company to have a seal to execute deeds. 73 For example, modern legislation typically follows s.44 of the Companies Act 2006 in England, which states that a document can be executed by a company by affixing its common seal or by signature of a director in the presence of a witness to attest the signature. ## 4.5.9  Appointment of bankers, brokers, custodians

Most companies, from a practical point of view, require a bank account. The board of directors must therefore appoint bankers to the company as soon as possible. Often one bank is sufficient but in some cases a variety of banks may be appointed, usually if the funds involved are substantial.

The directors must decide which bank(s) to appoint and record their decision in writing. To save time, the directors often obtain each bank's standard account-opening documentation (a mandate form giving specimen signatures, specifying who can sign cheques and whether there is a limit). These papers are often produced to the meeting for completion and signature, and a copy is attached to the minutes and deemed part thereof. If the papers are not available, it is usual for the directors to decide who is empowered to complete and sign the papers on behalf of the board at a future date. See Resolution 5 of Appendix 9.

A similar resolution is required to open and operate a brokerage and custody account (in order to buy and sell securities such as stocks and shares). See Resolution 6 of Appendix 9.

## 4.5.10 Appointment of agents

If agents, such as an investment manager or tax attorney, are required, the directors may discuss these appointments and make their decision at the time of their first meeting. More than one agent may be required and, if this is the case, each appointment should be minuted in turn. The fees of the agent(s) and terms of their engagement are often left to be decided by the directors at some future date. See Resolution 7 of Appendix 9.

## 4.5.11 Financial year end

If the directors intend to produce formal management accounts or financial statements for the company, it is usual to fix an accounting reference date. This is the date to which the company must make up its accounts each year, i.e. the final day of the company's accounting year. See Resolution 8 of Appendix 9.

## 4.5.12 Subscriber shares

When a company limited by shares is incorporated, the subscribers agree to take at least one share in the company. On incorporation, the subscriber(s) become(s) the first member(s) of the company (save for (I)BCs). Nothing further is required to complete the issue of the subscriber shares as they are issued by operation of law when incorporation occurs. It is the usual practice, however, for the directors to arrange for the share certificate(s) to be completed, sealed if necessary and delivered to the subscriber(s) or held on file. (If the subscribers do not intend to retain their share ownership, the subscriber shares will be transferred to nominees.)

Most offshore corporate service providers use nominee companies to act as the subscribers for new companies. In such cases the shares are issued in the name of the nominee(s) who become(s) the registered shareholder(s). The name(s) and address(es) of the subscriber(s) and details of the share(s) taken by them must be entered in the register of members.

In some cases, however, employees of the service provider may act as the subscribers. In these circumstances the shares issued to the subscribers will probably, upon the issue of the Certificate of Incorporation, be transferred into the name of a nominee company. The provisions found in the articles in relation to the transfer of shares must therefore be complied with, including any provision relating to rights of pre-emption.

## 4.5.13 Allotment of shares

It may have been the intention of the promoters to issue additional shares to third parties, following incorporation, in exchange for capital. This is often done (particularly in the onshore corporate model structure used for trading, as opposed to asset holding, purposes) to raise money for the company and give it some working capital. The issue of shares in this way, in exchange for cash or other monetary consideration, is called the allotment (when the company enters into a binding contract to issue shares at an agreed price) and an issue of shares (when the ownership in new shares passes to the new shareholder).

The procedures and documentation involved in the issue of new shares are reviewed in Module 6 section 1. In a nutshell, directors must:

- be authorised, either by legislation or by the articles, to allot shares (see Appendix 1B model arts 2.1 and 2.3)

- comply with any pre-emption rights (see Appendix 1B alternative art.8.3, which confers pre-emption rights upon the shareholders)
- follow the procedure, if any, for allotment set out in the articles, which usually involves a directors' resolution to allot and issue shares to those persons who have applied for them, and requires them to complete, sign and seal (or execute) the appropriate share certificates
- update the register of members
- update the register of beneficial ownership, if affected by the new issue of shares.

New shareholders normally pay for their shares in cash (although they may provide non-cash consideration) in full upon allotment in exchange for their shares. So, for example, a prospective member who buys 100 × $100 shares for $100 each must pay the company $10,000. The directors may, however, agree to let the prospective member take partly paid shares. In these circumstances, the whole price must be paid eventually, but does not have to be paid immediately.

Whether the shares are fully, or partly paid-up, should usually be recorded in the minutes. The articles illustrated in Appendix 1C of the online Study Resources state in art.21 that all shares must be fully paid-up.

## 4.5.14 Any other business

The first meeting of the directors may also include other business dealing with the future management or operation of the company that has not been referred to above. Furthermore, it may include the approval of the costs of formation so that the costs of incorporating the company come out of company funds, or the adoption of any pre-incorporation contracts.
