## An Introduction to Company Law and Practice

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- introduce company law and the practice of private company management
- review the position of the offshore company in context, i.e. as part of a structure used by trustees, private banking institutions and other firms to hold and manage clients' assets
- summarise the role of this text, give a brief overview of the study of company law and introduce what is to come in subsequent modules.

## Introduction: the evolution of private banking

Why should private bankers, wealth managers and trust practitioners have to study company law and practice? Surely this is an area for corporate lawyers and company directors engaged in advising or operating a commercial undertaking involved in the operation of a business?

The answer, of course, lies in the development of private banking, and in particular, the use of offshore financial centres for wealth management business.

Private banking and wealth management have been around for a very long period of time. For example, since the emergence of banking in the 18th century, companies in Switzerland have been heavily engaged in the holding, investing and managing of assets for high-net-worth individuals (HNWIs). 1

More recently, since the early 1970s, the large US investment banks have developed private client business aimed specifically at wealth management for US and foreign (non-US) HNWIs. Then, when the Glass-Steagall Act of 1931 (which prevented US commercial banks from being involved in investment banking business by selling or buying company securities 2 ) was abolished in 1999, it paved the way for the larger US commercial banks to enter into the private banking arena. 3

The period from 1970 to the financial crisis in 2008 saw a growth in offshore private banking and wealth management. This led to a growth in the popularity of the two types of vehicle used, in common law financial centres, by tax and estate planners and investment managers alike, namely the trust and the underlying asset-holding company. Although the offshore wealth management business has plateaued and fragmented somewhat since 2008, with a number of global financial conglomerates divesting themselves of their private wealth management divisions and trust companies to smaller boutique firms, it is nonetheless still a thriving industry.

The typical offshore structure used for holding assets has been described as a 'trust and company combo'. It is a structure established by a HNWI (the settlor), usually on the advice of a wealth manager, whereby the settlor transfers funds to an offshore trustee to hold upon the trusts set out in the trust instrument. The trustee then transfers the trust fund to a special purpose company (either by way of loan or in exchange for shares representing its entire capital). It is therefore the company that actually holds and invests the trust fund. It is the company that buys and sells the portfolio of securities, the real estate, the intellectual property, etc., held in trust. Most of the day-to-day investment and asset management activity therefore takes place at the company level rather than at the trust level, insofar as:

- securities that comprise the trust's portfolio are constantly being bought and sold by the company , upon the advice of the asset managers and investment advisers
- dividends and distributions of profits or capital surplus are declared, or loan repayments made, by the company , as distributions to the trustee who passes them onwards to the beneficiaries.

A professional trustee engaged in managing a trust therefore must also be in a position to manage the affairs of a private limited company that acts as a special purpose holding vehicle. The trustee must be conversant with the terminology, practices and procedures involved in managing a company.

## 1. The learning objectives of this Advanced Certificate

There are two key objectives of this Advanced Certificate:

- i. to review relevant company law provisions, i.e. the legal rules that regulate and control a company and its affairs; and
- ii. to guide and train you, in a practical manner, in the practice of company administration.

As an employee of a trust company or other service provider carrying on company management business offshore, you may already have some experience of the practical side of incorporation, administration and dissolution of companies. In order to fully grasp the many day-to-day administrative requirements and issues relating to an offshore company, and to be able to advise confidently and knowledgeably, however, you must have an understanding of the legal framework within which the company exists.

In other words, you need to know the legal rules and regulations affecting companies under your management, as well as the practical administrative procedures, often followed checklist-style, by corporate administrators.

## 2. The role of this text

This course serves two purposes:

- an academic, sometimes theoretical, review of company law, often comparing onshore requirements with their offshore counterparts
- a practical guide to everyday matters encountered in offshore company management practice, such as the terms upon which service providers are retained; the process of incorporation, continuance and dissolution of companies; decision making; the transaction of business; provision of shareholders, directors and officers; the reduction or control of risk; and many other issues of practical importance.

This course does not, however, deal with regulatory and compliance requirements. Modern governmental regulations that impose new duties upon offshore companies and corporate service providers are reviewed in the STEP Advanced Certificate in Principles of International Taxation, AML and Compliance. This course touches on the following matters, but does not go into detail:

- principles of corporate taxation
- regulation of company management business
- transparency and disclosure requirements imposed by anti-money laundering (AML) legislation, the US Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) Regulations, the Common Reporting Standards (CRS) and the Economic Substance regulations.

If you are interested in learning more about these matters, you may wish to consider studying the STEP Advanced Certificate in Principles of International Taxation, AML and Compliance.

Before we embark upon a detailed review of company law and practice, it is worthwhile, at this stage, to state some fundamental legal principles and make a few observations to enable you to grasp the basics.

## 3. Company law

A partnership is essentially a private agreement made between two or more persons to run a business together for their mutual benefit. The relationship of the partners to one another (and third parties), their rights, duties, responsibilities and liabilities are a matter of contract. The partnership agreement (which may be oral or in writing) defines the partners' mutual rights and obligations.

By contrast, a company is the product of statute. In order to obtain the advantages of concepts such as a separate legal personality and limited liability of shareholders, a company must fulfil detailed and sometimes complex conditions and agree to abide by statutory rules and regulations. In short, it must comply with the requirements of applicable companies legislation.

Companies legislation, however, often provides only a general framework. In many instances it allows a company to govern itself. Each company has its own, private, set of rules and regulations found in its constitution - i.e., its memorandum and its articles of association. Accordingly, a company is subject to three sets of rules:

- i. the companies legislation that applies to all companies incorporated in that jurisdiction (see section 3.1);
- ii. the provisions contained in the memorandum and articles of association of the company. Provided that they comply with the minimum requirements set out in the companies legislation, these provisions apply only to that company and may be as varied as the legislative framework permits (see section 3.2 below); and
- iii. modern jurisdiction-specific regulations that impose requirements upon companies, and the directors and regulated service providers who manage them, to ensure compliance with the new duties imposed by AML legislation, FATCA, CRS, Economic Substance rules, etc. (These are covered in the STEP Advanced Certificate in Principles of International Taxation, AML and Compliance).

## 3.1 Companies legislation

Legislation, generally, can be divided into three types:

- i. mandatory rules - the so-called 'must' or 'shall' rules; for example, a law that states 'the directors of the company must wear red socks/ must not wear white socks';
- ii. permissive rules - the so-called 'may' rules; for example, a law that states 'the directors may , if authorised by the articles, wear red socks'; and
- iii. presumptive rules - for example, a law that states ' subject to any contrary provision in the articles, all the directors of the company must wear red socks' .

Mandatory rules are intended to regulate behaviour: to prohibit or inhibit certain behaviour in the interests of some public good. The mandatory rules apply automatically to all persons and entities and must be complied with.

The other two types of rule, permissive and presumptive rules, are intended to make it easier for persons and entities to arrange their own rules as they think fit. Permissive rules do not apply automatically, but do so if the person or entity decides to opt into the regime. Presumptive rules apply automatically unless the person or entity decides to opt out.

Accordingly, in the above example of a permissive rule, our company may , if it thinks it is appropriate, opt to prescribe regulations making it a requirement that its directors wear red socks. In the example of a presumptive rule, all the directors must wear red socks, unless the company, if it thinks it is appropriate, opts out of the regime and prescribes regulations allowing its directors to wear socks of a different colour.

A great deal of companies legislation, both onshore and offshore, is permissive and/or presumptive. For example:

- '[1] A company may, if authorised by its articles, purchase its own shares.'
- '[2] Subject to any limitations in its memorandum or articles, the business affairs of a company shall be managed by a board of directors that shall comprise ... .'

Paragraph [1] is permissive in so far as it allows a company to buy its own shares; it cannot do so unless it opts in and includes a provision in its articles authorising the company to buy its own shares.

Paragraph [2] is prima facie mandatory insofar as the company shall be managed by a board of directors; but this provision applies unless the company opts out and makes some other provision in its memorandum and articles (for example, by allowing its shareholders to manage the business).

Accordingly, companies legislation is not like a criminal code, which contains mandatory rules applicable to everyone (the must or shall rules, for example: 'Thou shalt not ...' , or 'Thou shalt ...'). Companies legislation creates a framework within which a company (by its memorandum and articles) or its participants (by resolution of the directors or shareholders) may make their own regulations. This is why, even in one isolated jurisdiction with uniform laws, it is conceivable that no two companies are identical. It is also why the recommendations contained in section 3.2 should be heeded.

## 3.2 Memorandum and articles of association

During the course of corporate administration, whenever a query arises in relation to the powers, rights, procedural requirements, etc. of a company under your management, the answer will inevitably be found in one of two sources: either in the companies legislation or in the company's constitutive documents, namely its memorandum and articles of the company - and possibly in both.

The companies legislation of the common law offshore jurisdictions, although typically based upon older English statutes such as the Companies Act 1948, differs from jurisdiction to jurisdiction. The memorandum and articles may differ from company to company. Given the international nature of this STEP qualification, we have sought to describe rules of general application in this text and, where applicable, refer you to sections of jurisdiction-specific legislation in the footnotes.

It is impossible, however, to cover an infinite number of variations of memoranda and articles of association. We have quoted from, or summarised, typical articles where possible, but it is probable that the articles of companies under your management are different, to a lesser or greater extent. During the course of your career in company management and administration, it is inevitable that you will encounter many different provisions. You must, therefore, have regard to the specific provisions found in the articles of association of the companies under your management.

Thankfully, trust companies and corporate service providers try to use uniform memoranda and articles whenever they can. Standard articles have been drafted and are in use, often being adopted by new companies intact, or subject only to minor amendments to comply with the client's wishes. We recommend that, as you work through this course, you review the specimen articles found in Appendices 1B and 1C, alongside the companies legislation enacted in the jurisdiction where you work or have some other connection. In this way you can relate the general provisions reviewed in the course to jurisdiction-specific provisions, and to the standard provisions applicable to the organisation where you work.

## 4. Overview

Accordingly, this manual gives a broad, general overview of fundamental corporate principles. Tasks within each module will ask you to:

- review the companies legislation enacted in your jurisdiction to cross-check general propositions and establish local requirements
- consider a specific set of articles of association typically adopted by companies under your management to establish the precise terms of their self-regulation.

In Appendices 1B and 1C we have provided two sets of articles for you to review periodically, both to familiarise yourself with them and to compare with your standard articles. Whenever possible, you should use and become familiar with the standard articles adopted by your organisation: the constitutions of most of the companies under your management will be based on these. In summary, when this text asks you to review typical articles, use the ones you encounter most frequently in the office.

But don't panic! The examination is based on general principles and can be tackled by any well-prepared candidate who has worked through this text. You do not need to memorise the provisions of applicable companies legislation or the precise provisions of our specimen articles found in Appendices 1B and 1C to be able to pass the exam. Concentrate on the general corporate principles and standard practice. In the office you can consult the companies legislation and the memorandum and articles of the company under scrutiny. Our aim is to give you the ability and confidence to research these documents, to understand their provisions and to rely upon the conclusions reached. The examination reflects this objective. You will be tested on understanding, not on the minutiae. Good luck!
