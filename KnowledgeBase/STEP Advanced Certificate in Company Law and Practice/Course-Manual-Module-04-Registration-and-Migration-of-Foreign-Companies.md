## Module 4:

## Registration and Migration of Foreign Companies

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- review the requirements that must be complied with when a foreign company wishes to establish a place of business in, and operate such business from, a typical offshore financial centre
- review the requirements that must be complied with when a foreign company wishes to migrate to, and re-domicile in, a typical offshore financial centre.

## Introduction

A topic related to the incorporation of a new company is the registration or continuation of an existing foreign company in an offshore centre.

A foreign company (incorporated for instance in State A, say, America) may wish to establish a place of business in an offshore jurisdiction (State B, say, the Bahamas). The companies legislation of a number of common law offshore jurisdictions permits this under what is known as a registration procedure applicable to foreign (or overseas) companies.

Alternatively, a foreign company (incorporated in State A) may wish to migrate from State A and become re-domiciled as a company in another jurisdiction (say, State B, the Bahamas). The companies legislation of a number of offshore jurisdictions permits this under what is known as a continuation procedure applicable to foreign (overseas) companies.

## 1. Registration of a foreign company

An established trading company incorporated in one jurisdiction (State A) may wish to carry on business in a jurisdiction other than the one in which it was incorporated (say, State B). In these circumstances the usual procedure adopted is for the company to do so through a subsidiary, specifically incorporated in State B for the purpose of carrying on its business in State B.

The registration procedure is an alternative to the incorporation of a subsidiary. Rather than an established foreign company forming a new company in State B, wholly owned by its State A parent, it may, as an alternative, simply register itself as a foreign company in State B. The theory underpinning the registration requirement is that it will prevent a foreign company avoiding the compliance and regulatory regime that exists (in State B) by operating overseas otherwise than through a subsidiary.

Typical companies legislation based upon Pt X ss.406-423 of the English 1948 Act requires a foreign company, before or within a specified period of establishing a place of business in an offshore jurisdiction, to become registered as a foreign company with the registrar of companies in that jurisdiction.

## 1.1 Establishing a place of business

Whether a company incorporated in State A has established a place of business in an alternative jurisdiction (State B, and therefore must comply with the registration requirements of State B) is a question of fact. A place of business means something more than simply doing business. It requires a degree of permanence. There must be a specific place of business and identifiable persons conducting it.

In Artemis v Artemis Transportation Corp 74 it was held that for a company to establish a place of business in a particular place it required:

- some fixed place of business (such as an office)
- human agents who reside in the jurisdiction for the purposes of carrying on the business of the company
- the conduct of business should endure for a sufficiently substantial period of time.

Other jurisdictions have endeavoured to provide a statutory definition of the term 'establishing a place of business' .

For example, the Bermudian legislation 75  provides that:

'a company shall be deemed to ... carry on a business in Bermuda if it occupies premises in Bermuda, or if it makes known by way of advertisement or in a directory or by means of its letterheads that it may be contacted at a particular address in Bermuda or is otherwise seen to be ... carrying on any trade or business within Bermuda on a continuing basis; provided that it shall not be deemed to carry on business by reason only that:

- (i) a travelling salesman representing the company has been permitted to land in Bermuda and as such establishes a temporary place of business
- (ii) meetings of its officers or members are held in Bermuda
- (iii) the company is buying or selling or otherwise dealing in shares, bonds, stock or other securities issued by an exempted undertaking.'

You will appreciate that the registration of a foreign company with the registrar of companies in an offshore jurisdiction in these circumstances may qualify the foreign company as a local/domestic/resident company in so far as it is permitted to establish a place of business and carry on its operations within the geographical territory of the offshore centre. In these circumstances it is not possible to register a foreign company as an IBC in one of the IBC jurisdictions (save for the BVI), as IBCs are prohibited from conducting the operations of a business in or from the jurisdiction of incorporation.

## 1.2 The registration procedure

The registration procedure is set out in the companies legislation of the jurisdiction where the foreign company wishes to establish and operate a place of business. 76

Before or within a specified number of months (usually one) of establishing a place of business in an offshore jurisdiction, a foreign company must apply to become registered in that jurisdiction. The application requires the foreign company to file the following documents with the registrar of companies:

- a certified copy of its memorandum and articles, charter and by-laws or other documents setting out its constitution and internal regulations
- a certified copy of its certificate of incorporation, or other evidence of incorporation
- particulars of the directors and other officers of the company, including names, addresses and nationalities
- the name and address of at least one person, resident within the offshore jurisdiction where the foreign company intends to operate, who is authorised to accept service of process (legal or formal communications) on behalf of the company; this person is usually called the 'authorised representative' or 'authorised agent' of the company
- the appropriate registration fee.

If content that the application is proper and in order, the registrar will enter the name of the company on the Register of Foreign Companies (or equivalent) and issue a certificate of registration.

## 1.3 Authorised representative/agent

The foreign company must, upon registration, appoint an authorised agent (sometimes known as an 'authorised representative'). The authorised agent/representative has two functions.

Firstly, the authorised agent/representative is designated to accept service of legal process on behalf of the company so that it thereby submits to the authority of the court of the offshore centre. For example, a writ (complaint or similar legal process) issued by the court of the offshore centre is deemed sufficiently served upon the foreign company if it is properly addressed and delivered to the authorised agent/ representative. The foreign company will not then be able to ignore the proceedings by, claiming that the court has no jurisdiction over it by arguing that it is only subject to the laws and the courts of its jurisdiction of incorporation.

Secondly, the authorised agent/representative ensures that, so far as is possible, the company complies with applicable local laws and regulations.

## 2. Migration (sometimes referred to as re-domiciliation)

## 2.1 Introduction to migration issues

Clearly, however, not all foreign companies may wish to carry on business in the offshore jurisdiction in the traditional sense of conducting business operations. A company may wish merely to change its state of incorporation, the state of location of its registered office and its governing law and thereby obtain the advantages of an offshore vehicle simply to own and manage income-producing assets on behalf of the beneficial owner of its shares in a tax-efficient manner. A foreign company may, therefore, prefer to migrate , i.e. to de-register in the foreign jurisdiction of its incorporation, and re-register in an offshore jurisdiction so as to convert to an offshore entity. In these circumstances, continuance is the appropriate statutory procedure, reviewed below.

According to English law, the promoters of a company can choose to incorporate in any jurisdiction of the world and then, by being registered as a foreign company as described above, carry on business entirely in the UK. In other words, English conflict of laws rules recognises the validity of a company that has been incorporated elsewhere. Furthermore, if that foreign company wishes to migrate to another jurisdiction, that is a matter for the laws of the two jurisdictions involved: the law of the state of incorporation and the law of the jurisdiction to which the company intends to migrate. But English law does not permit a company to migrate from or to England. A company formed in England cannot migrate to Scotland, nor to another EU member state, nor to any other jurisdiction; nor will England accept an incoming company.

Some jurisdictions have, however, extended the international dimension of the registration requirement and made provision for a foreign company to migrate to the offshore jurisdiction. While English companies legislation does not allow migration, a number of the progressive offshore financial centres now provide for it by modern migration (or re-domiciliation) provisions enacted to promote and attract international business.

The procedure involves de-registration of the company in the jurisdiction where it was originally incorporated and re-registration (or continuance ) in the offshore centre. Accordingly, for instance, a company incorporated in State A may remove itself from the register of companies there, and migrate from State A and continue as, for instance, an IBC in State B (without the need to wind up its affairs in State A and incorporate an entirely new entity in State B).

## 2.2 The continuation procedure

A foreign company may apply to be re-registered (or continued) as an offshore entity, provided that the companies legislation of the offshore centre concerned contains modern company migration provisions.

It is therefore possible for a company incorporated and existing under the provisions of the company laws of State A to migrate to and re-register or continue as:

- an IBC in the Bahamas, or BC in the BVI 77
- an exempt company limited by shares in Bermuda, Turks and Caicos Islands or the Cayman Islands 78
- a private company in Guernsey, Jersey or Isle of Man 79
- a Gibraltarian private company 80
- a Hong Kong or Singaporean company. 81

The application for continuation typically requires the service provider to file the following documents with the registrar:

- a copy of an appropriate (often, special) resolution of the shareholders of the company confirming the company's intention to migrate (an appropriate board resolution being illustrated in Appendix 10A and a shareholders' resolution in Appendix 10B of the online Study Resources) 82
- a certified copy of the memorandum and articles of association (and translations, if appropriate)
- articles of continuation, if necessary, containing amendments to the memorandum and articles that are to be effective upon re-registration and that will leave the company compliant with local companies legislation requirements
- satisfactory evidence that the company is in good standing in the jurisdiction of incorporation
- satisfactory evidence that the company is solvent (a requirement that exists to ensure that the company is not trying to run away from payment of its debts and liabilities).

Most jurisdictions state that the foreign company must be authorised to migrate and transfer its domicile according to the law of the state of incorporation (or at least is not prohibited from doing so), and has complied with the requirements of the laws of the state of incorporation for de-registering in such jurisdiction. In the BVI, a company wishing to migrate out of the BVI must now advertise their intention to do so and give 14 days' notice to all members and creditors - see s.21 of the 2022 amendments which amend s.184 of the BVI BCA 2020 Rev.

The 'satisfactory evidence' referred to above is typically an affidavit by the sole director, or one of the board members, of the company, which confirms compliance with each of the statutory requirements and, where necessary, exhibits verifying documentation. A sample affidavit is illustrated in Appendix 10C of the online Study Resources.

If the application is proper and in order, the registrar will issue a 'certificate of continuation' .

## 2.3 Legal effect of continuation

Upon the issue of the certificate of continuation the (former foreign) company is deemed to be a company incorporated under the offshore companies legislation, and in particular:

- its memorandum and articles, as amended by the certificate of continuation, continue to apply
- all property, including the benefit of contracts, remains vested in the company
- the company continues to be liable for all its existing debts, claims and liabilities
- all shares owned by shareholders are deemed issued in conformity with local companies legislation and continue to be held by the registered shareholders.

In a nutshell, the company continues to exist but has simply migrated to another jurisdiction.

Having regard to the consequences set out above, if a client wishes to 'offshore' corporate assets, it is clearly easier to apply for an onshore asset-holding company to be continued in an offshore jurisdiction than to wind-up the onshore company and transfer each individual asset, corporate contract or item of property to a new company formed offshore. As the name suggests, continuation has the advantage of continuity.

## 2.4 Reasons for migrating

Statutory migration provides a much sleeker mechanism for the transfer of a business from one jurisdiction to another than by starting a new business. It avoids winding-up the company in the first jurisdiction and transferring each and every asset to a new company incorporated in the second jurisdiction. But why do so? The reasons may be manifold:

- to take advantage of a more favourable regulatory or tax regime or more conducive business environment
- to cut costs and streamline administration of the company
- to consolidate or merge with existing group structures
- to obtain access to new business opportunities
- to access specialist capital markets.
