# Appendix 6B: Types of company available in selected jurisdictions

## Appendix 6B: Types of company available in selected jurisdictions

## 1. The Bahamas

The Bahamas are a nil-tax jurisdiction insofar as they do not impose corporation or withholding taxes upon companies, nor income taxes etc. upon individuals.

The main corporate entity incorporated in the Bahamas and used for offshore asset holding purposes is the IBC, incorporated under the International Business Companies Act 2000 as last amended in 2014 ('IBCA'). This legislation is copied from the old BVI legislation of the same name, the IBCA 1984, itself sharing some of the features of the English model as well as US company legislation. The IBCA did not supersede the existing companies law when first introduced in 1989, but complemented it insofar as it exists alongside the traditional companies legislation, most recently restated in the Companies Act 1992. The Companies Act 1992 is based upon the English 1948 Act and permits the incorporation of companies to carry on business in the Bahamas - they can be public or private, unlimited, limited by shares or guarantee. The Bahamas have also passed additional companies legislation for the provision of special purpose corporate vehicles.

## 1.1 Domestic limited company

A domestic resident company limited by shares can be incorporated under the Companies Act 1992. This type of company is used for the purpose of carrying on business in the Bahamas. It must have at least two directors, and the company's annual return must include a list of shareholders which is kept on the public register. Financial statements do not have to be audited and are not filed.

## 1.2 Company limited by guarantee

For a general description of this type of company you are referred to sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4. The memorandum of a company limited by guarantee must state the amount of the members' guarantee. The articles may state that members have differing 'shares' of assets and liabilities and, as such, can be structured as a 'hybrid' . Particulars of members are not filed.

## 1.3 Public company

Formed under the Companies Act 1992, it must be intended for the shares of a public company to be distributed to the public. A public company in the Bahamas must have at least three directors, it must produce annual accounts and have them audited. A list of all directors and officers must be filed with the company's annual return.

## 1.4 International Business Company

The IBC incorporated under the IBCA 1989, now 2000 as amended, is used for offshore asset holding purposes. The company only has IBC status on condition that it does not carry on business, nor hold real estate, in the Bahamas (although it may hold shares in other Bahamian companies). It may have one director and one shareholder (who may or may not be a resident in the Bahamas), and must also have a registered agent (who must reside in the Bahamas). There is no minimum share capital, shares must be registered, not bearer, denominated in any currency. Meetings may be held in the Bahamas, and it can be managed and operated from the Bahamas or elsewhere. The Bahamian government guarantees that an IBC will not attract taxes in the Bahamas for 20 years. The memorandum and articles are the only documents held on the public record. When the IBCA 1989 was passed there was no requirement to file particulars of directors, officers or shareholders. However, under the 2001 and 2004 amendments, IBCs are now required to file the names and addresses of directors, officers and 'beneficial owners' .

## 1.5 Limited duration companies

The limited duration company ('LDC') was introduced by the International Business Companies (Amendment) Act 1994, and the provisions are now found in Pt 10 of the IBCA 2000, as amended. This type of company is essentially an IBC, but (guess what?) it has a limited duration of up to 30 years. The LDC is a structure designed for US citizens as, under US tax legislation, it is treated as a US LLC so does not become liable to US corporation tax at the corporate level. Instead its annual profits flow through to the shareholders.

## 1.6 Bahamas foreign company

A foreign, non-Bahamian company can become registered under the Companies Act 1992 as a 'foreign' company. When granted a Certificate of Registration, the foreign company can operate a 'branch' or undertake business within the Bahamas, but must also obtain a business licence from the Investment Authority.

## 1.7 Segregated account company

Incorporated under the Segregated Account Companies Act 2004, this type of company is structured as a separate legal entity, but one that can segregate assets and liabilities of different accounts (or shares) from assets and liabilities of other accounts (or classes of shares). Each segregated account (or class) is insulated from the others. Assets of one account (or class) are not available to meet liabilities of another account (or class). In other words, creditors of portfolio no.1 cannot attach assets of the company that are held in, or constitute, portfolio no.2. The segregated account company is used by the investment funds industry when an asset management group structures its family of funds under a single 'umbrella' .

## 2. Bermuda

Bermuda is a nil-tax jurisdiction insofar as it does not impose direct income taxes, estate, wealth or capital taxes of any sort upon individuals (however, there is 'payroll' tax, payable by resident employees, which is a type of mandatory pension and social security provision). Nor does it impose corporation or withholding taxes upon companies.

The principal companies legislation in Bermuda is the Companies Act 1981. It underwent significant amendments in 2006 and more minor changes in 2009. The Bermuda Monetary Authority is responsible for corporate registrations.

## 2.1 Local company limited by shares

A local company is a company incorporated under Part 9 of the Companies Act 1981 that must have at least 60% of its shares owned by Bermudian citizens. In these circumstances a local company is allowed to carry on business within Bermuda. It must have at least two directors, who may be corporate and need not be Bermudian. A sole shareholder is permitted. The company must keep accounts at its registered office but they do not have to be filed and they are not, therefore, open to inspection. The register of directors, register of shareholders, memorandum and articles are all held in the public domain and are available for inspection.

## 2.2 Mutual company

A mutual company incorporated under Pt 12 of the Companies Act 1981 is essentially a guarantee company, in respect of which you are referred to sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4.

## 2.3 Bermuda 'permit' company

A foreign, non-Bermudian company can apply to the Ministry of Finance under the Companies Act 1981 to become registered as a 'permit' company. When granted an appropriate 'permit' under Pt 11 of the Act, the company can operate a 'branch' or undertake business within Bermuda.

## 2.4 Exempt company

The name 'exempt' under Pt 10 of the 1981 Act does not derive from the exemption from local corporation tax, as it did in the Channel Islands (because, as a nil-tax haven, Bermuda does not impose corporation taxes), but it derives from the fact that the company is exempt from the 60% local ownership requirement that applies to a company that wishes to carry on business from within Bermuda. Exempt companies must have at least two officers, who cannot be corporate, who reside in Bermuda either two directors, or one director and a secretary or 'permanent representative' . The annual fee for a Bermudian exempt company is expensive!

## 2.5 Mutual fund company

A 'mutual fund company' (as opposed to a 'mutual company') incorporated under Part 12A of the Companies Act 1981 as amended is clearly designed for the Bermudian mutual funds industry. The mutual fund company has the essential characteristics of a segregated account company referred to in Appendix 4, Section 9 and 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund.

## 3. British Virgin Islands (BVI)

The BVI are a nil-tax jurisdiction insofar as they do not impose direct income taxes, estate, wealth or capital taxes of any sort upon individuals. Nor do they impose corporation or withholding taxes upon companies.

Historically, the main corporate entity incorporated in the BVI and used for offshore asset holding purposes was the hugely successful IBC, incorporated under the International Business Companies Act 1984. This legislation shares some of the features of the UK model as well as US companies legislation. The IBCA did not supersede the existing companies law in 1984, but complemented it insofar as it existed alongside the traditional companies legislation, namely the Companies Act 1963 (Cap 285). The Companies Act 1963 was based upon the English 1948 Act and allowed for the incorporation of companies to carry on business in the BVI - they could be public or private, unlimited, limited by shares or guarantee, and became known colloquially as Cap 285 companies.

However, this regime was changed from 2006 by the enactment of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 ('BVI BCA'). The BVI BCA became the only piece of legislation applicable to companies in the BVI, whether public or private, carrying on business in the BVI or elsewhere, for the purposes of commercial trading or asset-holding. Just one type of company is available, namely the BVI Business Company (BVI BC), but this can be:

- limited by shares - the most popular form of BVI BC
- limited by guarantee, whether prohibited from issuing shares (i.e. a standard type of guarantee company) or permitted to issue shares (i.e. a 'hybrid' guarantee company, reviewed in section 8 of Appendix 4)
- with unlimited liability
- with segregated portfolios or cells (pursuant to Pt 7 BVI BCA, an entity similar to the type overviewed in section 1(7) above).

The new BVI BC of whatever type or colour is not subject to local corporation tax, as there is none. It therefore complies with OECD notions of 'fair' taxation principles, and also satisfies the EU Savings Tax Directive and Code of Conduct on Business Taxation (as required by the UK for all its Overseas Territories) insofar as there is no discriminatory treatment between resident and truly 'offshore' companies.

The following types of company formerly available in the BVI have been superseded: the IBC (transitional provisions stated that all existing IBC's convert automatically to BVI BCs by 2008), the public company, the ordinary resident company, the ordinary non-resident company, the company limited by guarantee (both resident and nonresident), and finally the Cap 285 company.

## 4. The Cayman Islands

The Cayman Islands are a nil-tax jurisdiction insofar as they do not impose direct income taxes, estate, wealth or capital taxes of any sort upon individuals. Nor do they impose corporation or withholding taxes upon companies.

The principal companies legislation is the Companies Law 1961, which has been amended many times and was most recently revised in 2020. The Companies Law is largely based upon the English 1948 Act and provides for the incorporation of several types of company.

## 4.1 The ordinary resident company

The ordinary resident company is typically incorporated in order to carry on business in the local economy within the geographic territory of the Cayman Islands. It is also governed by the Local Companies (Control) Law 1995, which states that it must be at least 60% owned by Caymanian citizens or obtain a local license authorising it to trade notwithstanding that it does not have majority Caymanian ownership. Hence the colloquial name given to this type of company is a 'local company' . A list of shareholders must be filed with the Registrar of Companies with the annual return, which is available for inspection. Bearer shares are not permitted. An AGM must be held each year. Accounts must be maintained, although formal financial statements are not mandatory and do not have to be filed, nor is an audit necessary.

## 4.2 The ordinary non-resident company

The ordinary non-resident company is subject to much the same statutory requirements under the Companies Law 2020 Revision as the resident company, except that pursuant to the Local Companies (Control) Law it cannot carry on any business within the jurisdiction of the Cayman Islands. Meetings of directors and of shareholders can take place in the Cayman Islands or abroad, although the annual return contains an undertaking that local business is not being transacted. Registers of directors, officers and shareholders must be kept, but are not filed and they are not in the public domain. The company must keep accounts, but there is no audit or filing requirement. This type of company is used for foreign asset holding purposes as an alternative to the exempt company.

## 4.3 Exempt company

The name 'exempt' does not derive from the exemption from local corporation tax, as it did in the Channel Islands (as the Cayman Islands are a nil-tax haven, and do not impose corporation taxes), but it derives from the fact that the company is exempt from the 60% local ownership requirement that applies to a company that wishes to carry on business from within the Cayman Islands. The exempt company is basically the same as the non-resident company except that, under Pt 7 of the Companies Law, it:

- may issue bearer shares, subject to immobilisation provisions
- does not have to use the word 'Limited' in its name
- has a government guarantee that it will not be subject to corporation taxes for 20 years.

## 4.4 Exempted limited duration company ('LDC')

Under Pt 8 of the Companies Law, the LDC shares some of the characteristics of the Delaware LLC reviewed in Appendix 4, section 10.3 and in 1(5) above and, of course, has a limited duration.

## 4.5 Segregated portfolio company

Under Pt 14 of the Companies Law 2020 Revision, the segregated portfolio company is similar to the segregated account company reviewed in section 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund.

## 4.6 Foreign company

A foreign, non-Caymanian company can apply to the Registrar under Pt 9 of the Companies Law 2020 to become registered as a 'foreign' company. When granted an appropriate certificate of registration under Pt 9 of the Law, the company can operate a 'branch' or undertake business within the Cayman Islands.

## 4.7 Guarantee company

For a description of guarantee companies generally you are referred to sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4, and s.9 of the Companies Law, 2020 Revision.

## 4.8 Limited Liability Company (LLC)

The Cayman Islands LLC is based upon the Delaware template and governed by the Limited Liability Companies Law 2016. As in Delaware, a Caymanian LLC does not have a board of directors, but is managed by a managing member elected by the shareholders. The managing member is typically given all powers of management of the business of the company on a day-to-day basis. It has Articles but, in addition, it is typical for the members and managing member to enter into an LLC Agreement to set out the internal capital structure and rules governing management and administration. Because it enjoys greater flexibility than an ordinary company in terms of its management, organisation and allocation of profits and losses (in a manner similar to a partnership) it is typically used as a vehicle for investment funds, joint venture companies, private equity transactions, securitisations and other international financing structures.

## 5. Gibraltar

Gibraltar was classified by OECD as a 'tax haven' . It would be better described as a not so-low-tax haven. Ordinary companies are subject to Gibraltar corporation tax at a standard rate, which has been, until recently, 35% (with a small company rate of 20% where profits are less than £35,000 p.a.). What makes it a tax haven in the eyes of OECD is that the 'exempt' company is not subject to taxes in Gibraltar. The exempt company is essentially one that does not carry on business in the local economy and is not owned, beneficially or otherwise, by Gibraltarian citizens or residents. This has lead to an interesting legal battle with Europe.

Having regard to OECD and EU criticism of the duplicity of the ordinary private company regime for 'locals' being subject to corporation tax of 35% and the exempt and qualifying company regime for 'foreigners' being essentially exempt from tax, the Gibraltar government proposed to abolish the exempt and qualifying company in 2003 and introduce a new form of corporation tax payable by all Gibraltarian companies. The new corporation tax would not be assessed upon the company's profits in the normal

(EU-recognised) way, but upon the size of its payroll and occupation of business premises.

It was observed that the typical Gibraltarian asset holding company (with no more than a P.O. box in Gibraltar) would not become liable to pay any such tax. This was exactly why it was designed in this way, namely to protect Gibraltar's financial services industry. Accordingly the European Commission brought a lawsuit alleging, amongst other things, that the proposed tax reforms were unfair under 'regional selectivity' because they were advantageous to Gibraltar companies when compared to those incorporated elsewhere in Europe, and in the UK in particular.

Anticipating defeat, and after years of litigation, Gibraltar phased out the exempt and qualifying company by 2010.

In order to preserve Gibraltar's financial service industry some out-of-the-box thinking was required. It could become a nil-tax jurisdiction like, for instance, the Cayman Islands, or become a quasi-nil-corporate-tax jurisdiction and introduce a Zero/10 tax regime like the Channel Islands and the Isle of Man. But, for fiscal reasons specifically applicable to Gibraltar, its expected tax revenues would be insufficient to maintain its public expenditures. Alternatively, Gibraltar could follow the Cyprus model and move to a low rate of corporation tax of 10% that would apply to all Gibraltarian companies across the board.

Negotiations with the EU continued alongside the lawsuits. Announcements of a 10% rate of corporation tax were made then renounced. Then in 2008 the European Court ruled in favour of Gibraltar stating that it could indeed introduce its own tax system, and did not have to adopt the UK model. Accordingly, since 2011 when the Income Tax Act 2010 came into force, a Gibraltarian Ordinary Resident Company pays corporate income tax at the current rate of 10% per annum upon global earnings; whereas a Non-Resident Company (defined as a company incorporated in Gibraltar that does not trade or derive any earnings in or from Gibraltar) pays tax at a nil rate.

Gibraltar now offers the following types of corporate entity.

## 5.1 Private company limited by shares

Companies incorporated in Gibraltar were governed by the Companies Ordinance 1930 as amended, which was based upon the English Companies Acts of 1929 and 1948. The Companies Ordinance was modernised and amended by the introduction of the Companies Act 2014. A private company is one with a maximum of 50 shareholders, particulars of which are filed with the annual return and available for public inspection. A sole director is permitted who may be corporate.  As indicated above, a private company incorporated in Gibraltar may be an Ordinary Resident company or Non-Resident.

- The Ordinary Resident Company is one that carries on business in Gibraltar or whose management and control is exercised in Gibraltar. In these circumstances it pays corporate income tax at the current rate of 10% per annum upon global earnings.
- The Non-Resident Company is a company incorporated in Gibraltar that does not trade or derive any earnings in or from Gibraltar and pays corporate income tax at a nil rate.

## 5.2 Public company limited by shares

A public company is one that is not a private company and must have 'plc' in its name.

## 5.3 Company limited by guarantee

The Gibraltar Companies Ordinance permits companies to be limited by guarantee in the normal way, and to be limited by guarantee but still issue shares as a 'hybrid'. The hybrids can be used as a holding vehicle instead of the discretionary trust see section 8 of Appendix 4.

## 5.4 Exempt company (abolished)

An exempt company used to be a private company limited by shares that applied for and obtained tax-exempt status. In these circumstances it was exempt from Gibraltar corporation tax and dividends were not subject to withholding taxes. It is for these reasons that the exempt company had to be abolished.

## 5.5 Qualifying company (abolished)

This used to be a company incorporated in Gibraltar limited by shares that applied for and obtained 'Qualifying company' status. To qualify the company must not have conducted business in Gibraltar, and satisfied the same conditions that applied to the exempt company. In fact the qualifying company was merely an extension of the exempt company, because if it qualified as such, instead of paying nil tax, it could agree the rate of tax it paid with the Financial and Development Secretary. This was usually between 1% and 35% in order to ensure that the company paid just enough tax to avoid any tax-avoidance rules that apply in the state in which the company carries on its main business. The qualifying company has now been withdrawn under OECD and EU pressure.

## 5.6 Overseas company

A foreign company can obtain permission to carry on its business in or from Gibraltar by registering as an 'overseas' company.

## 6. Guernsey

Guernsey was classified by OECD as a 'tax haven' , but ought to be described as a nil-corporate-tax haven. There is no corporation tax as such. Until 1 January 2008 both individuals and Guernsey companies paid income tax at the rate of 20%. However, Guernsey companies could apply for and obtain tax-exempt status it which case they paid no corporate income tax at all. Tax exemption was essentially conferred when the company did not carry on business in the local economy and was beneficially owned by foreigners, i.e. non-Guernsey citizens or residents.

The dichotomy of one tax regime for 'local, resident companies' and another for 'exempt' companies lead to much criticism by OECD. Clearly OECD wanted Guernsey to impose the same level of corporate income taxes upon both resident and otherwise exempt companies. Seeing the writing on the wall, Guernsey decided to abolish the concept of the exempt company (as well as the international company). The government then surprised OECD by introducing a new 'Zero/10' tax regime from 2008 which applies to companies across the board, irrespective of whether they carry on business in Guernsey or elsewhere. 'Zero/10' refers to the fact that ordinary companies pay corporate income tax at a nil rate, whereas banks and financial service providers regulated by the Guernsey FSC pay tax at 10%.

To coincide with the new tax system, Guernsey enacted new companies legislation in the form of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008, which streamlined incorporation procedures and introduced a modern companies registry. So, for instance, it is now possible to incorporate a Guernsey company by application to the registrar, rather than involving the court.

Under the new Guernsey regime, the exempt company and the international company have been abolished. This leaves the following types of company still available.

## 6.1 Company limited by shares

In respect of Guernsey companies currently available, see Part 1, sections 1 through 9 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008. There is no distinction between private and public companies. A company limited by shares is the bog-standard. A sole director is permitted and there must be a secretary. Corporate procedures are simplified and there are no requirements for the holding of meetings.

## 6.2 Company limited by guarantee

A company limited by guarantee in accordance with s.7 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008 is no different to other guarantee companies, in respect of which see sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4.

## 6.3 Unlimited liability company

Permitted under s.8 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008. There is also a mixed liability 'hybrid' available under s.9.

## 6.4 Cell companies

Originally known as 'protected cell companies' under the Protected Cell Companies Ordinance 1997, these are now governed by Pt 27 of the Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008. A cell company has the essential features that are identical to the segregated portfolio company, in respect of which you are referred to section 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund. There is also a variation called the Incorporated Cell Company under s.4.

## 6.5 Exempt company (abolished 2008)

Prior to 2008, a private company could apply for and obtain tax exempt status under the Income Tax (Exempt Bodies) (Guernsey) Ordinance on condition it did not trade within the local economy. The annual fee was £600. The exempt company has been phased out and any exempt companies became resident for tax purposes on 1 January 2008, so that their profits are liable to tax at the rate of nil% (or 10% if a bank, etc.).

## 6.6 International company (abolished 2008)

Prior to 2008, a private company could apply for and obtain 'international company' status under the Income Tax (International Bodies)(Guernsey) Law 1993. In order to qualify the company had to be resident for tax purposes in Guernsey but must not conduct business with Guernsey citizens or residents. Upon acquiring international company status the company could, instead of paying nil tax as an exempt company, agree the rate of tax it pays with the Guernsey tax authorities. This was usually between 1% and 30% in order to ensure that the company paid just enough tax to avoid any tax-avoidance rules that apply in the state in which it carries on its main business. The international company was abolished as of 1 January 2008.

## 7. Hong Kong

Hong Kong is a low-tax centre. There is no corporation tax as such. Instead, income tax is imposed upon profits made by 'persons' who carry on a trade or profession in or from Hong Kong. 'Persons' in this context includes individuals, and also companies that are incorporated or registered in Hong Kong. Accordingly, both Hong Kong-resident individuals and Hong Kong-resident companies pay income tax (historically, companies at the rate of 16% and individuals at 15%).

The type of corporate entity available in Hong Kong is fairly basic: public, private and registered branch of an overseas company. However, it is the tax treatment of these 'persons' that create tax planning opportunities. Tax is charged on a territorial basis. In other words, corporate income tax is only imposed upon profits made by a Hong Kong company that are actually earned in or derive from Hong Kong. All foreign earned profits, or earnings that derive from outside of Hong Kong, are free from corporate income tax. In these circumstances, a Hong Kong private company that holds and manages income producing assets outside Hong Kong can earn profits tax-free, without incurring any liability in Hong Kong to pay corporate income tax.

The old companies legislation in Hong Kong, namely the Companies Ordinance 1984, consolidated into a 1997 version was based upon the English Acts of 1948 and 1985. However, this was modernised and significantly changed by the introduction of amendments to the Companies Ordinance, Cap 622 in 2014. Hong Kong has never catered for the exempt company nor the BVI style IBC.

## 7.1 Private company limited by shares

A private company incorporated under the Hong Kong Companies Ordinance looks, feels and smells much like an English company incorporated under the 1948 Act. Having said that, the 2014 legislation is a modern equivalent and introduces one or two interesting concepts as well as a considerable amount of detail and additional complexity. The private company limited by shares, in accordance with section 8 Companies Ordinance, Cap 622, 2014, is the type of vehicle used by businessmen for trading purposes within Hong Kong and also by international investors, family offices and fiduciaries for asset holding purposes. There is also available a private company limited by guarantee in accordance with s.9 of the Companies Ordinance, Cap 622, 2014, and an unlimited company in accordance with s.10.

## 7.2 Public company limited by shares

A public company is any company other than a private company as defined in s.12 of the Companies Ordinance, Cap 622, 2014.

## 7.3 Branch of an overseas company

See Pt 16, Divisions 1 and 2, ss.774-795 of the Companies Ordinance, Cap 622, 2014.

## 8. The Isle of Man

Like the Channel Islands, the Isle of Man was classified by OECD as a 'tax haven' . But it ought to be described as a nil-corporate tax haven. There is no corporation tax as such. Until 1 January 2006 both individuals and Manx companies paid income tax (historically companies paid corporate income tax at the rate of 20% but, from 2003, the first £500,000 was taxed at 10% and, in the last year before the introduction of 'Zero/10' in 2006, the rate was 15% for trading companies and 18% for non-trading companies). However, prior to 2006, Manx companies could apply for and obtain tax-exempt status, in which case they paid no corporate income tax at all. Tax exemption was essentially conferred when the company did not carry on business in the local economy and was beneficially owned by foreigners, i.e. non-Manx citizens or residents.

The dichotomy of one tax regime for 'local, resident companies' and another for 'exempt' companies lead to much criticism by OECD. Clearly OECD wanted the Isle of Man to impose the same level of taxes upon both resident and otherwise exempt companies. The Isle of Man therefore decided to abolish the concept of the exempt company (and the non-resident and international company). The Manx government then surprised OECD by introducing a new 'Zero/10' tax regime from 2006 which applies to companies across the board, irrespective of whether they carry on business in the Isle of Man or elsewhere. 'Zero/10' refers to the fact that ordinary companies pay corporate income tax at a nil rate, whereas banks and financial service providers regulated by the Manx FSC pay corporate income tax of 10%.

To coincide with the new tax system, the Isle of Man enacted new companies legislation in the form of the Companies Act 2006. The 2006 Act operates alongside the old Companies Act 1931, as amended from time to time, and introduced a new company, called (rather appropriately) the 'New Manx Vehicle' .

Under the new Manx corporate and tax regime, the exempt company, non-resident company and the international company have been abolished. This leaves the following types of company still available in the Isle of Man.

## 8.1 Private company limited by shares

The Manx Companies Act 1931, as amended, distinguishes between a public and private company. A private company must state it is a private company in its memorandum; it may have a sole shareholder and particulars must be filed in the annual return and are then available for public inspection. A company limited by shares is the bog-standard company based upon the English 1948 Act.

## 8.2 Public company

A public company is one that it not a private company, as defined, and must have 'plc' in its name. It must have a minimum of two shareholders although it is anticipated that the shares will be made available to the investing public.

## 8.3 Company limited by guarantee (and 'hybrid')

A company limited by guarantee in the Isle of Man is no different to other guarantee companies and the company limited by guarantee with shares is the classic 'hybrid' company, in respect of which you are referred to sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4.

## 8.4 Unlimited liability company

Permitted under Pt 4 s.61 of the Companies Act 2006.

## 8.5 Protected cell companies

Protected cell companies' under the Manx Protected Cell Companies Act 2004 have the essential features that are similar to the segregated portfolio company, in respect of which you are referred to 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund.

## 8.6 Limited liability company ('LLC')

The LLC was introduced into Manx law by the Limited Liability Companies Act 1996. It does not have directors, but must have at least two members and a registered agent who is required to reside in the Isle of Man. The internal rules of governance of an LLC are set out in Articles of Organisation. It has a maximum duration of up to 30 years. One of the important features of an LLC is that it is not subject to corporate income tax (even at the new nil rate). Instead it is taxed as a partnership, so that corporate profits flow-through to the members and are taxed in the hands of the individual members. (For a detailed description of an LLC you are referred to Appendix 4, section 10.3).

## 8.7 New Manx Vehicle

The Companies Act 2006 created a new, stream-lined, cheap-and-cheerful, easy-tomanage company, which has become known as the 'New Manx Vehicle' . Each such company is given a company number in the normal way, but it contains the suffix 'V' to distinguish it from the ordinary company incorporated under the Companies Act 1931. The New Manx Vehicle is used for asset holding purposes and is therefore taxed at the zero rate of corporate income tax applicable in the Isle of Man. It is similar to the BVI BC.

## 8.8 Exempt company (abolished 2006)

Prior to 2006, a Manx private company could apply for and obtain tax exempt status under the Income Tax (Exempt Companies) Act 1984 on condition that it did not trade within the local economy and was not owned, beneficially or otherwise, by Manx citizens or residents and had no Manx income. The annual fee was £475. The exempt company was phased out and any existing exempt companies became resident for tax purposes on 1 January 2007, so that their profits are now liable to tax at the nil rate (or 10% if a bank, etc.).

## 8.9 International company (abolished 2006)

Prior to 2006, a Manx private company could apply for and obtain 'international company' status under the International Business Act 1994. In order to qualify the company must have been resident for tax purposes in the Isle of Man, but not conduct business with Manx citizens or residents so that its income was derived from outside the Isle of Man. Upon acquiring international company status the company could, instead of paying nil tax as an exempt company, agree the rate of tax it paid to the Manx tax authorities. This was usually between 1% and 30% in order to ensure that the company paid just enough tax to avoid any tax-avoidance rules that apply in the state in which it carries on its main business. The international company was abolished as of 1 January 2006.

## 8.10 Non-resident company (abolished 2006)

A private Manx company could apply to become non-resident by submitting a Declaration of Non-Residency, upon condition that its central management and control was exercised overseas, namely outside the geographical territory of the Isle of Man. In these circumstances it was not liable to pay Manx corporate income tax. For the reasons explained in Appendix 4, section 3, they have been phased out and ultimately abolished in 2006.

## 9. Jersey

Jersey, like Guernsey, was classified by OECD as a 'tax haven' . However, like Guernsey and the Isle of Man, it ought to be described as a nil-corporate-tax centre. There is no corporation tax as such. Until 2008 both Jersey-resident individuals and Jersey-resident companies paid income tax at the rate of 20%. However, Jersey companies could apply for and obtain tax-exempt status in which case they paid no corporate income tax at all. Tax exemption was essentially conferred when the company did not carry on business in the local economy and was beneficially owned by foreigners, i.e. non-Jersey citizens or residents.

The dichotomy of one tax regime for 'local, resident companies' and another for 'exempt' companies lead to much criticism by OECD. Clearly OECD wanted Jersey to impose the same level of corporate income taxes upon both resident and otherwise exempt companies. Accordingly, Jersey (like Guernsey and the Isle of Man, which had similar regimes) decided to abolish the concept of the exempt company (as well as the international business company). The government then introduced a new 'Zero/10' tax regime by the Income Tax (Amendment No.28)(Jersey) Law 2007 which, from 1 January 2009, applies to companies across the board, irrespective of whether they carry on business in Jersey or elsewhere. 'Zero/10' refers to the fact that ordinary companies pay corporate income tax at a nil rate, whereas banks and financial service providers regulated by the Jersey FSC pay tax at 10%. This clearly resulted in lost tax revenues so, in order to bridge the gap, Jersey introduced a 'goods and sales tax' ('GST').

Under the new Jersey corporate income tax regime, the exempt company and the international business company have been abolished. This leaves the following types of company still available.

## 9.1 Private company limited by shares

In respect of Jersey companies currently available, see Pt 2, arts 3A-3I of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended from time to time and consolidated in 2019. There remains a distinction between private companies (art.3A) and public companies (art.3B). A company limited by shares is the bog-standard and is used for asset holding purposes. A sole director is permitted and there must be a secretary. Corporate procedures are simplified and there are no requirements for the holding of meetings.

## 9.2 Public companies limited by shares

See the above and art.3B of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended and consolidated 2019.

## 9.3 Company limited by guarantee

A company limited by guarantee in Jersey under art.3G of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991 as amended, is no different to other guarantee companies, in respect of which see sections 7 and 8 of Appendix 4.

## 9.4 Unlimited liability company

Permitted under art.3D of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991 as amended and revised 2019.

## 9.5 Par value and no-par value companies

See arts 3E and 3F of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended.

## 9.6 Limited Life companies

See art.3H of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended.

## 9.7 Cell companies

Sometimes known as 'protected cell companies' , these are now available under art.3I, and are governed by Pt 18D of the Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, as amended. The Jersey cell company has all of the essential features that apply to the segregated portfolio company, in respect of which see section 1(7) above. They are used as a holding structure for a particular type of investment fund.

## 9.8 Exempt company (abolished 2009)

Prior to June 2008, a Jersey private company could apply to the Comptroller of Income Tax and obtain tax exempt status under the Income Tax (Jersey) Law 1961 on condition it did not trade within the local economy. The annual fee was £600. The exempt company has been phased out and any existing exempt companies on 3 June 2009

became resident for tax purposes, so that their profits are liable to tax at the nil rate (or 10% if a bank, etc.).

## 9.9 International business company (abolished 2011)

Prior to January 2006, a Jersey private company could apply for and obtain 'international business company' status (not to be confused with the BVI company of the same name). In order to qualify the company must have been resident in Jersey for tax purposes but not conduct business with Jersey citizens or residents. Upon acquiring international business company status the company could, instead of paying nil tax as an exempt company, agree the rate of tax it pays with the Jersey tax authority. This was usually between 1% and 30% in order to ensure that the company paid just enough tax to avoid any tax-avoidance rules that apply in the state in which it carries on its main business. The Jersey version of the international business company was completely abolished in 2011.

## 10. Singapore

Singapore is a not-so-low-tax centre. Corporation tax at the rate of 17% (as of 2017) is imposed upon profits made by companies that are resident in Singapore (essentially incorporated in Singapore or that are managed and controlled from Singapore). The type of corporate entity available in Singapore is fairly basic: public, private and registered branch of an overseas company. However, it is the tax treatment of these companies that create tax planning opportunities. Corporation tax is charged on a territorial basis. In other words, corporation tax is only imposed upon earnings made by a Singaporean company that are actually earned in, or derive from, or are received in, Singapore. All foreign earned profits, or earnings that derive from sources outside Singapore, are free from corporation tax. In these circumstances, a Singaporean private company that holds and manages income producing assets outside Singapore can earn profits tax-free, without incurring any liability in Singapore to pay corporation tax. Singapore is also an attractive jurisdiction to establish a group holding company.

The companies legislation in Singapore, namely the Companies Act, Cap 50 consolidated 2017, is based upon the English 1948 and 1985 Acts, although a number of amendments were introduced in 2016 prior to the publication of a consolidated version of Cap 50 in 2017. Singapore has never catered for the exempt company nor BVI style IBC.

## 10.1 Private company limited by shares

A private company incorporated under the Singapore Companies Act Cap 50 is very similar to a private company incorporated under the English 1948 Act. This is the type of vehicle used by businessmen for trading purposes within Singapore, and also by international investors, family offices and fiduciaries for asset holding purposes. It is also used as a group holding structure.

The Exempt Private Company (EPC) must have less than 20 shareholders and enjoys certain exemptions, such as no audit requirement and accounts do not need to be filed with the Accounting and Corporate Regulatory Authority (ACRA).

## 10.2 Public company limited by shares

A public company is any company other than a private company, as defined in s.18 of the Companies Act, Cap 50.

## 10.3 Company limited by guarantee

Usually carry out not-for-profit activities; must register with ACRA, have its accounts audited and filed.

## 10.4 Branch of an overseas company

See Pt 11, Division 2, ss.365-386 of the Companies Act, Cap 50.
