## Module 2: Characteristics of a company

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- define a company
- outline the evolution of company law in England and assess its effect on company law in other common law jurisdictions, particularly the offshore financial centres
- consider some of the fundamental features of a company and distinguish companies from other forms of undertaking

- analyse the arguments for and against the integrity of 'one-person' companies and the limited liability of shareholders

## Introduction

In this module we look at how company law developed in England, as this forms the basis of the company law adopted in the common law jurisdictions of the world and in most of the offshore financial centres.

You do not need a detailed knowledge of the company law enacted in the UK. This module will provide a brief outline of the development of company law generally and place the role of the company in an historical context.

There are five sections. Section 1 analyses a traditional definition of a company; section 2 describes the historical evolution of company law from medieval England to its modern structure; section 3 introduces modern companies legislation enacted in various common law offshore jurisdictions; section 4 reviews the features and advantages of incorporation generally; and section 5 reviews some of the uses made of, and the advantages that accrue to, companies incorporated and managed offshore.

## 1. Definition of a company

A company is traditionally defined as a group of persons who organise themselves together to pursue a common objective or purpose that usually involves the carrying on of a trade or business for profit. 4

This definition is traditional insofar as it is most applicable to a company incorporated onshore. An analysis of the definition reveals that it does not travel well to the offshore financial jurisdictions. 5  Companies incorporated offshore are characteristically not owned and controlled by a group of persons - they often have only one shareholder, who also provides the company directors. Offshore companies do not typically carry on a trade or business in the traditional sense, i.e. buying materials, manufacturing goods and products, subsequent sales, etc.; they may simply hold investments. Offshore companies do not necessarily operate in the pursuit of profit in the traditional sense - they may exist for other primary objectives, unrelated to concerns for profit, for example they may be part of a structure designed to achieve asset protection, confidentiality, tax efficiency, estate planning, etc.

Offshore companies, in reality, provide a structure whereby assets 'belonging' to a high-net-worth individual (HNWI) or other entity are held and managed by another. As such, they resemble a trust where, as you will know, property is held and managed by trustees for the benefit of the beneficiaries. A company, however, is underpinned by a totally different legal regime: one developed in England from the 17th century onwards, primarily by statute (rather than by common law and equity), to meet the demands of industry and commerce.

In a colloquial or business context, the term 'company' may be used to include any form of business organisation, i.e. including sole proprietorships, partnerships 6  and unincorporated associations, 7  as well as incorporated entities. We shall, in this study text, use the word 'company' as meaning a corporation or corporate entity that has been registered, incorporated and exists under the provisions of relevant companies legislation.

Companies legislation confers upon a company a legal personality of its own. A company is said to be a separate legal entity, distinct from its owners (the shareholders or members). Decisions affecting the business of the company are made, not by the owners of the company, but by its board of directors. At its simplest, a company is owned by its members, but the business is run by its directors. We shall explore how and why this dichotomy developed as we review the historical evolution of the company in section 2.2 of this module.

A further characteristic of a company is the limitation of the liability of shareholders for company debts. The responsibility for payment of the debts of a company rests with the company itself. The shareholders cannot generally be required to pay any debts incurred by the company, even if the company is insolvent and unable to pay its debts in full. The liability of shareholders is said to be limited.

Owing to the possibilities for abuse of the principles of corporate personality and limited liability, a regulatory framework has developed. The regulatory framework provided by companies legislation requires a company to observe fundamental rules designed to protect shareholders, investors, creditors and persons having dealings with the company, and the public at large. Before we embark on a detailed review of the development of company law, it is probably worthwhile repeating some basic, but important, principles.

The objective of this diploma course is to review the applicable law and develop the administration skills required by a company management practitioner operating in the offshore environment. It therefore concentrates on offshore companies legislation and practices. There are many aspects of offshore company legislation and many fundamental corporate principles and practice that make sense only when you track their historical development and explore the rationale behind the various corporate concepts.

The companies legislation enacted in a number of offshore jurisdictions is based, to a large extent, upon the onshore legislation that existed in England in 1948. 8 Accordingly, in order to understand modern offshore company legislation, principles and practices, you need to know how company law developed in England, so that current rules and regulations can be viewed in context.

English company law itself has, of course, moved on from the 1948 Act and was substantially modernised by the Companies Act 2006. This legislation does two things:

- it is both a complete codification of company legislation enacted between 1948 and 1989
- it contains radical new provisions, many of which are designed to simplify the management and regulation of private companies.

## 2. Development of modern company law

## 2.1 The early sole trader and partnerships

The earliest form of business medium in medieval England was the sole trader. A sole trader is an individual who runs a business alone and on his or her own behalf. The sole trader:

- owns all the assets of the business
- makes all the decisions affecting it
- is entitled to all the profits
- is liable for all the losses, i.e. the sole trader must pay all the debts incurred by the business without limit.

The main disadvantages of the sole proprietorship as a business medium at this time included:

- undue risk (or at least the inability to share risk)
- the inability of the business to raise capital to finance its operations of an amount beyond the actual wealth of the individual proprietor.

In order to spread business risk and enable a trader to raise additional capital to finance an undertaking (or its expansion), the sole proprietor in medieval England often elected to take on partners and form an association called a societas , a structure similar to what is now known as a partnership. It consisted of two or more individuals who combined together to run their business jointly for their mutual benefit.

Many 16th-century business organisations were formed as a society of persons, or partnership. Each partner would execute a partnership agreement under seal. The partnership agreement regulated the relationship of the partners to one another and often divided the business into portions where the interests of the original partners were either expressly made non-transferable or, occasionally, were only transferable to third parties subject to restrictive conditions, such as a requirement that all the partners must give their consent to a transfer of one partner's interest to another.

This form of business medium, in itself, suffered from major drawbacks:

- the prohibition or restrictions upon the ability of partners to transfer their shares in the enterprise; a partner who wished to transfer their own share of the business without the consent of the remaining partners would have to dissolve the partnership
- any capital to finance the business and its expansion could be raised only by the partners making contributions to the common pot; the partnership structure did not allow the business to invite third parties from the public to invest in it.

## 2.2 The evolution of companies

## 2.2.1  Before the South Sea Bubble (pre-1720)

The growth of foreign trade conducted by an expanding merchant shipping fleet in England in the 16th century was an important factor in the evolution of the modern company as a business medium.

English merchants began to trade with their foreign counterparts all around the globe, but they did so on their own behalf and on their own account as sole proprietors. Later in the century, the merchants formed 'companies' of merchants by agreeing to trade in particular commodities in accordance with the rules of the 'company' . This was an extension of the guild principle to foreign, as opposed to merely domestic, trade. Nonetheless, the trade of each individual member of the company was kept entirely separate from the others. In other words, the 'company' as such did not trade on its own account as a separate personality.

At this stage in English history, the Crown actively encouraged foreign trade for political (colonisation) purposes. It often granted Royal Charters to companies of merchants, not in order for a company to acquire an independent legal personality to enable it to trade on its own behalf, but to confer upon the company a monopoly for its members to trade in a particular geographical territory.

The next development was crucial. Instead of each member trading on their own behalf, members started to trade jointly, i.e. with inventory owned jointly by the members of the company.

The often-quoted example of this type of undertaking is the East India Company, which in 1600 was granted a Royal Charter to trade as a monopoly with the Indian sub-continent. Originally, each member of the company conducted trade privately on their own account. Thereafter members traded jointly for their mutual benefit they each subscribed an agreed sum by way of initial investment and received 'joint stock' , 9 by way of a security document setting out their rights and liabilities. Profits from the trade were initially distributed among members after each voyage according to the amount invested. Soon, instead of a distribution of profits occurring after each voyage, profits were shared after a number of voyages or at the end of a 'financial year' . Eventually, ownership of permanent joint stock was introduced, but this presented problems of liquidity. What if any members wanted to sell their joint stock? There had to be a way of selling a member's interest in joint stock. This was eventually achieved by what was to become the London Stock Exchange, 10  which opened in 1773, but not before some spectacular incidents that are worth a passing mention.

At the end of the 17th and early in the 18th century, British commercial activity was flourishing. Both domestic and foreign trading companies - some having been given monopoly privileges by the Crown, others existing as bare joint-stock enterprises were proliferating. The structure of these new organisations was favoured because it enabled entrepreneurs to acquire much-needed capital to finance their ventures by way of direct investment by the public. There was an immense growth of public offerings of new shares. 11  Speculative investment in these new companies was common and it has been described as a period of incredible gambling mania. 12  And, as we now know, boom is followed by bust. This particular bubble burst in 1720 with the help of the financial collapse of the infamous South Sea Company, which occurred in the following manner.

In 1711 a company headed by the Lord Treasurer, Robert Harley, obtained a Royal Charter to trade in Spanish South America in the name of the South Sea Company. The company then entered into a complex financing deal with the English government, which had borrowed significantly to fund the War of Spanish Succession. The war did not end until 1713. The company agreed to assume a proportion of the government debt, namely £10 million of government bonds that paid a high rate of interest (6% p.a.) in return for a perpetual annuity of £500,000. The company then persuaded bondholders (by making extravagant, unsubstantiated claims relating to the anticipated success of the business of the company and its expected profits) to convert their bonds into shares in the company. Most bondholders converted their bonds into equity in this manner and made a fantastic short-term profit as the share price rose from the conversion price of £100 per share to almost £1,000. The whole stock market surged upwards in a bull market.

## 2.2.2  After the South Sea Bubble: 1720-1844

The bubble burst in a stock crash in 1720. This was precipitated by a run on the South Sea Company. Investors panicked and the company was unable to meet its liabilities. The whole enterprise collapsed. A significant number of investors lost a lot of money. The government was embarrassed. An investigation revealed widespread fraud and castigated the company and its officers, the government and its ministers and other leading figures of the day. Of considerable importance to students of company law, the English Parliament also questioned the desirability and legality of the corporate structure that had permitted the South Sea Company to defraud so many investors.

As a result of the South Sea Company debacle, the government enacted the Bubble Act in 1720. 13  Rather than legislating constructively by regulating companies and their management, to safeguard shareholders and the public against fraud and other abuse, the Act simply prohibited the use of companies as a legal structure and made their activities illegal. This effectively set back the development of company law for over a century. It caused a resurgence of the old type of business medium, the unincorporated partnership, with all its disadvantages:

- It did not have a separate legal entity, and could not therefore own property except in the name of all the partners; this caused considerable difficulty, particularly where there was to be a change of partners.
- Shares in a company were freely transferable, unlike a partner's share of the partnership, which could be transferred to a third party (if at all) only with the consent of the other partners.
- A company could raise unlimited amounts of capital from the investing public to finance its trade and expansion, whereas a partnership could generally call only on its members for investment.

The 18th century saw the dawn of the Industrial Revolution in England. Business enterprises required lawful and satisfactory legal structures that met the needs of commerce. Unincorporated associations were far from satisfactory. The Bubble Act had outlawed the joint stock company, which merely left, as an alternative corporate structure, those companies incorporated by Royal Charter or by private Act of Parliament. Charters and Acts were difficult and costly to obtain.

The onset of industrialisation and, in particular, the growth of the railway companies, which required significant amounts of capital investment, cried out for some constructive legislative measures that would permit a corporate structure to exist legally, specifically one that:

- could be formed without the need to petition the Crown for the grant of a Royal Charter (which was hardly ever granted) or without the need to obtain incorporation by the enactment of a private Act of Parliament (a prohibitively expensive, awkward and time-consuming procedure)
- could have a separate legal personality so it could own property and enter into or acquire its own contracts, etc.
- would be able to raise money from the public by issuing shares of stock to finance its business
- could ensure adequate protection existed to safeguard those persons who are most vulnerable, namely the investors and the creditors of the company
- could confer a limitation upon the potential liability that might be incurred by investors.

## 2.2.3  1844 to 1907

In 1844, the English Parliament enacted the Joint Stock Companies Act. 14  This was the first piece of modern companies legislation. Most of the principles found in the 1844 Act still form the basis of modern company law today.

It put the company structure on a sound legal foundation by:

- allowing an entity to be incorporated through a relatively simple registration procedure (rather than by Royal Charter or Act of Parliament), whereby prescribed documents were to be filed with a new public official, the registrar of companies, who could confer corporate privileges (such as those set out below) provided that new statutory requirements were complied with
- permitting the new type of registered company to have a separate legal personality which could endure indefinitely, and to raise money from the public by selling its shares
- allowing shares in the company to be freely transferable without the consent of the other members (a requirement of unincorporated associations and partnerships) safeguarding investors and others to prevent fraud by requiring a regime of transparency to be put in place, so that potential investors are not misled when contemplating whether to buy shares and potential creditors of the company are forewarned that they are proposing to deal with a company.

Surprisingly, the Joint Stock Company Act 1844 did not permit limited liability. The owners of shares in a registered company were still personally liable for the debts of the company, although creditors of the company had to sue the company first, and only if the company was unable to pay could they then seek payment from the shareholders.

It was not until 11 years after the Joint Stock Company Act was passed that limited liability for shareholders was introduced by legislation. The world of commerce had lobbied for this change as a crucial provision for boosting investment in the new regulated companies by making it less risky and therefore more attractive to the investing public. Limited liability, it was argued, would reduce risk for investors. This would enable companies to raise capital from a whole new breed of more cautious capitalist, and thus make capital easier to obtain and, therefore, cheaper. In theory, this would finance the continued progress of industrialisation, and facilitate the development of new enterprises and the expansion of old ones.

The concept of limited liability was not without its critics. It was forcefully argued that the limited liability of owners of these new regulated companies would encourage enterprises to trade beyond their means, and would cause businesses to be reckless and speculative with other people's money. Limited liability effectively transfers risk from the investor to the creditors of the company. Lawful creditors of a company who had traded with the company in good faith might be left without redress if the company were rendered insolvent. Was it fair to transfer risk to the unsuspecting creditor and away from the capitalist investor in this way?

These arguments were swept aside, however, in favour of economic progress. The rationale for permitting limited liability was to reduce risk to the investor and thus encourage investment. It was clear that unlimited liability acted as a 'chill' on investment and that therefore a limitation of liability was necessary for a successful and well-financed economy to grow and develop. Accordingly, the Limited Liability Act 1855 was passed.

This allowed a registered joint stock company (with at least five members) to attain limited liability provided that the company observed certain statutory safeguards to protect potential creditors, such as:

- the inclusion of the word 'Limited' (or 'Ltd') in its name to warn potential creditors that they were dealing with a company and could not obtain payment of debts from the shareholders (this is why English companies still use the abbreviation 'Ltd' , rather than 'Inc' which is used in the US)
- the compulsory appointment of approved auditors to prepare and publish annual accounts
- the adherence to a doctrine of capital maintenance, 15  whereby directors were subject to sanctions if they paid unlawful dividends or other distributions of capital to shareholders.

Accordingly, by 1855 the principles that underpin modern company law had been extensively debated and resolved. It was deemed imperative that the public be encouraged to invest money in industry to form an association between the entrepreneur and the capitalist. Although the capitalist shareholders were to own the enterprise, they were not expected to run it, and they were not liable for the debts of the company. The constitution of the company was established in the memorandum and articles of association of the company, which had to comply with the requirements of applicable companies legislation.

Further companies legislation, enacted in England between 1862 and 1989, 16 developed these corporate principles and consolidated earlier Acts. During this period, detailed reviews of company law were carried out approximately every 20 years or so. The most comprehensive 'modern' reviews undertaken in England were before the enactment of the Companies Act 1948, the so-called Jenkins Committee review before the Companies Act 1967 and the Company Law Review, which started in 1998. In this most recent review, the President of the Board of Trade produced a two-volume report in 2001 which led to the introduction of the Companies Act 2006.

Nonetheless, the most important statutory provision so far as offshore corporate practitioners are concerned is still the 1948 Act, as this is the statute upon which a substantial part of offshore companies legislation is based.

## 2.2.4  1907 to date

Until the Companies Act 1907, only one type of company could exist under English companies legislation - the basic joint stock limited liability company. This emerged to enable business to raise substantial amounts of capital from the public who, upon becoming shareholders and paying for their shares in full, were protected against incurring further liability for the debts of the business by the concept of limited liability. The shareholders, as a group, would then own the company, but the business would be run by their elected agents, the directors, subject to certain safeguards to prevent the business from being run in a fraudulent manner by a rogue director. This type of legal structure and method of doing business was of significant benefit to the manufacturing, transport (shipping and railways) and construction industries, where large amounts of capital were required together with extensive specialist expertise.

The corporate structure was not, however, without drawbacks. The benefits of incorporation came at a cost, including the requirement to comply with statutory provisions for administration and accounting. These provisions were aimed at minimising the opportunity to operate the company in a fraudulent manner, to the prejudice of investors, shareholders and creditors. The statutory requirements under English companies legislation included:

- obligatory publicity requirements that required directors to publish financial statements of the company on an annual basis together with details of directors' remuneration, with the consequential loss of privacy
- long-winded, time-consuming and inflexible internal procedures (such as meetings, minutes, audits, publication of accounts, capital maintenance, etc.) to deal with the separation and balance of power between ownership (shareholders) and management/control (directors) and for the protection of creditors
- the expense of incorporation, in professional fees, and, after incorporation, in the burdensome cost of compliance.

In the case of a small business operating in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, which did not require large amounts of capital to finance its activities, the disadvantages of incorporation outweighed the advantages. The compliance requirements, to protect investors and those who dealt with the company, were considered too onerous for a small enterprise.

Accordingly, the Companies Act 1907 amended the existing companies legislation to recognise that some small businesses would prefer to incorporate with limited liability but that, because of their nature, all the burdensome compliance requirements were not necessary. For instance, a small business has no need to raise capital from the public - the owner's own capital is often enough to meet requirements. There is, therefore, no need to separate ownership of the company from control. If the owner runs the business there is no need for checks and balances, or to adhere to safeguards designed to prevent rogue directors from abusing their position at the expense of the shareholders.

The 1907 Act therefore distinguished business undertakings with a single or few proprietors (designated 'private companies') from large, multi-shareholder entities (designated 'public companies'). 17  Private companies were characterised as companies that did not offer shares to the public, the shareholders were limited in number and generally also comprised the board of directors. Public companies were the converse: they were characterised as companies that offered their shares to the public, had numerous shareholders and - although the shareholders elected the board - had directors who were generally full-time paid executives who did not own (or who owned very few) shares in the company. The fact that a company is a public company does not mean that its shares are listed on a stock-exchange - but if any company does want an initial public offering (IPO)/company flotation, it cannot do so unless it is or becomes a public company.

The 1907 Act relaxed a number of the legal compliance requirements for a private company. This type of company was allowed a number of exemptions from the statutory requirements that a public company had to observe.

A private company, if authorised by its articles, could for instance:

- avoid the need to compile and publish formal annual accounts, have the accounts audited, and lay the accounts before the shareholders at their annual general meeting (AGM)
- avoid formal meetings by employing the written resolution procedure, allowing company (or directors') resolutions to be passed in written form without the necessity of a formal meeting.

Accordingly, from 1907 a private company could obtain a number of exemptions from the strict requirements appropriate for a public company. A small enterprise that did not require public participation to raise capital could obtain all the advantages of operating its business under a corporate structure but without having to comply with onerous accounting and administrative requirements aimed at protecting third parties. The concept of the private company has, of course, been embraced offshore for asset-holding purposes.

## 2.2.5  The historical importance of English company law

It is not an overstatement to say that the limited liability company has been a major influence on the industrial and commercial developments throughout the world over the last 200 years. English company law has been used as a model in all common law countries including, of course, the USA, Canada, India, Uganda, Ghana, Australia, New Zealand and the rest.

Note, however, that companies legislation does not provide an all-encompassing code of company law. It is a high-level framework that is supplemented by common law and equitable principles found in case law.

## 3. Development of modern companies legislation enacted offshore

The company law and related legislation enacted in the UK has, to a large extent, been used as a model and implemented by those offshore financial centres that have a common law background. In fact, the English statutory influence goes back to the Joint Stock Company Act 1856.

There follows a list of a sample of financial centres that have enacted companies legislation inspired by the English statutory model, most notably the Companies Act 1948.

- The Bahamas : Companies Act 1992 (Cap. 308), as amended for domestic resident companies; the International Business Companies Act 2000 (Cap. 309), as amended for asset-holding companies
- Bermuda : Companies Act 1981 (Cap. 59), as amended and consolidated 2020
- British Virgin Islands (BVI) : the BVI Business Companies Act, No.16 of 2004, as amended and consolidated
- Cayman Islands : Companies Law (Cap. 22) (2022 Revision)
- Gibraltar : Companies Act 2014
- Guernsey : Companies (Guernsey) Law 2008 as amended and consolidated
- Hong Kong : Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622) 2014
- Isle of Man : Companies Act 1931 as amended for local companies; and the Companies Act 2006 as amended for asset-holding companies
- Jersey : Companies (Jersey) Law 1991, Consolidated Revision 2019
- Mauritius : Companies Act 2001, as amended
- Singapore : Companies Act (Cap. 50) as amended, current version 2020
- Turks and Caicos Islands : Companies Ordinance Ch.16.08, revised 2014.

Legislators in the offshore centres mentioned above have carefully selected those early English provisions that they believe meet the needs of their centre and, where appropriate, they have also fashioned their own provisions and regulations. What you find, therefore, when reviewing the company laws of most offshore financial centres, is a combination of English-influenced provisions and an assortment of locally driven provisions that are designed to enable the centre to compete in the company management services marketplace.

The company law enacted in the UK, as well as in the various offshore financial centres, is designed to provide detailed and considered direction on how the affairs of a company should be conducted, both internally and in its dealings with the outside world.

The main features and underlying principles of companies legislation enacted in all the common law offshore financial centres are largely identical. Nonetheless, while there are significant similarities, there are also differences. For example, do not assume that a Bahamian international business company (IBC) is identical to a BVI business company (BC). There are differences.

Furthermore, company law does not stand still. It continues to develop. English company law has moved towards that of its European neighbours (but may soon depart). Developments in the US are being taken on board by Canada, Australia, New Zealand and other jurisdictions in the common law world. Offshore companies legislation and regulation are being constantly reviewed by the international community and pressure is brought, by bodies such as the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), to tighten regulation. 18

As competition between the offshore financial centres intensifies, further changes to their company laws are inevitable as certain centres seek to maintain or develop a competitive advantage.

Financial planning techniques are increasing in complexity, often in response to moves in onshore jurisdictions to reduce or close loop-holes that have previously existed. Many planners are taking a more aggressive view on how their clients' affairs should be structured to provide maximum protection and benefit. They often try to achieve their aims by using corporate structures in new and advanced ways.

Legislators in offshore centres often work closely with industry experts to assess the benefits and possible shortcomings in the laws that are in place. They collaborate to produce revisions, amendments, consolidations, and so on. Sometimes a new form of corporate entity may emerge, intended to appeal to a particular type of client, with a view to increasing the competitive advantage of the centre and of the service providers in that centre.

The provision of company management services, and how they are regulated, particularly in offshore financial centres, has created much debate in recent years. The various reviews that have been undertaken in a number of jurisdictions have triggered changes in administration practices. This has necessitated alterations to company legislation. 19

The provision of company management services can be a demanding industry in which to work, as the law and requirements seem to be constantly changing. It is important that company administrators keep up to date with developments, not only in the centre where they practise but also in the industry as a whole, both onshore and offshore.

## 4. Features and advantages of incorporation generally

Incorporation confers important wide-ranging benefits upon both onshore and offshore companies. We will now summarise these benefits and comment upon the typical uses made of offshore companies.

## 4.1 Separate legal personality

The fundamental feature of a company is its corporate personality. This is the key feature that leads to all the other advantages.

A company is regarded in law as having a separate legal personality. It is often said that a company is an artificial person and has an existence of its own, quite distinct from that of its members. As a result of its separate corporate existence, a company may, in its own name (not that of its shareholders):

- own property
- conduct business
- enter into deals and transactions
- open and operate a bank account
- agree to be bound by contract
- sue in a court of law
- be held liable, for instance, for breach of contract, the commission of a tort or a crime.

The acts of a company are not regarded as the acts of its members. A third party cannot, as a general rule, go behind the 'veil of incorporation' and attack the shareholders personally for acts done, or debts incurred, by the company.

The concept of corporate personality was firmly established in the often-quoted case of Salomon v A Salomon &amp; Co Ltd. 20  The facts, arguments developed by each side and rationale behind the decision are particularly relevant to offshore corporate practice, as they relate to the integrity and validity of a 'one-man' company, a common feature of many offshore corporate structures.

Mr Salomon was a sole trader who manufactured boots and shoes. His business was profitable and in 1892 Mr Salomon formed a registered company, A Salomon &amp; Co Ltd, and transferred his business to it at a sale price of £39,000. The price was made up of £9,000 in cash and £20,000 represented by 20,000 fully paid up shares of £1 each, issued by the company to Mr Salomon and his nominees. The remaining £10,000 was left owing, secured by a loan note and mortgage over the assets of the company (in Britain called a debenture) in favour of Mr Salomon.

At the time when the company was registered, the applicable legislation required a company to have a minimum of seven shareholders. Accordingly, Mr Salomon received 19,994 shares and a single share was issued to each of his wife, his daughter and four sons, each of whom agreed to hold one share as nominee for Mr Salomon.

Soon after its incorporation the company experienced financial difficulties, suffered losses and the court ordered the company to be wound up. At the conclusion of the winding-up process, the company owed £7,000 to unsecured creditors against assets of £6,000. The loan note and mortgage in favour of Mr Salomon of £10,000 was also outstanding, although Mr Salomon had sold his rights under it to another party. The holder of the loan note therefore sought to take the remaining assets of the company in satisfaction of the monies owed under the note, leaving the unsecured creditors with nothing.

The unsecured creditors questioned the validity of the loan note and mortgage by claiming that the legal structure of the company lacked integrity and was no more than a sham . It was argued that:

- Mr Salomon remained the real proprietor of the business after its incorporation.
- The company, A Salomon &amp; Co Ltd, was a 'sham' , as evidenced by the fact the Mr Salomon in reality held all the shares, and that the requirement (at that time under existing company legislation) of a minimum of seven shareholders was satisfied only as Mr Salomon's family held single shares as bare nominees on behalf of their spouse and father.
- The company was no more than an alias or alter ego of Mr Salomon and, as such, he should pay all outstanding trade debts incurred by the company as if he were a sole proprietor, without the benefit of limited liability.

The courts of first instance and on first appeal were impressed by these arguments. They held that the company was in fact Mr Salomon's alter ego and ordered Mr Salomon to pay the debts incurred by the company out of his own pocket. Nonetheless, the House of Lords, in the final appeal, held that:

- The company had been validly incorporated, complying with all the companies legislation registration requirements.
- The constitution and the company's corporate structure complied with the requirements of the legislation (which, back in 1897, required seven members, but did not stipulate that they had to be independent or actively and independently exercise their rights as shareholders). This is a very important aspect of the decision in that it sanctioned the use of nominee shareholders.
- The company was valid - a 'one-man company' was not an abuse of the companies legislation nor a 'sham' , provided all the requirements of the relevant statutory provisions had been complied with; the House of Lords said that a shareholder cannot 'be liable in any shape or form [for the debts of the company] except to the extent and in the manner provided by the Companies Act' .

- As the company was valid, therefore, the holder of the loan note and mortgage was entitled to be paid from the remaining assets of the company and the unsecured creditors were left with nothing.

The Salomon case confirmed that, provided the relevant legislation has been complied with, a company is lawful and will obtain, upon incorporation, a separate legal personality. Furthermore, incorporators such as Mr Salomon may not only obtain the benefit of limited liability, they may also take a valid security over assets upon making a loan to the company. 21

The legal existence of the company as an independent entity is separated from its members by a ' veil of incorporation ' . Creditors of, or claimants from, the company cannot go behind or lift the veil in order to pursue their debts or claims against the shareholders in their personal capacity.

Assume that Mr Salomon had not sold his interest in the loan note to the third party. Would Mr Salomon, in his personal capacity as shareholder and holder of the note, have been able to resist the claims of the unsecured creditors of the company? Would the outcome of the case have been any different?

Yes and no, respectively. Mr Salomon would be a secured creditor (by virtue of the fact that he held a secured loan note, effecting a mortgage over the company's assets) and was therefore entitled to be paid in priority to the unsecured creditors of the company. The company owed moneys to Mr Salomon who, because of his security interest, was entitled to be paid before and in priority to unsecured trade creditors. The creditors were not permitted to go behind the veil of incorporation and seek payment of debts incurred by the company from its shareholders. But is this fair ?

Prima facie , the principle of corporate liability operates against the interests of creditors of a company. The Salomon decision says, in effect, that persons who have dealings with limited companies do so at their peril. The risk of loss caused by the financial collapse of a company is suffered not by the businessperson/investor, but by the company's creditors. On the face of it, this is unfair, but the law takes a pragmatic approach: it justifies the apparent unfairness by economic expediency. The company structure provides many economic advantages, and commerce must be allowed to develop for the public good of the economy as a whole. Unpaid creditors, it has been said, are treated as a casualty of commerce.

This shift in risk, from investor to creditor, and its resulting unfairness, has caused potential creditors of a company to come up with ways of protecting themselves against the economic collapse of a company that may, at any one time, owe them money. Creditors 22  often seek to protect themselves by:

- obtaining security for monies owed by the company in the form of a traditional fixed mortgage over certain specified assets of the company (usually real estate) or a floating charge over other assets such as moveable chattels belonging to the company

- contracting around the corporate personality by obtaining personal guarantees from shareholders, whereby the shareholders effectively guarantee to discharge the specified debts of the company in the event that the company cannot pay.

There are also certain circumstances in which creditors of, and claimants from, a company are allowed, in the absence of security or shareholders' guarantees, to lift the veil of incorporation , and go behind the corporate personality to attach the assets of the individual shareholders (or directors) in order to satisfy a debt owed by the company.

## 4.2 Limited liability

It follows from Salomon v A Salomon &amp; Co Ltd that, if a company enters into a contract in its own name and incurs a debt that it cannot pay, the company alone is liable to pay that debt. The creditors of the company cannot, as a general rule, seek redress from the shareholders of the company. The liability of the company in respect of its debts is unlimited, but there is a limitation upon the liability of its shareholders.

To be precise, the liability of the shareholders is limited either to the amount that they are required to pay for their shares (if the company is limited by shares) or to the amount that the members have guaranteed to contribute should the company be wound up (if the company is limited by guarantee).

The memorandum of association 23  confirms whether the shareholders have limited liability and, if so, the way in which the liability is limited. A limited liability company may be limited either by shares or by guarantee.

## 4.2.1  A company limited by shares

Most companies incorporated, both onshore and offshore, are limited by shares. If the members have paid the agreed issue price for their shares (which in the case of private companies is often the nominal par value), they will have no further liability. If they paid only part of the price at the time the shares were issued, however, they are liable for the unpaid amount. Their liability for the debts of the company can never exceed the amount that is unpaid on the issue of their shares. See, for example, cl.3 of the memorandum of association illustrated in Appendix 1A of the online Study Resources.

## 4.2.2  A company limited by guarantee

The memorandum may specify that, should the company be placed into liquidation and wound up, the members are required to contribute a certain sum to assist in the payment of the company's debts. The members are required to guarantee that they will contribute the relevant sum. Their liability to the company will extend to the amount of their guarantee. Such a company is generally referred to as a guarantee or not-for-profit company. See, by way of example, the alternative cl.3 in the memorandum illustrated in Appendix 1A of the online Study Resources.

## 4.3 Corporate ownership of assets

The assets of a company belong to the company. They do not belong to the members or the directors or other officers of the company. The rights and benefits of contracts entered into by the company belong to the company, not the members, directors or officers.

A leading case on this issue is Macaura v Northern Assurance Co. Ltd . 24  Assets owned by a company were insured with Northern. The assets were destroyed by fire. When an insurance claim was made, Northern discovered that the policy had been taken out by Macaura - the sole shareholder of the company - in his personal name, rather than by the company in its corporate name. As a result, the loss caused by the fire was not covered under the policy of insurance. The claim was rejected.

Because corporate assets belong to the company, not its shareholders or officers, a change of membership occurring when a shareholder dies or sells their shares, or a retirement or incapacity of one of the directors or officers, does not affect the company property at all. The property remains vested in the company. The shares are merely transferred to the new owner (or a new director is appointed, as the case may be). This is in marked contrast to a partnership structure where, upon the death of a partner or enforced sale of a partner's interest, for instance, the partnership property may have to be sold and the proceeds distributed. Likewise, the benefit of contracts remains vested in the company and is not terminated upon, for instance, the incapacity or retirement of a director.

As a consequence of a company's ability to own its own property, it follows that a company can also take legal action in its own name to enforce its legal rights; and it can have legal action brought against it for breach of any of its legal duties.

## 4.4 Perpetual succession

A company is created by a registration process. It exists until such time as a legal process of winding-up is undertaken to terminate it. This means that a company can, in theory, exist forever (in perpetuity). This is in contrast to:

- unincorporated entities that trade as sole proprietorships or partnerships, where the proprietor or partners may, sooner or later, become incapacitated, retire or die
- trusts, which are subject to the so-called perpetuity period and, therefore, have a maximum duration.

Gower sums it up nicely in the following passage:

'The vicissitudes of the flesh have no direct effect on the disembodied company. The death of a member leaves the company unmoved; members may come and go but the company can go on forever. The insanity of the managing director will not be calamitous to the company provided that he is removed promptly; he may be the company's brains but lobectomy is a simpler operation than on a natural person.' 25

A member who wishes to liquidate an investment in a company merely has to sell the relevant shares.

## 4.5 Transferability of ownership

As discussed in the historical introduction to company law, one of the main reasons behind the separation of ownership of a company (vested in the hands of shareholders) from management and control (delegated to the entrepreneurial directors) was to promote and facilitate investment in business by the public.

If the public are to invest in a business undertaking, they must be able to buy its shares in the knowledge that they can liquidate their investment if they wish to sell. Nonetheless, a shareholder who buys shares in a company ought not to be able simply to hand back or cancel those shares and get a refund. In addition, an outgoing shareholder ought not to be able to claim an appropriate proportion of corporate assets. This would inevitably have a disastrous effect upon the business of the company. Instead, a shareholder is generally free to transfer ownership of shares for whatever price they will fetch on the open market (unless the right to sell or transfer is restricted by the articles of the company).

This is in contrast to a partnership structure, where the ability of partners to sell or transfer their interest in the partnership is fraught with legal difficulties. The remaining partners must unanimously agree that the outgoing partner may transfer their interest and, even then, the outgoing partner remains liable for those debts of the business that were incurred while still a partner. In the case of a company, a shareholder's interest can simply be transferred to a third party by share transfer.

Whether a transfer of share ownership is permitted, the extent of any restrictions and the procedure that must be followed to effect a transfer are matters covered by the company's articles. The desirability of restrictions upon the transferability of shares in a company often depends upon the number of shareholders and the extent of their personal involvement in the organisation. If the shareholders are numerous and comprise pure, impersonal investment capitalists, it would be unusual for there to be any restrictions upon the transferability of shares. On the other hand, if the number of shareholders is small and the shareholders have a personal involvement (for instance, they are also directors) in the business, which is run as a quasi-partnership, it is usual to find a restriction upon transferability. This is so that the shareholders may control the identity of the membership of the company. In effect, it means that the freedom to transfer shares often depends upon whether the company is public or private.

- In the case of a private company, it is common for some form of transfer restriction to be found in the articles in order to prevent shares in the company

from being offered to the general public. This type of restriction may be a simple requirement imposed upon a shareholder who wishes to transfer shares to obtain the prior approval of the board of directors. (See art.8.3 of the articles illustrated in Appendix 1B of the online Study Resources.) Alternatively, it may be a more complex restriction which confers a so-called right of pre-emption upon the remaining shareholders to ensure that any member who wishes to transfer shares must first offer them to the existing members. (See the alternative art.8.3 illustrated in Appendix 1B.)

- In the case of a public company, there is nothing to prevent its articles from containing a similar restriction upon transferability, although such restrictions are unusual, particularly if it is envisaged that the shares will be offered for sale to the public or listed on a stock exchange.

## 4.6 Separation of ownership from management and control

Although a company is treated as a separate person, it is nevertheless an artificial person insofar as it is unable to manage itself. A company, therefore, requires natural persons to act for it in relation to its management and day-to-day administration. The management of a company is usually conducted by its board of directors.

The directors have power, conferred by the articles of the company, to buy and sell assets on behalf of the company, to bind the company to deals and contracts, and generally to conduct its business. In order to protect the shareholders from a rogue director's abuse of position, and use of their powers for personal gain, the law imposes upon directors:

- a fiduciary duty of good faith
- a duty to exercise their powers with reasonable care and skill.

While the companies legislation makes it mandatory for a company to have a board of directors, the composition, structure and functions of the board are prescribed by its articles, rather than statute. The extent of the separation of ownership (shareholding) and control (management) is therefore a matter for the company to decide for itself and set out in its constitutive documents. These aspects of the role, function, powers of management and duties of a director are explored in detail in Modules 7 and 8.

## 5. Use of offshore companies

The advantages of incorporation identified above are predominantly relevant to large, public operating companies. Incorporation allows business entrepreneurs to acquire substantial amounts of capital from the investing public, and permits an individual capitalist to invest in business without the inherent risk of incurring further liability, and without the need to take part in the day-to-day management of the business.

These are advantages for business undertakings that require a considerable amount of capital investment (way beyond the means of a single individual or group of individuals) or that are inherently risky ventures (where there is not only a risk of losing the initial capital investment, but also a risk of being held liable for further calls for capital to satisfy creditors or other claimants).

Although these sorts of activity may occasionally be conducted by companies incorporated offshore, they are not those companies'  main types of activity. Offshore companies are often used simply to own and hold assets. Their purpose is similar to that of a trust: to hold assets which are then managed by the directors on behalf of others - in this case, the shareholders (or beneficial owners of the shares). Nonetheless, a corporate vehicle is a structure underpinned by a completely different legal regime from that of a trust.

Assets such as real estate, stocks and shares, intellectual property (patents, copyrights, registered designs) and other investments may therefore be transferred to a company incorporated offshore for the specific purpose of holding the legal ownership interest in those assets on behalf of the original owner, in exchange for the issue of shares in the company.

The offshore company thus becomes the legal owner of the capital asset as well as the recipient of any income produced by it (such as rent, dividends, interest payments, royalties and licence fees).

Some of the common uses of companies that have been incorporated in offshore financial centres are listed below:

- To hold trust assets : an offshore company is often incorporated to act as an asset-holding vehicle underlying a trust - the assets are vested in the name of the underlying company, the shares of which are registered in the name of the trustees and held in trust in accordance with the provisions of the trust instrument.
- To hold passive assets , such as real estate, works of art, yachts, etc.
- To hold shares of a group of subsidiary companies , trading and carrying on business onshore.
- To conduct investment activities , such as the buying and selling of marketable securities - for instance, stocks, shares, bonds and fixed interest securities, options, futures and derivative products, and other exotic investments.
- To act as a collective investment fund , such as an open-ended or closed-ended investment company.
- To provide estate planning opportunities by enabling assets to be held for future generations; some clients, particularly those who are unfamiliar with the concept of a common law trust, may prefer to use a company rather than a trust for this purpose.

Some clients may consider that their offshore company is merely a 'money box' , used to receive funds and make payments as they instruct. Some may wish to be a signatory on the company's bank account. In these situations, the directors are often expected to disregard their implied duties and responsibilities and act only as directed by the client. We will consider the relationship that clients may have with their companies in later modules 26  and discuss some of the problems caused by allowing the client too much control. Using a company in the manner of a money box, or as a glorified bank account for the personal use of the client, is contrary to what is considered to be 'best practice' within the corporate services industry.

This practice has also been severely criticised by the OECD in its Report on Misuse of Corporate Vehicles for Illicit Purposes (May 1998) and in the OECD annual reports on harmful tax competition. The controlled foreign company (CFC) anti-tax-avoidance provisions, FATCA and the OECD's FATCA-inspired CRS and Economic Substance rules are designed to eliminate the tax avoidance opportunities of the 'corporate money box' structure.

In summary, therefore, the advantages identified in section 4 accrue to companies incorporated both onshore and offshore. Bearing in mind the predominant use of offshore entities as asset-holding purposes, what possible advantages accrue to them alone? Why are offshore companies used to hold the assets of HNWIs who reside onshore?

We have identified three reasons why offshore companies are preferred over similar entities incorporated onshore, namely: streamlined regulation, confidentiality and tax planning opportunities.

Onshore regulation of companies, particularly in the case of public companies, where there is a separation of ownership and control, is detailed, long and complex. This often leads to cumbersome compliance requirements. Company regulation offshore is often far more streamlined and reflects the fact that a number of companies incorporated offshore are simple structures: private, often one-person companies similar to Mr Salomon's. Some of the special features available to companies incorporated offshore are reviewed in later modules. These special features include:

- sole or corporate directors
- single shareholders
- migration or re-domiciliation of companies
- audit exemption and exemption from the formal presentation of accounts
- relaxation of the capital maintenance rules, and distribution flexibility
- written resolutions in lieu of formal meetings
- relaxation of voluntary winding-up rules.

The remaining matters, namely tax planning opportunities and confidentiality are, since the syllabus changes in 2020, explored in detail in the STEP Advanced Certificate in Principles of International Taxation, AML and Compliance.
