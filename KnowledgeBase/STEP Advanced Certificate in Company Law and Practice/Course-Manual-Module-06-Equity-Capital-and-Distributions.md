## Module 6: Equity, Capital and Distributions

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- introduce the concept of equity finance and 'capital' in different contexts
- explain how a company may issue shares to raise capital, and is required to allocate and preserve the consideration received
- review how the capital maintenance rules restrict the funds that can be used to distribute as dividends to shareholders
- investigate how such restrictions are circumnavigated.

## Introduction

On incorporation, a company acquires its own legal personality and corporate structure: the subscribers become the initial shareholders who appoint the directors, who, in turn, organise the company into a commercial unit ready to operate a business. At this stage it does not have any money - it does not have ' capital ' .

The word 'capital' has many different meanings. In a colloquial context, used in business, it is the sum of money or other property that is used in a business to earn profits.

It is sometimes used in the context of the net amount belonging to a business, after deducting the debts it owes. For instance, in the case of a sole proprietor, it is usual for the owner to invest money or other property into the business as initial capital with a view to putting that capital to work to make a profit. Historically, it was practical to place a value on the proprietor's capital in the business accounts in order to establish the annual trading profit and loss. At the start-up of the business, the value of any property invested is entered in the accounts as the initial capital. Money subsequently borrowed, or any liability owed, by the business is deducted to arrive at a figure of net capital (or equity ). At the end of the first year of trading the annual net profit can be calculated. Assuming there have been no withdrawals, the proprietor calculates existing capital and deducts the initial capital and liabilities. The profit can be taken out of the business to be enjoyed by the proprietor, or retained and ploughed back into the business. To the extent that profits are not distributed, the proprietor starts year two with a larger capital base.

The word capital also has a narrower, more technical historical meaning when used in the context of company law. It is the sum of money shown in the balance sheet as capital in respect of which certain rules have developed called the capital maintenance doctrine. 1 It is calculated by the formula: number of issued shares multiplied by the par value per share.

This module reviews the concept of equity finance. In particular it reviews how capital is introduced, and the rules that require the company to maintain its capital. Later in the module we identify how these rules inhibit an asset-holding investment company from making distributions and we explore ways of getting around such rules.

## 1. Equity finance

A business conducted as a partnership can acquire working capital from contributions made by the individual partners, as well as by borrowing from creditors. But there is a limit to the amount that can be borrowed. Lenders often require collateral. So the working capital of a partnership is limited to the combined wealth of the individual partners together with the extent of their borrowing power.

It was because of this limitation upon the amount of money that could be raised to conduct business as a partnership that the corporate entity emerged in the 17th century. As discussed in Module 2, an increase in international trade, and the Industrial Revolution in particular, saw the development of the company as a separate entity and trading structure that could raise significant amounts of capital by equity finance - i.e., by selling shares to the investing public.

The process by which a company raises equity finance and finds a willing buyer to purchase new shares is called an offering. An offering can be made to an identified individual or small group of individuals on a private basis , or it can be made to the general public as a public offering of new shares.

A public offering can be made by a public company and is essentially an invitation to the public at large to buy shares in the company on what is now described as the 'primary market' . Investors are more willing to buy shares on the primary market if there exists an available and liquid 'secondary market' on which they may subsequently trade the securities. Accordingly, new shares issued by public companies are often, but not necessarily, listed on a stock exchange. An initial public offering of new securities therefore involves an offer for sale (or subscription) on the primary market together with an application for a listing on the secondary market. 2

Company law says very little about a private placement of new shares whereas a public offering is heavily regulated. The procedure can be complex, but essentially involves the following three stages: underwriting, promotion and issuing the new securities. We shall review concisely the issue of shares via public offering in section 1.1 and the issue of shares by a private company via a private placement in section 1.2 below.

## 1.1 A public offering

## 1.1.1  Engagement of investment bank and underwriting

At the outset, the issuing company retains an investment bank to advise upon and underwrite the issue. Underwriting is arranged in order to guarantee a successful issue.

Essentially the investment bank, in exchange for an underwriting fee, promises to buy all the shares that are not subscribed for by the investing public. 3

## 1.1.2  Promotion

The investment bank markets the new securities to the public. A critical part of the promotion involves the publication of a prospectus (or offering document) which discloses the precise terms of the offer, including the issue price . The issue price for the new shares is fixed by the issuing company upon the advice of its investment bankers. It's a tricky decision. If set too low, the issue will be over-subscribed and the company will not receive as much as it should, as a result of it selling some of its business at too low a price. If set too high, the price may discourage investors and the offer will be under-subscribed. Although the underwriters are obliged to buy the unsold new issue of shares, it will reduce the value of the company's shares in the market (as the underwriters generally dump the shares for a loss) and affect the ability of the company to raise further capital in the future.

## 1.1.3 Issue of the new securities

Prospective investors are invited to scrutinise the prospectus and, if they decide to invest, are asked to make a written application to subscribe. Applications are made to the investment bank that manages the new issue on behalf of the issuing company. The investment bank processes the applications and sends out allotment letters, thereby agreeing on behalf of the company to issue shares at the offer price. Allotment is a contract that confers a right upon the prospective investor to buy the shares at the agreed price. In due course that contract is completed by the company by issuing the shares in consideration for the payment of the issue price, at which time the investor becomes legal owner of the new shares, and is recorded as a shareholder.

## 1.2 Equity finance on a private basis

The whole process of raising equity finance is simplified for a company that issues shares on a private basis. A large public company may wish to sell new shares by private placement, usually to institutional investors. There is, in these circumstances, no need for underwriting. Nor is there a requirement to publish a detailed prospectus. The company still engages the services of an investment bank to put the deal together, negotiating the terms of the new issue on behalf of the company with prospective institutional investors.

A private company, of course, may also issue shares on a private basis with little formality. There is no need for the private company to retain an investment bank, underwriting is unnecessary and there are no disclosure requirements for the publication of a detailed prospectus. Essentially, if someone wants to invest in a private company, and the company wants that investment, the terms are agreed without fuss and the investor is issued with a share certificate in exchange for payment of the agreed consideration.

A private company issues shares in the following manner.

## 1.2.1  Subscriber shares

When a company limited by shares is formed, one or more persons (or entities) must subscribe to the company by signing the memorandum. The subscriber(s) agree to take at least one share in the company upon incorporation. Accordingly, once incorporated, the subscriber(s) automatically become the first shareholder(s). This occurs by operation of law and is not a true issue of shares on a private basis by way of private placement. The subscriber(s) pay the agreed consideration, which is likely to be nominal, or may leave it outstanding, and details of their identity/ies and the number, class, par value and amount paid for the shares are entered in the company's register of members and usually noted at the first board meeting.

In offshore company management practice, however, the subscribers are usually nominee subscribers , namely employees or corporate nominees of the service provider engaged to incorporate the company. The subscriber shares are therefore retained by the service provider as nominee for the beneficial owner. The subscribers do not, therefore, provide capital (in the wider sense of the word) or finance to a typical offshore company. They act in an administrative capacity to get the company incorporated. For example, the promoter, who becomes the sole beneficial owner of the shares of the company, may agree for a nominee to take one (or two) shares in consideration of a capital contribution made to the company of $1 million (or, alternatively, in consideration for a nominal capital contribution together with an interest-free loan of $1 million, repayable upon demand). In these circumstances, the subscriber(s), acting as agent for the beneficial owner, provide(s) capital funding in exchange for the issue of the subscriber shares. As the company is to remain a one person company, the number of shares issued is of little importance as long as the beneficial owner owns all of them. The company could issue a further 1 million shares to 1 million nominees, but if the principal of each nominee is the beneficial owner, then the beneficial owner still beneficially owns and controls all the issued shares.

Accordingly, it is not unusual for an offshore asset-holding company simply to issue the subscriber shares and no more. If the sole subscriber takes up a single share and holds it for the beneficial owner, then the beneficial owner essentially owns 100% of the issued shares of the company. However, if the company is designed to have multiple (beneficial) owners, it will allot and issue additional shares soon, or some time, after incorporation.

## 1.2.2  Share allotment and issue

When a company is to issue additional shares, beyond those issued to the subscriber(s), the following procedure should be followed.

## Stage 1: Ascertain who has the power to allot and issue new shares.

The power to allot and issue new shares is typically conferred upon the board of directors of the company by legislation, or by the company's articles or by resolution of its shareholders.

- Companies legislation: the 1948 Act does not confer a statutory power to issue shares upon the board; a statutory power was first introduced in England by s.80 of the Companies Act 1985, which states: 'The directors of a company shall

not exercise any power of the company to allot relevant securities, unless they are … authorised to do so by (a) the company in general meeting; or (b) the company's articles' . More authoritative provisions are enacted by IBC legislation. For example, s.45 of the BVI BCA states: 'Subject to contrary provisions in the memorandum and articles, shares in a company may be issued … at such times, to such persons, for such consideration and on such terms as the directors may determine' . 4  Similar statutory powers to issue shares are conferred upon the board by modern legislation enacted in the Crown Dependencies of Guernsey and Isle of Man. 5

- In those jurisdictions where no statutory power to issue shares is conferred upon the board, the company's articles will generally confer such a power. 6 Article 2.3 of the specimen articles illustrated in Appendix 1B of the online Study Resources confers upon the directors a general power to issue ordinary shares of the company. The authority of the directors to issue shares is also recited in the board resolution to actually issue shares; see Note (B) of the board resolution illustrated in Appendix 16C of the online Study Resources.
- As a final alternative, the shareholders may confer upon the board a general or specific authority to allot and issue shares by ordinary resolution. A specimen written resolution of a sole shareholder is illustrated in Appendix 16A of the online Study Resources.

## Stage 2: Directors seek expressions of interest

The directors of a private company in need of equity finance and/or wishing to expand its body of shareholders, use informal means to seek outside investment from third parties. When prospective investors express an interest, negotiations take place between the company's board and the third party about, e.g., the number of shares to be taken up and at what price. For a private, one-person, asset-holding company, the beneficial owner may wish simply to donate new shares, without consideration, to other members of their family.

## Stage 3: Application to subscribe

When terms have been agreed, it is good practice for the third party to apply informally by letter to buy shares, called an application to subscribe. A specimen application is illustrated in Appendix 16B of the online Study Resources. If new shares are being issued without consideration, this stage can be dispensed with.

## Stage 4: Allotment

The directors convene a board meeting to resolve to make the allotment, or pass a written resolution, which records: the name of the prospective shareholder (the 'allottee'), the number of shares to be issued and agreed consideration. This is technically when shares are 'allotted' . A specimen written resolution of a sole director is illustrated in Appendix 16C.

## Stage 5: Issue shares

The allottee pays, the company receives the agreed consideration, if any, and the corporate service provider should update the register of members. This is technically when shares are 'issued' as the new shareholder becomes legal owner of the shares. The service provider may also prepare and deliver a new share certificate to the new member, if required by the company's articles. A specimen share certificate is illustrated in Appendix 16D of the online Study Resources and is prima facie evidence of the holder's ownership of the shares stated therein. Historically, a share certificate would be issued to shareholders in accordance with the terms of model articles (see arts 3.1 and 3.2 of Appendix 1B), and the company's seal was affixed to it, but nowadays the certificate may simply be executed without using a seal. As share certificates may be lost, damaged or destroyed it is not unusual for special articles of a private asset-holding company to state that shareholders are not entitled to a share certificate, in which case this last requirement can be dispensed with. 7

## Stage 6: Return of allotment

In England, a return of allotment must be filed with the registrar. This is not generally necessary in the offshore financial centres, save for public companies and companies incorporated in Hong Kong and Singapore.

Finally, a private asset-holding investment company does not typically attract outside investors. The vast majority of offshore companies are private companies, limited by shares, where the whole issued capital is beneficially owned by a single individual (namely, the service provider's client) or a corporate trustee (if the company underlies a trust). The company's sole purpose is to hold assets for a single shareholder. Accordingly, the initial capital and any additional capital is simply provided from time to time by the (beneficial) owner. The introduction of capital is dressed up by the corporate administrator to look like a private placement and/or shareholder loan as the case may be (depending upon the distribution flexibility afforded by the governing companies legislation: see section 4 below).

## 2. Classes of shares

A company does not have to issue just one type or class of share (the ordinary share of common stock). A company may, if authorised in its articles, issue different classes of shares. A class of share is a group of shares that have the same rights as each other. Accordingly, a company with an article similar to art.2.1 of the model articles illustrated in Appendix 1B may issue different classes of shares, each class conferring different rights upon holders of shares of such class (for instance, for voting, receipt of dividends, return of capital on the dissolution of the company or other special rights).

Thus the capital of a company may be divided into any number of different classes of shares. Where a class of share does not confer upon holders of such share any right to vote at shareholders' meetings, the disenfranchised shares are often referred to as non-voting shares . Where a class confers economic superiority over the ordinary shares of common stock (such as priority payment of a dividend, or return of capital upon a winding-up) they are referred to as preference shares (known as preferred stock in the US).

For example, a company with articles that contain article 2.1 referred to above may, by a resolution of its members, issue, say, four classes of shares: Class A may be ordinary voting shares, Class B may be non-voting preference shares, Class C shares may confer a dividend preference, Class D shares may confer some sort of priority if the company is wound up. An assortment of typical class rights are found in Appendix 17A of the online Study Resources.

There is often no reason to issue different classes of shares of a private company that has a sole shareholder, or beneficial owner. The sole beneficial owner owns all the shares, can vote them as they think fit, is entitled to the entire dividend, and has sole right to any surplus assets, once all creditors have been paid in full upon a winding-up. What would be the point in distinguishing ordinary and preference shares divided into different classes that confer different rights upon the holders, when all the shares are held by one and the same person?

## 3. Borrowing, the creation of debt

A company can finance its operations by two methods: by raising share capital when shares are issued and sold; and by borrowing.

As private companies do not generally seek outside investment, their only source of finance is the share capital provided by shareholders together with any borrowing from creditors. Credit may be obtained from lending institutions at commercial rates. Or alternatively, by borrowing from shareholders, so that the shareholders also become creditors of the company. In these circumstances the borrowing from shareholders is referred to as a shareholder loan , which may be at commercial rates but, in the case of a one-person-company, is usually interest-free, repayable upon demand. 8

When a company finances its operations by borrowing (from banks or by shareholder loan) the value of such a loan is a liability of the company rather than classified as capital and, as such, is not subject to the capital maintenance rules. This is because the rules purport to protect creditors. As will be discussed in Module 12 section 4, creditors must always be paid before the return of capital to shareholders, in the event of a company's insolvency. Accordingly, only assets contributed by shareholders can fully satisfy claims brought by creditors. Loans made to a company fall outside the capital maintenance rules and are repayable according to their agreed terms.

## 4. The introduction and allocation of capital when shares are issued

It was noted in Module 2 section 2.2.3 that the concept of limited liability was introduced in England by the Limited Liability Act 1855. The potential unlimited liability of shareholders for paying corporate debts in the event of the insolvency of the company acted as a chill to deter investment. Accordingly, in order to address the problem and encourage investment, the English legislature permitted a company to incorporate with limited liability. In these circumstances, from the Limited Liability Act 1855 onwards, the shareholders of a limited company were not liable to pay corporate debts.

Limited liability transfers risk of corporate failure from shareholders to the creditors of the company. Persons who have extended credit to, or are otherwise lawfully owed money by, a limited company may be left without redress if the company is rendered insolvent and unable to pay them. In order to address this prejudice to creditors as a quid pro quo the courts devised (and later the legislature codified) protective rules that became known as the capital maintenance doctrine. 9 This is essentially a package of measures designed to ensure that the capital of a company is preserved within the company as a separate account to pay its creditors. It affects a company at two stages: firstly when the company is formed and issues shares to raise share capital (reviewed in this section); and secondly when the company makes distributions to shareholders (reviewed in sections 6 and 7 below).

## 4.1 Capital structure of a company

The preliminary part of the capital maintenance doctrine states that a company must receive proper value when it issues shares, so that its share capital is a real and meaningful amount. Capital is thus the monetary value of cash and assets contributed to a company by those who acquire its shares. This monetary value of the issued share capital must be credited to an account called the company's share capital account . The share capital account must be maintained and cannot be reduced by, for instance, being used to finance distributions to shareholders or redeem shares. Accordingly, at least in theory, the sum of share capital must be maintained within the company to ensure that monies are available to pay creditors if necessary. In these circumstances the creditors ought not to be prejudiced by the fact that shareholders are not responsible for payment of the money due to them. 10

Share capital is the amount raised by the company on the issue of shares. 11  When a company is governed by traditional English companies legislation the capital clause in its memorandum must state: 12

- the authorised share capital, namely the maximum amount the company is authorised to raise by issuing shares 13
- how the capital is divided into shares
- the par value of each share.

So, for example, the memorandum of AB Inc. may state:

'The share capital of the company shall be $50,000 divided into 5,000 shares each with a par value of $10.00.'

A company does not have to issue its entire share capital. It can issue just part. But when it does issue shares it must get proper value, either in the form of cash or by the transfer of property of adequate value to the company. The requirement is for a company to issue shares in consideration for the payment of a price equal to their par value or above. 14  A company is prohibited from issuing shares at a discount for less than their par value. If a company issues shares for less than their par value it is said to have issued 'watered' stock. This is because it dilutes the value of its share capital fund and prejudices creditors. It is also a criminal offence contrary to typical companies legislation for a company to issue shares below their par value: it renders the recipient shareholder liable to pay the balance to creditors, in the event that the company becomes insolvent and is unable to pay creditors in full.

## 4.2 Allocation of capital between share capital and share premium (or between capital and capital surplus)

The actual price that a company sells its shares for is the issue price . It is a matter for the company's directors to decide the price the company asks for its shares. The price must not be below the par value, but it may be above it. Shares can therefore be issued at any price above par that the market will bear. The difference between par and the issue price is called the ' premium '  (British terminology) or the ' surplus '  (historically, US terminology). The amount of share capital raised by issuing shares at or up to their par value is credited to ' share capital '  in the balance sheet of the company (simply ' capital ' in US terminology). Whereas, the amount of capital raised by issuing shares above their par value attributable to the premium is credited to the ' share premium '  account in the balance sheet (' capital surplus '  in US terminology). The distinction between share capital and share premium (or the distinction between capital and capital surplus) is critical when it comes to what such funds can be used for, and in particular whether they can be used to distribute to shareholders to finance a dividend - reviewed in sections 6 and 7 below.

In order to illustrate these provisions, assume that AB Inc., whose capital clause in its memorandum is reproduced in section 2.1 above, issues 1,000 shares to X for $40 per share, and a further 500 shares to Y for a similar amount per share.

The company's capital position is summarised in its balance sheet, illustrated in Appendix 17B of the online Study Resources and explained below:

- the authorised share capital of the company is $50,000 (per the memorandum)
- each share has a (nominal) par value of $10 (per the memorandum)
- the company's issued share capital is $15,000, namely the par value of all the shares that have been issued i.e. 1,500 x $10.

If AB Inc wishes to issue more shares to raise further share capital it may issue an additional 3,500 shares to shareholders but no more (without first increasing the authorised share capital of the company, usually by special resolution).

The consideration paid by the shareholders X and Y for their shares is credited to different accounts of the company which are then subject to different rules: the issued share capital of $15,000 is credited to share capital in the balance sheet (or just 'capital'); the premium of $45,000 (i.e. 1,500 multiplied by ($40 minus $10)) is credited to share premium (or capital surplus). The share premium (capital surplus) may or may not be available to distribute to shareholders depending upon applicable company law, a topic reviewed in sections 6 and 7 below.

## 5. Transfer of shares

## 5.1 Transfers

Once shares have been issued, a shareholder who wishes to liquidate his or her investment has to sell the shares to a willing buyer at the market price. If the company is a public listed company, then the shares are freely tradable on an exchange without restriction. If the company is a private company, the articles usually impose some kind of restriction. Shareholders' rights to transfer their shares to a willing buyer may be subject to either:

- a. the approval of the board of directors, as prescribed by art.8.3 of the model articles illustrated in Appendix 1B; or
- b. pre-emption rights conferred upon existing shareholders, so that the shares of any shareholder wishing to sell must first be offered to the other shareholders before they can be sold to any third party, as prescribed in the alternative article to art.8.3 in Appendix 1B.

A transfer of shares in a private company is carried out in accordance with the articles, typically in the following manner.

## Stage 1: Shareholder finds a willing buyer

A seller of shares in a private company makes it known, sometimes with the help and assistance of brokers, that the shareholder's shares are for sale. A potential buyer expresses an interest and they negotiate terms of the sale, in particular the consideration. When the shares represent all, the majority or a significant minority of the shares of the company, the agreed terms are reduced into writing in a share purchase agreement containing all sorts of warranties. When the shareholder intends to transfer the shares by way of gift for nil consideration this stage is dispensed with as the transferor and transferee are clearly intimately known to one another. Note that AML and CTF requirements should be complied with, to ensure the purchaser transferee is neither a criminal nor a terrorist.

## Stage 2: Share transfer form

The methodology by which shares are transferred is set out in the articles of the company. Article 8 of Appendix 1B is typical insofar that it requires the transferor and transferee to sign a prescribed share transfer form. An example of a share transfer form is provided in Appendix 18A of the online Study Resources, and also in art.8.2 of Appendix 1B. The executed share transfer form is delivered to the transferee together with the relevant share certificate(s) in consideration for the transferee's payment of the agreed price to the transferor. If there are joint holders of the share(s) being transferred, both should sign the share transfer form. If one joint holder has died then the deceased's death certificate should be produced as evidence of death so that the rights of the deceased holder vest automatically in the survivor in accordance with art.8.6 of Appendix 1B.

In the UK and certain other jurisdictions there is stamp duty to pay unless the transaction is exempt, but this is unusual offshore.

## Stage 3: Delivery of executed share transfer form to the company for registration

Upon receipt by the company of the executed share transfer form and share certificate(s) the company secretary/registered agent/corporate service provider should firstly ascertain whether any pre-emption rights exist, have been complied with or waived.

## Stage 4: board approval

If there are no restrictions upon transfers of shares in the articles then the shares are freely transferable by a shareholder to whomsoever they wish. In these circumstances, the transferee is entitled as of right to become the new shareholder and have their name inserted into the company's register of members. In practice, according to fairly standard articles such as art.8.3 of Appendix 1B, the transfer must be approved by resolution of the board. A specimen resolution approving the transfer is illustrated in Appendix 18B of the online Study Resources. Nonetheless, the directors may decide by resolution to refuse to register the proposed transfer. In these circumstances the directors must give notice of such refusal as soon as possible and in any event within two months of such refusal in order to comply with art.8.5 of Appendix 1B. A specimen director's resolution refusing to register a share transfer is illustrated in Appendix 18C and a notice of refusal in 18D. There is a debate about whether the board should record reasons for their refusal to register the transfer in (a) the minutes of the meeting at which it was considered or the written resolution; and (b) the notification to the proposed transferee. In Berry and Stewart v Tottenham Hotspur Football Co , a case decided in accordance with the Companies Act 1929 (a predecessor to the 1948 Act but similar in relation to this issue), it was held that directors are not required to give reasons for their refusal to register a transfer, 15  whereas s.771 of the 2006 Act now states that reasons must be given. It is anyone's guess whether the offshore jurisdictions would follow the Tottenham Hotspur principle; but it is the author's view that if reasons are not given, or if the reasons are not factually correct or are frivolous, the decision could be challenged as unreasonable.

## Stage 5: registration

When board approval is given, the service provider:

- a. makes the required entries in the register of members (illustrated in Appendix 7A of the online Study Resources, i.e. by inserting the date of the transfer in the column for the existing shareholder, entering particulars of the transferee and, under the heading 'Remarks' , inserting 'Transfer');
- b. makes the required entries, if necessary, in the register of beneficial ownership, and files particulars with the competent authority;
- c. cancels the old share certificate by writing 'cancelled' across it and filing it away; and
- d. prepares and delivers a new share certificate to the transferee (if the transferee is entitled to one: see section 1.2.2, stage 5).

Finally, before leaving the topic of share transfers, we should observe that it is unusual for a holder of shares in a single-owner, offshore asset-holding company to transfer his or her shares to a third party. A transfer will occur, however, in the following circumstances. Firstly, if shares are held by a nominee on behalf of the beneficial owner and the beneficial owner changes service providers; in these circumstances the nominee shareholder is required to transfer the shares to a new nominee owned and controlled by the new service provider. Secondly, if shares of an underlying company are owned by a corporate trustee and the trustee is removed and replaced by a new trustee: in these circumstances the outgoing trustee is required to transfer the shares to the incoming trustee. Thirdly, if a company owns a single asset, such as a piece of real estate, a yacht or private jet, and sells the asset to a willing buyer, it is sometimes easier to transfer the shares in the company to the buyer rather than the asset itself.

## 5.2 Transmission

A transmission of shares occurs by operation of law when a shareholder dies or is declared bankrupt.

When a person dies, the deceased's personal representative will apply to the probate court of the jurisdiction of the state where the deceased was domiciled (or a citizen) at the time of his or her death, for a grant of probate. The grant of probate is evidence of the identity of the person entitled lawfully to represent the heirs and estate of the deceased.

Accordingly, on the death of a registered shareholder of a private company the personal representative of the deceased should deliver the deceased's death certificate and the grant of probate to the registered office of the company. Typical articles, similar to art.8.7 of Appendix 1B, provide that the personal representative may then elect either:

- a. to become registered as the shareholder in place of the deceased by delivering to the company a written request similar to Appendix 18E of the online Study Resources; or
- b. to bypass the personal representative's own registration and have the person(s) entitled to the share, namely the deceased's heirs, registered as the new shareholder(s). In these circumstances the personal representative should deliver to the company at its registered office a share transfer form in the manner discussed in section 5.1 above and sign it as transferor of the deceased shareholder.

Upon receipt of the letter envisaged by sub-para.(a) above, or the share transfer form envisaged by sub-para.(b), the company secretary/registered agent/corporate service provider should update the register of members of the company under administration, update the beneficial owner register, cancel the old share certificate and issue a new one, if necessary.

Similar provisions apply if a registered shareholder is declared bankrupt, save that the service provider will be contacted by the court-appointed trustee-in-bankruptcy of the bankrupt shareholder who will produce the court order as the trustee-in-bankruptcy's authority to become registered as shareholder in place of the bankrupt.

Finally, once more, before leaving the topic of share transmissions, we emphasise that it is unusual for a registered holder of shares in a single-owner, offshore asset-holding company to die or be made bankrupt. This is because the registered holder of the shares is likely to be a corporate nominee that holds the shares on behalf of the principal, the beneficial owner of the shares or, alternatively, a corporate trustee. The beneficial owner may, however, die or be made bankrupt.

If the beneficial owner dies, the personal representative of the estate of the deceased will still deliver the deceased's death certificate and grant of probate to the registered office of the company. However, pursuant to their inherited powers that derive from a standard nominee shareholder agreement, the deceased's personal representative can either:

- a. become registered as shareholder;
- b. ask that the deceased's heir(s) be registered as shareholder(s); or
- c. re-engage the services of the nominee and ask the nominee to acknowledge that the nominee holds the shares for and on behalf of the new beneficial owner, namely the heirs of the deceased's estate, by executing a new nominee shareholder agreement.

Similar provisions will apply when the beneficial owner is declared bankrupt, save that the court-appointed trustee-in-bankruptcy assumes all of the rights of the beneficial owner.

## 6. Distributions to shareholders: the traditional approach

Having reviewed the first stage of the capital maintenance doctrine in relation to the introduction of capital into a company in section 4, we shall now examine the second stage of the doctrine, which prevents such capital being used to finance any distributions to shareholders by way of dividend. Before we review those rules, let us look at some basics.

## 6.1 The power to distribute dividends

The provisions that empower a company to declare a dividend are set out in the articles and are also subject to restrictions found in companies legislation. A traditional article (found in the English 1948 Act Table A model articles, and reproduced in art.23.1 of Appendix 1B) states:

'The company in general meeting [i.e. the shareholders] may declare a dividend, but no dividend shall exceed the amount recommended by the directors.'

This article therefore requires two resolutions to be passed in order for the company lawfully to pay a dividend to its shareholders:

- a. a directors' resolution to recommend the payment of a dividend, similar to the one illustrated in Appendix 19A of the online Study Resources; and
- b. an ordinary resolution of the shareholders to declare and pay the dividend of an amount that cannot exceed the amount recommended by the directors; a specimen resolution of a sole member to declare a dividend recommended

by the board is illustrated in Appendix 19B of the online Study Resources. In real-world onshore companies the shareholders' resolution to declare a dividend is typically passed at the company's AGM. 16

Alternatively, the articles of an offshore company may be drafted to permit distributions to be made in a streamlined manner by a resolution of just one of the two decision-making organs: either by resolution of the shareholders or by resolution of the board, but not both. The alternative article to art.23.1 of Appendix 1B authorises the board of directors to declare a dividend without the need for a second resolution of members; in these circumstances a written resolution of the directors similar to the one illustrated in Appendix 19C of the online Study Resources will suffice.

In practice, of course, it is usual for a commercial operating and trading company to hold back some of its profits rather than distribute its entire annual earnings to shareholders. If some or all of a company's profits are retained they are transferred to a reserve, typically referred to as retained earnings in the balance sheet (see Appendix 17B).

Retained earnings are still treated as profits. They are essentially a pot of money that can be used at any time in the future to pay current debts in the normal course of business, or used to fund distributions to shareholders on a future date, or converted into capital. Conversion of profits into capital occurs when, instead of using profits to pay a dividend in the form of cash, the directors decide to use the profits to pay for further shares that are then issued to shareholders free-of-charge in the form of fully paid bonus shares (or stock dividend). In these circumstances the issued share capital of the company is increased by the par value of the free bonus shares issued, and the retained earnings account is reduced by a similar amount.

## 6.2 Distributions of dividends

The capital maintenance doctrine in Re Flitcroft's Case prohibit the payment of dividends or other distributions to shareholders out of a company's capital. 17 This is because the capital must not be depleted by using such funds to reward shareholders. Such distributions would reduce the company's capital, prejudice creditors and defeat the objective of the capital maintenance doctrine.

Put another way, dividends must only be paid out of a company's profits. What is considered as 'profits' for this purpose was the subject of much litigation under common law principles. 18  Nonetheless, basically, profits for the purposes of justifying a distribution to shareholders mean trading profits earned by a company in the current financial year or earned and accumulated in a reserve of retained earnings in prior financial years. This is because the common law rules were tightened by companies legislation enacted in the UK by the Companies Act 1948 which, you will recall, has been used as a template by a number of offshore jurisdictions. 19  We review some of the onshore provisions enacted in the UK, not because you need to know the onshore requirements, but because it provides a convenient basis with which to compare the more liberal provisions that exist in most offshore jurisdictions.

a. out of trading profits earned in a financial year, (even though the company may have made losses in previous years ( Ammonia Soda Co Ltd v Chamberlain [1918] 1 Ch 266, i.e. so-called 'nimble dividends'));

b. out of profits earned and accumulated in a reserve of retained earnings in prior financial years, unless those profits have been capitalised by a bonus issue of shares or stock dividend;

c. out of a profit made on the sale of a capital asset (such as land, buildings, machinery, and/or plant belonging to the company) or, indeed, out of an unrealised 'profit' made when, for instance, a capital asset appreciates in value and is simply revalued in the records of the company for accounting purposes (Dimbula Valley (Ceylon) Tea Co Ltd v Laurie [1961] Ch 353); and/or

d. out of the monies received by the company when it issues shares at a premium, i.e. at a price above their par value (in other words the monies received in excess of par that would, under modern onshore companies legislation, be allocated to capital surplus or the share premium account) ( Drown v Gaumont-British Picture Corp Ltd [1937] Ch 402).

The following broad principles can be distilled from the English companies legislation.

- The figures that represent profits, capital and such like must be based upon those set out in the company's accounts, which must have been prepared in accordance with generally accepted accounting principles and professionally audited.
- Dividends cannot be distributed out of profits earned in an isolated year if the company has made losses in previous years.
- Unrealised profits cannot be used to pay a dividend, only actual realised profits.
- Profits cannot be paid out of that part of the capital of the company attributable to the premium paid on the issue of shares, i.e. a dividend cannot be paid out of the share premium account, which must be treated so far as distributions are concerned, just like share capital. 20
- A company must not pay a dividend if by doing so the value of its net assets would fall below the value of its issued share capital plus undistributed reserves such as the share premium account and any other reserves it is prohibited, by its articles, from distributing.

These provisions are succinctly summarised by the following formula: dividends may be paid from a company's earnings only, that is, net realised trading profits made by a company over its lifetime minus total dividends already paid.

You will appreciate that these restrictive distribution provisions impede the typical requirements of a beneficial owner of an offshore asset-holding investment vehicle. The essential prerequisites of a holding company are to enable the beneficial owner to make unrestricted capital contributions to the company from time to time as and when desired. The holding vehicle must also allow the owner to make unrestricted withdrawals from the company of varying amounts from time to time as and when cash is required for personal use. The same prerequisites are required of a company underlying a trust; the trustee may receive additions to the trust fund from time to time that are often added to the assets of the underlying company, and the trustee also requires unrestricted access to those same assets when making capital distributions to beneficiaries. The capital structure of an offshore company, or its governing legislation, has to facilitate these prerequisites lawfully. We shall examine how this is achieved in the next section.

## 7. Distributions to shareholders: an offshore perspective

As discussed above, offshore companies legislation tends to follow the English 1948 Act as a model. This model legislation has a restrictive distribution regime, where distributions of capital are prohibited unless the company is wound-up and the only distributions that may be made to shareholders are dividends payable from profits, i.e. realised earned surplus. Accordingly, nimble profits in any one year, which ignore prior losses, cannot be distributed; nor can unrealised profits be distributed; nor can capital surplus in the share premium account be used to finance a dividend. These restrictions curtail the use of a company as an asset-holding vehicle, either as a one-person investment vehicle or as a holding entity underlying a trust.

In order to accommodate the prerequisites of a holding structure that permits unrestricted, unlimited withdrawals, the company's capital structure and/or applicable companies legislation must be modified. This section reviews the modifications necessary to facilitate liberal distributions.

## 7.1 Nominal capital and shareholder loan

Back in the days before amendments were made that modify companies legislation to permit flexible and liberal distributions, offshore asset-holding companies were structured so that only a nominal sum was introduced as share capital, with the bulk of the funding being introduced by the beneficial owner as a shareholder loan.

In fact, the nominee subscribers agree to take just one (or two) shares in the company for a consideration equal to their par value, usually for $1 (or $2, which may not actually be transferred but left partly paid and uncalled). The beneficial owner of the company then transfers money or other assets to the company by way of loan. The loan is documented by a loan agreement or promissory note executed on behalf of the company by the directors. Some offshore professionals prefer the loan to carry a market rate of interest and be repayable on a specific agreed future date. It is usual, however, for loans to be made interest-free and repayable upon demand. A typical promissory note in favour of the beneficial owner is illustrated in Appendix 20A of the online Study Resources.

The advantage of financing an asset-holding company in this way is distribution flexibility. A company that has borrowed money must comply with the agreed terms of the borrowing. Accordingly, if the lender (namely the beneficial owner, wearing the hat not of the beneficial owner of the shares of the company, but of a creditor) makes a written demand for payment, in whole or in part of the outstanding balance of the loan, in accordance with the terms of the original lending, the company must comply. A simple demand for partial repayment is illustrated in Appendix 20B.

Assume, therefore, that AB Inc. issues just two shares: one to Nominee No.1 and the other to Nominee No.2, both of whom hold the shares as agents for the beneficial owner. The beneficial owner then lends the company $1 million. A board resolution is required to acknowledge that the company has received the funds and to authorise the directors to execute a loan document or promissory note, an example of which is illustrated in Appendix 20C of the online Study Resources.

The loan is recorded in the financial statements of the company as a liability, usually in the form of a current liability, as opposed to a long-term liability, whenever it is repayable upon demand.  If the loan is repayable on an identified future date it is recorded as a long-term liability.

From time to time, the beneficial owner may wish to add to the funds under the management of the company , and can do so by making further loans. Equally, the beneficial owner may wish to withdraw funds from the company for personal use, and can do so by making a demand for repayment, in whole or in part, of such loan. In these circumstances, all distributions are treated as loan repayments, and must be recorded as such. For example, the beneficial owner's request ought to be in writing, expressed as a demand for repayment of a fixed sum, and the directors of the company should resolve to make the payment as a repayment of the shareholder loan in the manner illustrated in Appendix 20D of the online Study Resources. A running account must be maintained in order to record the company's indebtedness to the beneficial owner at any one time.

This method of financing asset-holding companies was prevalent throughout most of the offshore centres until modern companies legislation was enacted to permit a more flexible distribution regime. It is still an option under modern legislation. It is essential, however, in those jurisdictions that restrict the ability of a company to pay dividends from earned profits only, such as Hong Kong, 21  Gibraltar 22 and Singapore. 23

## 7.2 Amendment of the capital maintenance doctrine to permit distributions from share premium

In order to cater for the demands of the typical HNWI or corporate trustee, both of whom wish to use an offshore corporate structure for asset-holding purposes but without the restrictions and complexity of the capital maintenance rules, a number of offshore jurisdictions have amended the rules or repealed them altogether.

Some offshore jurisdictions preserve the distinction between share capital and share premium (referred to in IBC legislation as capital and capital surplus), but simply allow the company to distribute dividends from the share premium (capital surplus) account.

Accordingly, when the share capital account of a typical asset-holding company is Accordingly, when the share capital account of a typical asset-holding company is a nominal or modest amount ($2 in the example in section 7.1), and the share premium (capital surplus) account is of a far greater realistic figure such as $1 million, the directors are able lawfully to make distributions of dividends of an amount up to the value of the share premium account (of up to $1 million) without offending the capital maintenance rule.

There is, however, one important proviso: the company cannot make any distributions from the share premium account unless, immediately following the distribution, the company satisfies a statutory 'solvency test'; namely, that it is:

- a. solvent (assets exceed liabilities); and
- b. able to pay its debts as they fall due (has adequate cash-flow).

<!-- image -->

This simple rule is clearly designed to achieve a similar objective to the capital maintenance rules and operates as a satisfactory protection to creditors to prevent a company from denuding itself of assets while leaving lawful creditors without payment or redress.

Distributions are allowed from the share premium account of exempt companies incorporated in the Cayman Islands, 24  Bermuda, 25  private companies incorporated in Jersey 26  and in those jurisdictions that have companies legislation based upon the old BVI IBCA, such as the Bahamas 27  and others 28  (where the share premium account is, of course, referred to as 'capital surplus' and is distributable provided the company is and remains solvent).

For example, consider the flexible statutory distribution regime adopted by a couple of jurisdictions; firstly, the Bahamas (to illustrated typical IBC provisions) and, secondly, the Crown Dependency of Jersey.

## Summary of amendments to the capital maintenance doctrine

Accordingly, distributions of dividends can be made from IBCs or from Cayman Islands and Bermudian exempt companies or Jersey private companies in the form of cash, shares or other property (irrespective of whether earned or realised or not) out of the share premium (capital surplus) account provided that, at the time it declares the dividend and immediately thereafter, the directors reasonably believe the company is solvent. There are slightly different versions of the solvency test across the jurisdictions, but, basically, the company must be:

- strategically solvent , namely, the realisable value of its assets must exceed total liabilities and issued share capital
- cash-flow solvent , namely, able to pay its debts as they fall due.

There is also a typical statutory duty imposed upon the board to establish the company's solvency before a distribution is made and to record a statement that it satisfies the solvency test in the resolution that declares, or recommends the payment of, a dividend. ## 7.3 Complete repeal of the capital maintenance doctrine

It could be argued that the BVI have made things even more flexible and straightforward than other jurisdictions.

There is no longer a distinction between share capital and share premium (capital and surplus) under the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 insofar as BVI companies do not have to allocate the introduction of funds between the two accounts. Instead BVI companies are authorised, in the discretion of their directors, 30  to issue a maximum number of shares, and the consideration received is simply credited to a capital account. 31  BVI BCs are not required to state in their memorandum an authorised share capital, nor the par value of the shares or even their currency. 32

So far as distributions are concerned, under the old BVI IBCA, upon which the Bahamian IBCA is based, dividends could be declared and paid out of surplus. 33  Under the more recent BVI BCA 2004, the concept of a dividend being declared no longer applies. Instead, 'distributions' can be made by the company to its shareholders by resolution of the directors and there are no complex restrictions relating to the source of such distributions. The only limitation is that before the distribution the directors must be satisfied on reasonable grounds that the company will, immediately after the distribution, satisfy the statutory solvency test. 34 This must be stated in the resolution authorising the distribution. 35 A draft resolution of a sole director to make a distribution from a BVI BC can be found in Appendix 20E.

These liberal distribution provisions are not confined to the distribution of dividends, but apply to any 'distribution' to a shareholder. The definition of distribution is extremely wide, encompassing the direct and indirect transfer of cash or any asset to or for the benefit of a shareholder, and includes the purchase of an asset and the redemption or acquisition of shares, as well as the payment of a dividend from profits. 36
