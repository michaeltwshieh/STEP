**Preliminary: chosen jurisdiction**

The jurisdiction chosen is Bermuda, and Bermuda law is applied throughout both parts of this answer. Company is treated as a Bermuda company limited by shares. Its winding-up is governed by Part 13 of the Companies Act 1981, ss.201–224 of which deal with voluntary winding-up while ss.225–233 apply to every winding-up. Bermuda is one of the many offshore centres whose insolvency provisions were built on the template of the English Companies Act 1948 rather than on the later English Insolvency Act 1986; the BVI and Guernsey took the modern route, Bermuda did not. The consequence matters for what follows: the 1948 scheme, including its order of priority for the payment of debts (ss.317 and 319 of that Act), is the order applied to Company, and it is the order used in the statement of account at 5.2.

**5.1 The two voluntary routes compared, and which one Company must take**

Start with what the two processes share, because it is a good deal. Both are voluntary in the true sense: the company puts itself into liquidation rather than having liquidation forced upon it by a creditor's petition to the court, and no judge is involved in either. Both begin in the same room. The directors convene a general meeting of the shareholders and the members pass the resolution to wind up, a special resolution, which under Company's articles means a two-thirds super-majority of the votes of shareholders entitled to vote (art.1). A copy of that resolution goes to the registrar and the winding-up is advertised. In each process a liquidator takes office, and on the moment he does so the powers of the directors cease; they hand over the books, the records and the assets and they stop managing. The liquidator's work then looks identical whichever route was taken. He closes the business down rather than continuing it, gets in the assets, sells them with reasonable care, decides which creditors can prove and for how much, pays the debts in the statutory order, and accounts at the end. Both processes finish the same way too: a final statement of account, a final meeting, filing with the registrar, and dissolution once the prescribed period (usually three months) has run.

The divide between them is solvency, and the document that proves it.

A members' voluntary winding-up rests on a declaration of solvency made by the directors. They must make a full enquiry into the affairs of the company and form the opinion, having done so, that the company will be able to pay its debts in full within twelve months of the commencement of the winding-up; a statement of the company's assets and liabilities as at the latest practicable date is annexed to substantiate the opinion. This is no formality. A director who is fraudulent, or merely negligent, in checking what goes into a declaration of solvency exposes himself to criminal penalties: a fine, imprisonment, or both. Where the directors cannot honestly swear that document, the members' route is simply shut to them, and the winding-up must proceed as a creditors' voluntary winding-up.

That closed door explains everything else that differs. In a members' winding-up the members appoint the liquidator, and they usually do it in the same special resolution that puts the company into liquidation. In a creditors' winding-up the directors must go on to convene a meeting of all the creditors of the company, lay the company's financial position before them, including a list of its assets and liabilities, and it is then the creditors, by majority vote of those present in person or by proxy, who choose the liquidator. Control of the office passes out of the members' hands. The creditors, having chosen him, are also the constituency to whom he answers as the liquidation runs, through the meetings he must convene periodically (usually within three months of each anniversary of commencement) and at the end. On a members' winding-up those meetings are meetings of shareholders only; on a creditors' winding-up final meetings of both members and creditors are held, and the accounts are laid before each.

On the liquidation committee the course materials are silent. Nothing in the 1948 model as described there requires one to be formed, and creditor oversight in a Bermudian creditors' voluntary winding-up is exercised instead through those periodic and final creditors' meetings, and through the creditors' power to choose the liquidator in the first place. That should be stated plainly rather than papered over.

Publication and filing are the same on either route and are not a point of distinction: the resolution is filed with the registrar, the appointment of the liquidator is published in the local press and the Gazette, and notice of the appointment is sent to the registrar. Article 28, which allows a liquidator with the sanction of a special resolution to divide the assets among the shareholders in specie, is a members'-winding-up provision in substance and does not arise on these facts, since there is nothing left to divide.

Now to the advice. Company's assets, on the values given, come to $2,605,000. Its liabilities come to $3,375,000, before a cent of liquidation costs is added. Even leaving the arithmetic aside, the loan from Mortgagee is admittedly 'underwater': the security over Blackacre is worth $1.7 million against a debt of $2.4 million, and Mortgagee will be left chasing $700,000 with nothing to chase it with. Company is insolvent, and manifestly so. Its directors could not make the declaration of solvency without lying, and if they were to make it anyway they would be exposing themselves to a fine or a prison sentence to buy a procedure they are not entitled to. They also cannot sit still, because continuing to incur liabilities while the company is insolvent puts them at risk of civil and criminal liability for wrongful and fraudulent trading. The appropriate process, and the only one honestly available, is a **creditors' voluntary winding-up**: the directors convene the general meeting at which the members pass the special resolution to wind up, and they then convene the creditors' meeting at which the creditors appoint the liquidator. Taking that route voluntarily also has the practical merit of getting ahead of Mortgagee, who could otherwise petition for a compulsory winding-up on the ground that Company cannot pay its debts.

One further scenario should be dealt with, because it is the trap. Suppose a members' voluntary winding-up were begun on the strength of a declaration of solvency, and the liquidator, once in office and once he has the books, formed the view that the company cannot in fact pay its debts in full within the twelve months. The whole foundation of the members' route has gone. A liquidator is in law an agent who owes fiduciary duties and a common law duty of care to the company (*Knowles v Scott* [1891] 1 Ch 717), and where the company is insolvent that duty is in practice owed indirectly to its creditors, who are now the people with the economic interest in the estate. He cannot go on distributing to members. The course materials do not set out an express statutory conversion mechanism for Bermuda, and it is right to say so rather than invent one; but the answer that follows from the model is that the liquidator must put the true position before the creditors by summoning a meeting of them, at which the creditors are given the financial position and may choose the liquidator, so that the liquidation continues thereafter as a creditors' voluntary winding-up, with the creditors in control and the assets distributed under the statutory order. The directors who signed the declaration are left to answer for it.

### NOTES FORMING PART OF THIS STATEMENT OF ACCOUNT

1. **Mortgagee: fixed legal charge over Blackacre.** A creditor secured by a fixed charge over property of the Company is not paid by the Liquidator out of the general assets; it enforces its security by sale of the secured property and stands outside the liquidation to that extent. Blackacre was realised for $1,700,000 and the whole of that sum has been applied to Mortgagee in discharge, so far as it goes, of the secured debt of $2,400,000. The proceeds of Blackacre form no part of the free assets available to the Liquidator. The security being 'underwater', Mortgagee's shortfall of $700,000 has been admitted as an ordinary unsecured claim and has received the same dividend as every other ordinary unsecured creditor.

2. **Costs of the liquidation.** The Liquidator's fees and disbursements of $40,000 are paid in priority to all other debts, save the debt secured by the fixed charge.

3. **Preferential debt.** The tax of $55,000 lawfully and enforceably due to the Revenue Authority of the state of incorporation is a debt due to the government of the jurisdiction of incorporation and is therefore preferential. It ranks after the costs of the liquidation and ahead of the ordinary unsecured creditors, and the fund was sufficient to pay it in full.

4. **Floating charges.** No creditor holds a floating charge over the assets of the Company. No payment accordingly falls to be made at that level of the statutory order.

5. **The Beneficial Owner's advance of $500,000.** The sum was advanced to the Company as a shareholder loan and is recorded by a simple promissory note. It is a loan and not share capital: money lent to a company is a liability of the company, repayable according to its terms, and is not subject to the capital maintenance rules. The Beneficial Owner therefore proves in the winding-up in his character as a creditor on the note, and ranks pari passu with the other ordinary unsecured creditors. Nothing in the applicable order of priority subordinates him to the other unsecured creditors by reason of his also being the beneficial owner of the shares, and he has been paid the same 50 cents in the dollar. Had he instead subscribed the $500,000 as capital, he would have recovered nothing at all.

6. **Computation of the dividend.** Free assets $905,000, less costs of the liquidation $40,000, less the preferential debt $55,000, leaves $810,000. Admitted ordinary unsecured claims total $1,620,000 ($120,000 + $15,000 + $60,000 + $225,000 + $700,000 + $500,000). The rate of dividend is therefore 50 cents in the dollar.

7. **Members.** The assets of the Company have been exhausted in paying the secured creditor, the costs of the liquidation, the preferential debt and a dividend of 50 cents in the dollar to the ordinary unsecured creditors. The debts of the Company have not been paid in full. There is no surplus, no return of capital, and no distribution of any kind to the members, who receive nothing.
