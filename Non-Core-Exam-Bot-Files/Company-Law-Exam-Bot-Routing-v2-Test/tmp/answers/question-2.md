The chosen jurisdiction is The Bahamas. Company Ltd is treated throughout as an international business company limited by shares incorporated under the International Business Companies Act 2000.

2.1.1

When Ms AB signed the GBP 1.5 million contract, Company Ltd did not exist. At common law it therefore lacked legal capacity to contract. The Company cannot enforce the Sotheby's contract if Sotheby's refuses to deliver the work, and Sotheby's cannot enforce it against the Company if the Company later refuses to complete. Incorporation does not cure the point because a company cannot ratify an agreement made before it existed.

Kelner v Baxter (1866) places the contractual consequences on the promoter who signs for the proposed company. Ms AB's signature, 'for and on behalf of Company Ltd', closely follows that authority. The better view is that she is personally bound and entitled under the contract. Sotheby's could pursue her for the GBP 1.5 million price if the buyer defaults, while she could seek to enforce delivery if Sotheby's reneges. A common-law solution after incorporation would be a novation among Ms AB, Sotheby's and the Company. That is a new contract and needs all three parties' consent. The common-law rule is hard on Ms AB because her chosen principal was never legally capable of accepting the agency, but Kelner v Baxter leaves that risk with the person who signed.

Section 70 of the International Business Companies Act 2000 changes the practical outcome in The Bahamas. The newly incorporated Company may adopt the pre-incorporation contract without obtaining Sotheby's consent. Once adopted, the Company is bound by the purchase and may enforce it: it can require delivery from Sotheby's, while Sotheby's can claim the GBP 1.5 million price from the Company. Until adoption, no liability should be assumed against the Company. The adopting resolution should expressly release Ms AB; the precise current wording of section 70 should be checked before relying upon an automatic statutory discharge of her personal liability.

2.1.2

Once the certificate of incorporation has been issued and the first director has been lawfully appointed, CSP (Directors) Ltd should obtain the executed Sotheby's contract and consider the proposed purchase as the Company's board. The subject fits the Company's stated fine-art and antiquities purpose, and AB says that a work bought for GBP 1.5 million is worth at least GBP 3 million. The corporate director should still satisfy itself about the contract and the claimed value. It owes its own duties to the Company and cannot treat AB's wish as a binding instruction.

CSP (Directors) Ltd should authorise a human representative to act for it. That representative may then sign the Company's sole-director written resolution under the applicable article of the Company's actual articles. The resolution adopts the contract under section 70 and releases AB. It should be dated, retained with the Company's records and followed by completion and payment in accordance with the adopted contract. Sotheby's need not join in a novation.

2.2.1

CSP (Directors) Ltd must first be eligible. Corporate directors are permitted in The Bahamas, but the company providing the directorship should hold the regulatory licence required for company-management business under the Financial and Corporate Service Providers Act 2000. Section 43 of the International Business Companies Act 2000 permits a sole director. Before appointment, CSP (Directors) Ltd should give written consent to act, confirm its eligibility and provide its name, registered office and prescribed particulars. That consent may be made conditional upon an agreed Directors Service Agreement.

Section 42(1) places the election of the first director with the subscriber to the memorandum. The certificate of incorporation should therefore be obtained before CSP (Nominees) Ltd, as sole subscriber, acts. A formal meeting is unnecessary: CSP (Nominees) Ltd may pass a written resolution appointing CSP (Directors) Ltd as first and sole director. Since the subscriber is itself a company, its own board should approve the appointment decision and authorise a named human representative to sign the subscriber's resolution. The appointment should then be entered in the Company's register of directors and the prescribed particulars filed with the registrar under section 44(4). The prescribed form and filing deadline must be checked against the current Bahamian requirement.

The Directors Service Agreement records a relationship which the bare appointment resolution leaves open. For this asset-holding Company, it can settle the term, fees and reimbursable expenses, the time and attention expected, permitted delegation, confidentiality, termination, notices and Bahamian governing law. It can require Company information to be supplied, permit proper professional advice at the Company's expense and state that CSP (Directors) Ltd need not act illegally or risk its own funds. The agreement does not appoint the director; section 42(1) and the subscriber's resolution do that.

Clauses 9 and 12 of the precedent address the main risk for CSP. Section 58 of the International Business Companies Act 2000 permits Bahamian exoneration and indemnity provisions, while section 59 permits directors' and officers' insurance. Protection should be confined to the extent allowed by Bahamian law and should not purport to excuse fraud or dishonesty. Rae v Meek (1889), applied offshore in Roywest Trust Corp (Bahamas) Ltd v Savannah NV (22 July 1987), also warns against language which is too wide or was never properly drawn to the client's attention. CSP (Directors) Ltd remains responsible for its own judgement and duties to the Company despite AB's wishes and despite the DSA.
