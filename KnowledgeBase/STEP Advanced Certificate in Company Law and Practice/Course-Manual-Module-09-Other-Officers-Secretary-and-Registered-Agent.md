## Module 9:

## Other Officers of a Company: Secretary and Registered Agent

## Learning objectives

The purpose of this module is to:

- place company officers in context
- explain the procedures for the appointment, removal and retirement of company secretaries
- review the usual duties and functions of a company secretary
- highlight the potential liability of the company secretary
- review the role, powers and duties of a registered agent.

## Introduction

A company must have a board of directors and may have other officers (such as a president, vice-presidents, treasurer and secretary). Generally, the board of directors choose officers and assign them specific responsibilities. In the same way, it is usual for the articles to contain a provision whereby officers may be removed by the directors (except where, under specific articles, the officer was appointed by the shareholders). Officers of a company are fiduciaries, just like directors. They therefore owe duties of care and loyalty to the company.

Because our standard offshore private company is used to hold assets rather than to operate a business in the traditional sense, there is little point in forming a company with a complex management structure comprising, say, a president and a battery of vice-presidents to operate as middle-management executives below board level. There are, however, two company officers who may be appointed to private offshore companies: a company secretary and, in those IBC jurisdictions where the appointment is mandatory, a registered agent.

In the first part of this module, we review the statutory requirements for, and the usual functions of, a company secretary in an offshore private limited company, and consider their potential risks and liabilities. We also cover the usual procedure involved in their appointment and retirement. In section 2, we introduce another corporate officer, one peculiar to IBCs and certain other offshore companies and not found in the onshore corporate model: namely the registered agent.

## 1. The company secretary

## 1.1 Companies legislation requirements

It was a statutory requirement of the 1948 Act that a private company must have a secretary. 131

<!-- image -->

The companies legislation of some offshore financial centres follows the 1948 Act model and requires a company to have a secretary. But a number of offshore centres, as in England under current companies legislation, make the appointment of a secretary optional. In these jurisdictions, the articles may, but are not required to, contain provisions for the appointment of a secretary. 132

Statutory requirements that specify who may be appointed as a company secretary vary between offshore centres. There is usually no restriction on any individual who is sui juris being appointed, although in a couple of jurisdictions it is a requirement that the company secretary must hold a recognised qualification, for example be an advocate (attorney-at-law), chartered or certified accountant, or chartered secretary. 133 Whoever is appointed must be considered by the directors to be fit and proper to act in the role of secretary and must possess the knowledge and experience to enable them to fulfil the secretary's duties and responsibilities.

A number of jurisdictions permit corporate secretaries. 134 In the centres where this is possible, service providers usually create a separate company to perform the secretarial functions for companies that they manage. The service providers must then ensure that the persons who are appointed to represent the corporate secretary are suitably experienced and of an acceptable level of seniority.

Under the provisions of companies legislation of Bermuda, the company secretary must be ordinarily resident within the jurisdiction of incorporation. 135

It is unlawful for a sole director to act also as company secretary. This is not only a prohibition set out in companies legislation but is also found in typical articles. 136

In jurisdictions where a company secretary is not mandatory, the articles of a company may, of course, confer a power upon the board of directors to appoint one and impose specific duties upon the secretary so appointed.

## 1.2 Appointment, retirement and removal

## 1.2.1  Appointment

In jurisdictions where it is mandatory for a company to have a secretary, the first company secretary must consent in writing to act and is appointed at the time of incorporation of the company. The appointment is deemed to be made on incorporation and is confirmed by a note made in the directors' resolution at their inaugural board meeting. The registrar of companies must be notified of such appointment and prescribed particulars entered in the register of directors and officers - see the second entry in Appendix 7B of the online Study Resources.

In jurisdictions where the appointment of a secretary is optional, there may be a mandatory requirement in the articles, such as art.21.1 of Appendix 1B. In these circumstances, the secretary is appointed by the directors at their inaugural board meeting - see Resolution 2 of Appendix 9 of the online Study Resources. There is no need to notify the registrar of the appointment, although particulars of the secretary should be entered in the register of directors and officers.

When companies legislation makes it optional and there is no requirement to have a secretary in the articles, the appointment of a secretary is a matter to be decided by the board - see art.5(1) of Appendix 1C of the online Study Resources. A directors' resolution appointing a secretary is illustrated in Resolution 2 of Appendix 9 and Appendix 24A of the online Study Resources. In the author's experience, where optional, it is most unusual for the board of a private asset-holding company to appoint a secretary. Factors to consider include whether it is felt necessary for a specific person to be in charge of corporate administration and for there to be another layer of corporate governance.

## 1.2.2  Retirement, removal

The company secretary may, of course resign the position. Subject to the terms of the articles, this is usually achieved by notifying the company, in writing, of the company secretary's wish to resign. A letter of resignation usually has to be delivered to the company's registered office and, depending on the articles, the resignation becomes effective either on receipt of the letter by the company or following the acceptance of the resignation by the directors.

It is also usual for the articles to confer a power upon the directors to be able to remove a company secretary from office, either with or without cause - see art.21.1 of Appendix 1B. A resolution removing a secretary and recording the resignation of the secretary and appointing a new one are illustrated in Appendix 24B of the online Study Resources.

Any changes to the company secretary have to be recorded in the company's register of officers and, in jurisdictions where a secretary is mandatory, the registrar of companies should be notified within a certain period of time.

## 1.3 The role of the company secretary

For many years it was generally accepted that the function of a company secretary was passive and unimportant. The secretary was expected simply to act in accordance with instructions received from the directors and was not expected to assume any responsibility for the activities of the company. This perception of the role was evidenced by the case of Barnett, Hoares &amp; Co. v South London Tramways Co. , 137  where it was said that:

'A secretary is a mere servant. His position is that he is to do what he is told. No person should assume that he has any authority to represent [the company] in anything at all.'

Accordingly, a secretary's function was formerly solely administrative - to do the bidding of the directors and often to ensure that the company's paperwork was in order. The secretary did not have any managerial powers to exercise in running the business.

Over the years, however, the role of the secretary expanded. In the case of Panorama Developments (Guildford) Ltd v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics Ltd 138 it was stated:

'[T]imes have changed. A company secretary is a much more important person nowadays than he was in 1887 [the date of the Barnett, Hoares case]. He is [now] an officer of the company with extensive duties and responsibilities. This appears not only in the modern Companies Acts, but also by the role he plays in the day-to-day business of companies. He is no longer a mere clerk.'

Since the observations of the court in the Panorama Developments case in 1977, the requirement for a private company to have a secretary has been debated as an unnecessary layer of corporate governance. The position of secretary has nowadays largely been abandoned. Where a secretary is optional, one is rarely appointed. Indeed, the English model articles published in the 2008 Companies (Model Articles) Regulations, and which are reproduced in Appendix 1C, do not appoint nor mention a secretary.

When a secretary is appointed as an officer of a company, the role is usually that of the company's chief administrative officer, ensuring that the company is in good standing and in compliance with the requirements of applicable companies legislation and regulation, and of its memorandum and articles. As such, there are certain core duties that a secretary should fulfil, such as maintenance of the company's statutory registers, making required filings with the registrar, servicing shareholders' and board meetings and keeping proper minutes and records of resolutions passed. Depending upon the company and requirements of the board, other wider and unusual duties may be imposed upon the secretary, such as AML compliance, health and safety compliance, data protection compliance, insurance, etc.

## 1.4 Usual duties and powers of a company secretary

## 1.4.1  Duties

As an officer of the company, the secretary owes similar duties to the company as a director: specifically, the fiduciary duty of good faith, integrity and loyalty, and a common law duty to exercise their powers with appropriate care and skill.

<!-- image -->

## 1.4.2  Powers

Companies legislation does not confer any specific powers upon the company secretary. Powers may be conferred by the articles of the company, but it is more likely that the responsibilities of a secretary are conferred by the delegation of powers by the directors - see Resolution 2 of Appendix 24A and the role description in Appendix 24C of the online Study Resources.

It is generally accepted that the company secretary is the key administrative officer of a company with responsibilities that extend beyond ensuring that the corporate files are kept up to date and that the company remains in good standing. ## i. Maintaining corporate records

The company secretary is usually responsible for ensuring that the records of the company are kept up to date and in good order. This usually entails making sure that the corporate registers (directors, members, officers and charges) are current and that the file contains copies of all documents received from or sent to the registrar of companies.

## ii. Issuing share certificates

The company secretary is usually empowered to issue share certificates to shareholders. This usually entails executing the certificates (with or without the common seal of the company), together with one of the directors.

## iii. Executing deeds and documents

Traditional articles 139  usually authorise the company secretary to execute deeds and documents on behalf of the company, together with one of the directors.

## iv. Fulfilling statutory requirements

If companies legislation 140  requires a company secretary to reside in the jurisdiction of incorporation, the secretary is often considered accountable locally for the activities of the company should problems arise. In these circumstances, the secretary is usually given a statutory responsibility of ensuring that the company complies with all local companies legislation requirements.

<!-- image -->

## v. Making representations on behalf of the company

If required by the directors, the company secretary often makes representations on behalf of the company. This usually involves providing confirmation of the status or good standing of the company or sending details of the directors or authorised officers to, for example, banks or investment managers.

## vi. Corresponding with shareholders

The company secretary is usually the person who contacts the shareholders as and when contact is required. This often involves sending notice of shareholders' meetings to members, as well as copies of the minutes of such meetings.

## vii. Submitting returns to registrar of companies

From time to time, information may be required by the registrar of companies. Examples include an annual return, notification of a change of directors, the allotment of shares and details of any special resolutions passed in general meetings of the company.

The company secretary is usually responsible for ensuring that any filing is carried out within the required time frame.

## viii. Taking minutes of meetings

The company secretary usually attends all general meetings as well as meetings of the directors, and takes the minutes. The minutes of meetings are then passed to the chairman, or chairman of the next meeting, for signature. The secretary is also usually responsible for circulating copies of documents that may be required for consideration at meetings, and for circulating copies of the minutes once they have been signed.

## ix. Acting as chief administrative officer

Finally, the company secretary is usually responsible for dealing with all other administrative functions that may be required from time to time. This tends to involve acting as the company's main point of contact with the directors, who may from time to time require information held by the company secretary (usually at the registered office). The secretary may also be responsible for organising the payment of dividends.

## 1.5 Potential liability

The company secretary is an officer of the company and as such owes the company similar duties to those owed by a director, namely:

- the fiduciary duty of honesty, integrity and loyalty
- the common law duty to exercise their powers with appropriate care and skill.

Any breach of duty places the secretary in the same position as a director of the company in relation to liability for their actions.

Under typical company and insolvency law it is possible for a company secretary to be held personally liable, with the directors, for allowing a company to trade while the directors/secretary know that the company is insolvent (generically referred to as fraudulent trading ): see Module 8 section 6. In Re Maidstone Buildings Provisions Ltd 141  a company secretary was potentially liable under the Companies Acts for failing to advise the directors that the company was insolvent and that business should cease. A company secretary may often be in a position to be aware of what the directors are doing with the company's assets. Much depends on the factual evidence - to what extent did the company secretary have knowledge of what was going on in the company? And how involved was the secretary in decisions taken by the company's directors?

It is possible to reduce the liability of the company secretary by including suitable provisions in the articles of association or in the company management agreement, in the same way that exoneration provisions may be included for the protection of a director. Look at our analysis of the legality of these provisions in Module 8 section 5. You may recall that any attempt to exempt an officer from liability for deliberate acts of default, dishonesty or bad faith is void. Furthermore, certain jurisdictions follow the English practice and make it unlawful for the articles, or other contractual provision, to limit or exclude the liability of a director, or other officer of the company (including secretaries) for acts of negligence or other default.

## 2. The registered agent (or authorised representative)

## 2.1 Statutory requirement

There is a statutory requirement found in the (I)BC legislation in jurisdictions such as the BVI, 142 the Bahamas 143  and the Isle of Man (for the New Manx Vehicle) 144 and in other jurisdictions that follow the IBC model, that every company incorporated under the relevant enactment must have an officer called a registered agent.

## 2.2 Appointment, removal and retirement of the registered agent

The IBC legislation stipulates that only a licensed registered agent can incorporate an IBC and deal with the registrar. 147

The initial registered agent is selected by the promoter of the company during the incorporation process and their name and address must appear in the company's memorandum. The memorandum must be signed by the registered agent as incorporator (not, technically, as subscriber) and must be filed with the registrar of companies. Upon incorporation, the name of the registered agent is recorded in the company's register of registered agents and with the registrar; it is therefore freely available to the public.

After the initial appointment, the registered agent can be removed and replaced by either:

- a. a resolution of the shareholders of the company; or
- b. if the articles permit, by a resolution of the board of directors. 148

When a registered agent is changed in this manner, notice of the change, in the prescribed form, must be filed by the outgoing registered agent and endorsed by the new registered agent, indicating consent to act. 149

Retirement is also permitted in circumstances when there is a replacement registered agent in the manner described above. In the event that a registered agent wishes to retire or vacate office, for whatever reason, and there is no immediate replacement, the agent must give to the directors not less than 90 days' written notice of the intention to vacate office, and send a copy of such notice to the registrar. If, at the time the notice expires, the company has not appointed a new registered agent, the outgoing registered agent must give notice to the registrar who shall, unless a new agent is appointed within 30 days of publication of notice of the default in the Gazette , strike the company from the register. Where a company has been struck off in these circumstances, the liability of the company and its directors, officers and members for all debts, claims and liabilities, if any, shall continue and be unaffected by the striking off.

## 2.3 Role and responsibilities of the registered agent

The licensing process means that only responsible persons who reside within the jurisdiction can fulfil the function of a registered agent. So what is that function? Surprisingly, there is little mention of the precise functions of a registered agent in the relevant companies legislation, save for the following.

## 2.3.1  Keeping of corporates documents

A BVI BC must keep the following documents at the office of its registered agent: the memorandum and articles of the company; the register of members and the register of directors; copies of all notices and other documents filed by the company [with the Registry] in the previous 10 years. 150  IBCs generally keep corporate records at the address of the registered office, which will probably, but does not have to, also be the office of the registered agent.

A BVI BC may keep minutes of meetings and resolutions of members and of directors at the office of the registered agent, or at such other place, within or outside the BVI, as the directors may determine.

Furthermore, the BVI BC may keep at the office of its registered agent or at such other place or places, within or outside the BVI, as the directors may determine, the records and underlying documentation of the company, including accounts and records, such as invoices, contracts, and similar documents, in relation to: all sums of money received and expended by the company; all sales and purchases of goods by the company; the assets and liabilities of the company.

## 2.3.2  Accepting service of process

Service of a document may be effected on the company by addressing the document to the company and leaving it at or sending it by a prescribed method to either the company's registered office or the office of the company's registered agent. 151

## 2.3.3   Notifying the registrar of changes of directors

Where the company keeps a copy of the register of directors at the office of its registered agent, it shall notify the registrar of any change within a specified number of days.

## 2.3.4  Keeping the common seal

A BVI BC is required to have a common seal, which should be kept at the office of the registered agent. 152 IBCs generally may have a seal, and if they do, it is kept at the registered office (usually the same address as the location of the registered agent).

## 2.3.5  Complying with reasonable requests of the board

Subject to the memorandum and articles of the company, the registered agent shall act on the instructions of the directors of the company when such instructions are contained in a resolution passed by the board.

## 2.3.6  Attesting documents

The ultra vires doctrine, so far as it affects the powers of the board of directors to enter into contracts, deals and transactions on behalf of the company, is simplified for IBCs. Provided a contract or other legal document is signed by a director whose signature is attested (witnessed) by the registered agent, the contract is deemed to be within the powers of the directors and valid. 153  But this does not apply to BVI BCs, which have simpler provisions than IBCs - see s.31 of the BVI BCA.

## 2.4 Summary

In conclusion, the role of a registered agent is not dissimilar to that of a company secretary insofar as they are appointed by the board and are required to comply with lawful instructions issued by board resolution. Nonetheless, a registered agents' main function is to ensure that the company complies with its rules of corporate governance. There is also an element of regulatory control insofar as the company has an officer who resides within the jurisdiction, and is accountable to the regulator, should information be sought during an investigation. The function of a registered agent can therefore be summarised quite succinctly: it is basically to ensure compliance with all the statutory requirements of the companies legislation and associated regulations. In default, the registered agent can be found criminally responsible. For example, s.243 of the BVI BCA 2004 provides: 154

'Where an offence is committed under the BVI BCA and a director or other officer of the company knowingly authorised, permitted or acquiesced in the commission of the offence, the director or officer is also guilty of that offence and shall be liable to the same criminal penalty.' .
