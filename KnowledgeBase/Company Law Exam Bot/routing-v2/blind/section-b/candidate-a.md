The notice should be served on C, D and E as the members of Company. It should also be served on A and B because Article 27.5(c) requires notice to every director who is not also a shareholder. Article 27.5 limits the people entitled to general-meeting notices to those categories. If an auditor has been appointed and the applicable Companies Legislation requires notice to that auditor, a copy should also be sent; no auditor is identified in the question.

Article 27.1 permits personal service, post to a member's registered address or email to the registered email address supplied to Company. Registered post or a recognised courier gives a reliable dispatch record. Under Article 27.2, properly addressed and prepaid post is deemed served on the fourth day after posting, while email is served on sending. The notice must give at least 14 clear days under Article 11.1, excluding the service day and 28 August. Personal service or email should therefore reach the recipient by 13 August [year]. For post deemed served on 13 August, it should be posted by 9 August [year]. Earlier service is preferable. Article 11.2 permits shorter notice if every member entitled to receive notice consents.

1.3

Proper notice is part of convening the meeting. A failure to give it to a person entitled to notice generally invalidates the meeting's proceedings. Article 11.3 saves an accidental omission to give notice, and accidental non-receipt, from invalidating the proceedings. That protection does not cover a deliberate omission or a notice that is otherwise defective. A member who attends personally or by proxy will generally be treated as having received valid notice. An absent member who was deliberately omitted could therefore challenge the resolution and the Company could not safely rely on the meeting for the liquidation filing.

1.4

C cannot pass the resolution at the appointed time. Article 12.2 requires two shareholders personally present for a quorum because Company has three shareholders; C's 70 per cent holding does not replace the two-person requirement. No business, including the election of a chairman or the vote, can be transacted before a quorum exists.

If the meeting was called by the directors, Article 12.3 requires it to stand adjourned when no quorum is present within half an hour, to the same day in the next week at the same time and place. If it was convened on a members' requisition, it is dissolved instead. At the adjourned meeting, the members present constitute the quorum if no quorum appears within half an hour. C could then vote alone. A poll would record C's 70 per cent holding under Article 13.1, which exceeds the two-thirds special-resolution threshold in Article 1, on the question's direction that the lesser article threshold may apply. C still cannot pass the resolution on 28 August itself.

1.5

On a show of hands Article 13.1 gives each member present one vote. C has one vote in favour and D and E have two votes against, so the motion is lost on that count. The chairman's declaration would record that result unless a poll is demanded.

C should demand a poll before, or at the time of, the declaration of the show-of-hands result. Article 12.6 allows one member holding at least 15 per cent of the paid-up capital to demand it. On the poll Article 13.1 gives C 70 votes and D and E together 30 votes. C therefore obtains more than the two-thirds threshold in Article 1 and the resolution passes on the poll. The question's wording treats that article threshold as the lesser super-majority permitted by the applicable legislation.

Question 3

The chosen jurisdiction is the British Virgin Islands, and Company is treated as a BVI business company. That choice is used throughout this question. International Trust Company is treated as the registered holder of the shares in its capacity as trustee of the Redhouse Trust, while Company owns its own assets.

3.1

The division between members and directors grew out of the weaknesses of the medieval sole trader and partnership. A sole trader owned the business, made its decisions and bore unlimited losses. A partnership pooled resources, yet a partner's interest was difficult to transfer and the business could call on little capital beyond the partners' contributions.

The Bubble Act 1720 stopped the lawful use of many joint-stock structures after the South Sea Company collapse. Industrialisation and the railways then required a form that could raise public money, hold property and continue despite changes among investors. The Joint Stock Companies Act 1844 introduced registration and separate corporate personality. The Limited Liability Act 1855 reduced the investor's exposure to the amount committed to the shares, and the Companies Act 1907 recognised the private company alongside the public company.

Those developments produced the traditional corporate model. Shareholders supply capital and own shares; directors, chosen by the shareholders, bring management expertise and run the business. The board takes the day-to-day and strategic decisions, while members use general meetings to alter the constitution, appoint or remove directors and decide matters reserved to them, such as a voluntary winding-up. Separation lets a company raise capital from people who do not want to manage it, allows continuity when members change and gives shareholders limited liability. The directors' fiduciary and care duties provide the check on the authority entrusted to them.

3.2

Article 19.1 adopts the 1948 Table A form. It gives the directors management of the Company's business and all Company powers that the Companies Legislation or the articles do not reserve to a general meeting. Section 109 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 expresses the same starting point by placing the business and affairs of a company under the management, direction or supervision of its directors.

The article leaves the members with the powers expressly reserved to them, including constitutional alterations and any matter that the legislation requires to be decided in general meeting. It gives the board a residual management power. A member resolution cannot take over that power merely by instructing the board to accept a transaction. Quin & Axtens Ltd v Salmon (1909) treated an equivalent article in that way: the members could control the result by altering the articles through the proper constitutional procedure, rather than by usurping a power already given to the directors. Article 19.1 therefore preserves board autonomy, subject to the reserved matters and any lawful alteration of the articles.

3.3

International Trust Company holds the shares as trustee of the Redhouse Trust. That position gives it the legal rights of the registered member, subject to the trust arrangement, but it does not make the trustee owner of Company's worldwide assets or of the Midcity Bank account. Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd (1897) treats the company as a person separate from its members, and Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd (1925) shows why a shareholder cannot treat corporate property as personal property. Company therefore owns and manages its assets in its own name.

The trustee may exercise member powers, including the power to pass a resolution on a reserved matter, appoint or remove directors and alter the articles where the required threshold is met. Article 19.1 gives the directors independent management of the business. A direction from the trustee or the trust's underlying client is therefore an instruction to be considered by the board, rather than an automatic command. A director provided by an offshore service provider remains a director with fiduciary and care duties. Describing that director as a nominee does not permit slavish compliance with a trustee's wishes.

The practical risk is greatest where the trustee or beneficial owner takes over day-to-day decisions, operates the bank account or gives a broad power of attorney. Management and control may then be located where that person acts, with possible tax consequences for Company, while the service provider and directors retain exposure for failures to supervise or protect corporate assets. The directors should remain sole or joint bank signatories, keep records of their own decisions and use a written shareholder resolution for a reserved matter. If a delegation is needed, a special, time-limited power of attorney tied to an identified transaction is safer than a general power. The service provider must refuse an instruction it believes unlawful or harmful to Company and should conduct the compliance checks relevant to the trust and the account.

3.4

Article 15.3 permits Company to remove a director before the end of the director's term by ordinary resolution, notwithstanding any agreement between Company and the director. The article does not require a reason or proof of cause. On the stated facts International Trust Company holds the shares, so if it is the sole registered member it may use the written-resolution procedure in Article 12.11. A meeting route would require the notice, quorum and voting procedure in Articles 11 to 13. If another registered member exists, the ordinary resolution requires the simple majority specified in Article 1.

Article 15.4 permits the members, by ordinary resolution, to appoint a replacement for the removed director. The incoming director's written consent should be obtained before the appointment. The company secretary or administrator should then update the register of directors and make the required notice to the Registrar. A directors' service agreement may record the new director's terms, but it does not displace the removal power in Article 15.3. Article 17.1 also identifies events that vacate office, including bankruptcy, unsound mind, written resignation and an interest in a Company contract, subject to the disclosure exception in Article 17.2. Those automatic vacation events and any statutory disqualification are distinct from a member's ordinary-resolution removal.

Question 4

A directorship brings authority to bind a company, access to its information and the possibility of remuneration. The same authority creates personal exposure. In the BVI example, sections 120 and 122 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 require honesty, good faith, the company's best interests and the care, skill and diligence of a reasonable director in the circumstances. The office therefore carries a duty attached to each decision, not a shield supplied by the fee.

Authority is one source of risk. Article 19.3 of the supplied articles lets directors borrow and mortgage company property, yet requires prior ordinary-resolution sanction once undischarged borrowing exceeds $1 million. A director who signs a $5 million facility without that sanction may breach the articles even if the lender can enforce the loan. Royal British Bank v Turquand (1856) protected a good-faith bank that had relied on a mortgage despite an internal approval defect, and section 31(1) of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 gives a similar statutory protection. That rule separates the company's external liability from the director's internal liability. The director should read the memorandum, articles and resolutions before signing, obtain the required member approval and record the authority in the minutes.

The fiduciary duty creates a second group of risks. The director must honestly pursue the company's interests and use powers for a proper purpose. Hutton v West Cork Railway Co (1883) links corporate spending to the company's business purpose. Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver (1942) required directors to account for a profit made from an opportunity that should have belonged to the company. Lee Panavision Ltd v Lee Lighting Ltd (1992) treated a management deal favouring the director's other company as a collateral use of power, and Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi (2005) treated the diversion of a customer as a breach. Full disclosure, a written record of the proposal and an independent decision by the disinterested board or members reduce this exposure.

Loyalty also covers self-dealing, secret profits and third-party benefits. Aberdeen Railway v Blaikie Bros (1854) illustrates the danger of a director's interest in a contract with the company. A director should disclose the interest before the decision, abstain where the governing rule requires it and obtain the approval of a disinterested quorum or the members. Article 18.2 of the supplied articles permits an interested contract after disclosure, while Appendices 23A and 23B show the board and member approval routes. A director who receives a gift or benefit because of the office should disclose it and obtain informed member approval where the materials permit that course. Approval cannot turn dishonest expropriation into proper conduct: Cook v Deeks (1916) and Franbar Holdings Ltd v Patel (2008) stand behind the rule against ratifying a fraud on the company or its minority.

Care, skill and supervision form a separate exposure. Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co (1925) applied an old, subjective standard to inexperienced non-executive directors. Dorchester Finance Co Ltd v Stebbing (1989) and Norman v Theodore Goddard (1991) point to the modern objective standard. Re Barings plc (No.5) (2000) held that delegation of trading functions did not remove the need for a monitoring system. Lexi Holdings plc (In Administration) v Luqman (2009) shows why directors cannot rely on family information where the accounts and past conduct call for inquiry, while Madoff Securities International Ltd (In Liquidation) v Raven (2013) recognises reliance where the circumstances give no reason for suspicion. Bishopsgate Investment Management (In Liquidation) v Maxwell (1994) is the warning against signing documents brought by a person who is not a company officer without asking what they do.

The practical response is a working board process. Directors should read the financial statements and contracts, ask questions when the facts indicate a problem, retain competent legal or accounting advice and keep minutes showing the reasons for the decision. Delegation is permitted, yet supervision remains. An honest, informed commercial decision does not become negligent merely because the venture loses money; a blank cheque or an unexplained transfer of corporate assets is a different matter.

Service-provider directorships carry a distinctive risk. There is no legal 'nominee director'. A director appointed by a corporate service provider owes the same duties as an executive director, and the statement that the director was merely following a client's instruction is no defence to a breach. A general power of attorney to a beneficial owner can move management and control to the owner's home state and leave the board exposed for losses. A special power limited to one transaction and one period, such as the form in Appendix 29A, carries less risk than Appendix 29B's general delegation. Bank mandates should keep directors as sole or joint signatories, and the provider should refuse an unlawful instruction and retain the compliance record.

Insolvency creates personal exposure to creditors. A liquidator investigates earlier management, and section 332 of the Companies Act 1948 permits the court, on the liquidator's application, to require persons knowingly involved in carrying on a business with intent to defraud creditors to contribute to the company's assets. Re William C Leitch Ltd (1932) illustrates fraudulent trading. In jurisdictions adopting the later objective rule, section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 addresses wrongful trading where directors knew or ought to have known that insolvent liquidation could not be avoided. For a BVI company, the course identifies sections 55 and 256 of the BVI Insolvency Act 2003 through section 211 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004. Directors should monitor cash flow and liabilities, stop incurring debts when the statutory test is met and take advice on a creditor arrangement, administration or liquidation.

The risk also includes the remedies available after a breach. The company may set aside a conflicted transaction, recover an undisclosed profit or claim compensation. Under Foss v Harbottle (1843), the company is ordinarily the proper plaintiff; a minority claim may proceed where fraud prevents the company from acting. Pavlides v Jensen (1956) shows that negligence alone did not give the minority shareholder standing on its facts, while Anglo Eastern v Knutz (1988), Daniels v Daniels (1978) and Prudential Assurance Co Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd (1991) illustrate the fraud exception. A director should therefore treat disclosure, minutes and independent approval as evidence that may prevent a claim, rather than as administrative decoration.

Shareholder approval can release a director from liability to the company for a disclosed proposed act or a later breach, normally by ordinary resolution. The disclosure must be full and the members must know what they are approving. The protection reaches an interested contract, a benefit, a corporate-asset decision or negligent management. It does not release a director from a dishonest appropriation that harms the company, creditors or minority members. Article 29.1 of the supplied articles, where the governing law permits it, excludes liability for error or oversight in the absence of bad faith, fraud or dishonesty. Article 29.2 gives an indemnity subject to wilful default, bad faith and fraud, and Article 29.3 preserves responsibility for a director's own wilful default.

The director can also negotiate a DSA and a company-management agreement. Paragraphs 9.1 and 9.2 of Appendix 21C contain the usual exoneration and indemnity wording, while clauses 7.1 and 7.2 of Appendix 22 protect the administrator and provide a client indemnity. Those clauses must be drawn narrowly and brought to the client's attention. Rae v Meek (1889), West v Lazard Brothers (1993) and Roywest Trust Corp (Bahamas) Ltd v Savannah NV (1987) support the warning against a clause drafted so widely that it purports to excuse every kind of default. Section 132 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 permits the relevant BVI contractual and article protection within its limits; section 232 of the Companies Act 2006 takes a more restrictive English position. An administrator's indemnity also does not automatically protect a director who was not made a party to the agreement.

Insurance supplies another layer. Paragraph 12.1 of Appendix 21C and section 133 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004 support directors' and officers' cover. A policy may meet negligent or inadvertent exposure where the director acted in good faith, yet deliberate default, fraud and dishonesty remain outside the protection. The director should check the insured persons, exclusions, limits, notification conditions and costs of defence before accepting office. The safeguards reduce financial exposure; they leave the duties and the possibility of liability intact.

The double edge is therefore real. Authority, fees and professional support reward a director, while the same office exposes the director to claims for misuse of power, conflicts, poor supervision, insolvent trading and failures in the records. A director who follows the constitution, makes an informed independent decision, records it and uses tailored contractual and insurance protection has the strongest available position. That conclusion follows from the cases and provisions above, while the limits on ratification and protection mean that fraud or wilful default remains a personal risk.

Question 5

The chosen jurisdiction is the British Virgin Islands, and Company is treated as a BVI business company. That choice is used throughout this question. The BVI Insolvency Act 2003 is the insolvency framework applied to an insolvent BVI company through section 211 of the BVI Business Companies Act 2004.

5.1

A members' voluntary winding-up assumes solvency. The directors first make a declaration, after a full enquiry, that the company can pay its debts in full within the stated period, with an assets-and-liabilities statement attached. The members then pass the required winding-up resolution and appoint a liquidator. The liquidator realises the assets, pays every debt and expense, presents the final account and returns any surplus to members. For a BVI company, a member or the directors may use a winding-up resolution where the memorandum or articles confer that power, but no such constitutional provision is supplied here.

A creditors' voluntary winding-up is the route for an insolvent company. The directors cannot honestly make the solvency declaration. The shareholders resolve to wind up, the directors arrange a creditors' meeting and the creditors choose the liquidator by the prescribed vote. Creditors therefore have the greater say in the conduct of the liquidation. The liquidator realises the assets, investigates the company's affairs, pays the liquidation costs and preferential claims, distributes the balance pari passu among ordinary unsecured creditors and presents the final account to the relevant meetings and Registrar.

Company's stated assets total $2,605,000, whereas its listed pre-liquidation liabilities total $3,375,000. The $2,400,000 Mortgagee claim is secured by a fixed legal charge over Blackacre. The Mortgagee can take the $1,700,000 realisation of Blackacre and prove the $700,000 shortfall as an unsecured claim. The tax claim is preferential under the course's payment order. The Lawyer, Accountancy Firm, Corporate Service Provider, trade creditors and Beneficial Owner's promissory-note loan are treated as ordinary unsecured claims because no other security or subordination is stated. The proper process is therefore a creditors' voluntary winding-up. A members' voluntary process would require a false declaration of solvency and would expose the directors to the consequences of an inaccurate declaration.
